On a humid night in April 2026, Avinash Kisanrao Patil, founder and sole proprietor of Sai Tex Ltd., stood on the factory floor of his unit in the Pride Co-operative Textile Park in Ichhalkaranji, Maharashtra. Five of his ten Rifa Electronic Rapier looms clattered through their cycles at 300 revolutions per minute. The other five stood dark and still, their reed frames gathering lint. A year ago, all ten had run through the night. Now, half the floor was silent.¹

His phone buzzed twice. The first notification was a ₹120,000 interest debit from his bank — the monthly cost of servicing the ₹1.2 crore (US$130,400)² debt he still owed on the very machines that stood idle before him. The second was a WhatsApp message from Shivmani Mishra, his manager and oldest employee: "Avinash-ji, two more boys from Bihar are leaving next week. The new unit on the other side of the Park has air-jet machines. They are offering 10 per cent more."³

Patil closed the message and opened his personal ledger — a ruled notebook that served as Sai Tex's only financial record. He did not maintain a formal balance sheet; he had never been trained to.⁴ But the numbers he had memorized were unforgiving. In the best months of 2025, the unit had generated ₹378,000 in revenue. In the worst, it had managed just ₹180,000. Against this, monthly expenses consumed ₹510,000 (see Exhibit 1). Every month, for the privilege of keeping his doors open, Patil was bleeding cash from savings that were almost gone.

By Monday morning, he had to call his bank manager back. The bank had offered to finance a ₹3.6 crore modernization — twelve new air-jet looms to replace the ageing Rifas — but only if Patil pledged his family's bungalow as additional collateral.⁵ His wife had been direct the previous evening: "We want this to work, Avinash. But not at the cost of everything else."⁶

Sai Tex employed 12 permanent workers and six to ten temporary workers during peak seasons.⁷ Two competitors in the same Park — M.F. Rathi Textiles and P.A. Patil Textiles — had already upgraded and were capturing his old clients.⁸ The job-work rate that determined his revenue had fallen below break-even and showed no sign of recovering. The question was no longer whether to change, but which change would not destroy everything he had built.

## THE MANCHESTER OF MAHARASHTRA

Ichhalkaranji, a town of roughly 300,000 people in the Kolhapur district of western Maharashtra, had been weaving cloth since before India's independence. The powerloom industry dates back to 1904, when the first decentralized powerloom units were established.⁹ Over the following century, the cluster grew into one of India's largest centres for greige (unfinished) fabric production, earning the title "Manchester of Maharashtra." By 2026, the cluster housed over 50,000 ordinary powerlooms, 12,000 air-jet and rapier looms, and 3,000 auto looms — roughly 65,000 machines spread across thousands of small and medium-sized units. The ecosystem operated on a distinctive decentralized model: thousands of family-owned units, most employing fewer than 50 workers, collectively produced a significant share of India's total cloth output. Yarn suppliers, weavers, sizing units, processors, and traders all operated within a few kilometres of one another, keeping transaction costs low. At the heart of this ecosystem was the job-work model. A job-work unit did not own the raw material or the finished product. A merchant supplied the yarn, the weaver converted it into fabric, and the weaver was paid a per-pick rate — typically measured in paisa per pick of the weft thread.¹⁰ The model kept capital requirements low, since the weaver bore no inventory risk, but it also meant the weaver had no pricing power. The rate was set by the merchant, and the weaver's only leverage was the quality and speed of his machines.

The workforce that powered this ecosystem had undergone a fundamental transformation. Where Ichhalkaranji's looms had once been operated by local Maharashtrian workers, by 2026 the industry was almost entirely dependent on migrant labour from Uttar Pradesh and Bihar.¹¹ These workers felt no generational attachment to specific mills. They were rational economic actors who compared wages, working conditions, and machinery across units — and they moved. A mill running 15-year-old machines was not merely less productive; it was less attractive to the very workers it needed to operate.

While competing clusters in Surat and Bhiwandi had aggressively adopted air-jet looms — machines that produced four times the volume at significantly higher speeds — Ichhalkaranji's smaller units remained largely tethered to older rapier and shuttle technology. The town's weavers were caught in a commodity trap: their costs were modern and rising, but their production technology was legacy and failing. When the "Manchester of Maharashtra" caught a cold, thousands of families — both owners and workers — felt the chill.

## SAI TEX: A DECADE OF WEAVING

Avinash Kisanrao Patil founded Sai Tex Ltd. in 2010, investing ₹1.2 crore in ten Rifa Electronic Rapier looms imported from China at ₹12 lakh each. Unlike the multi-generational weaving families that dominated Ichhalkaranji, Patil was a first-generation entrepreneur. Before striking out on his own, he had spent several years working in larger textile operations in the region, where he gained hands-on exposure to the sale-purchase model, yarn procurement, buyer negotiations, and the economics of fabric production at scale. When he launched Sai Tex, however, he chose the job-work model deliberately — it was the lower-risk path for a startup with no established buyer relationships and limited working capital.¹² His bet was on technology. The Rifa machines, with their electronic rapier weft-insertion systems operating at 300 revolutions per minute, were a significant step up from the traditional shuttle looms that most of the cluster still used. In 2010, they represented the "modern face" of Ichhalkaranji.

For a decade, the bet paid off. Sai Tex became a respected job-work unit, weaving grey fabric — primarily 100 per cent cotton and polyester-cotton blends used for shirting — from yarn supplied by India's large fabric exporters. The unit operated from a shed within the Pride Co-operative Textile Park, a local textile cooperative that provided shared infrastructure and a community of fellow mill owners. At its peak in 2023, the job-work rate stood at 25 paisa per pick, translating to roughly ₹15 per metre of fabric. On monthly sales of ₹675,000, Sai Tex generated a net profit of approximately ₹468,000 after total expenses of ₹207,000 (see Exhibit 1).¹³

The bond between Patil and his workers went beyond the transactional. He did not see his payroll as a line item; he saw school fees, hospital bills, and families whose stability depended on Sai Tex's continued operation. Patil had grown up watching his father, Kisanrao Patil — a politician and a figure widely respected in the community for his social work — devote himself to the welfare of others. The elder Patil's words had stayed with his son: "If you have the means to give someone a livelihood, you have the duty to do it."¹⁴ This identification between the founder and the enterprise — the sense that the mill was not merely a business but an extension of Patil's identity and social obligations — was both Sai Tex's greatest strength and, increasingly, its heaviest anchor.

By 2025, the "modern" machines of 2010 had become the relics of 2026. The Rifa looms, once cutting-edge, were now 15 years old. Their 300 RPM operating speed was a fraction of the 1,200 RPM that the latest air-jet looms could achieve (see Exhibit 4). The debt from the original 2010 investment had never been fully retired. Patil still owed ₹1.2 crore at 14 per cent per annum. The machines that secured this debt were now worth roughly ₹2 lakh each on the resale market — a total liquidation value of ₹20 lakh against ₹1.2 crore in outstanding loans. Sai Tex was no longer a growth story; it was a high-leverage survival act.

## THE PERFECT STORM

Three external forces converged between 2024 and 2026 to push Sai Tex from stagnation into crisis.

**The Tariff Shock.** In August 2024, the United States imposed steep tariffs on Indian fabric exports. Fabric export volumes from Ichhalkaranji fell by approximately 40 per cent.¹⁵ Large export-oriented units diverted surplus fabric into the domestic market, creating a classic bullwhip effect: a demand shock at the export end amplified into a price collapse at the production end. For Sai Tex, the pick rate dropped from 25 paisa in 2023 to 17–18 paisa by 2025, and the realized price per metre fell from ₹15 to as low as ₹6.40 in the worst months (see Exhibit 2). The China+1 sourcing strategy that some industry observers had predicted would benefit Indian textile MSMEs had, as of early 2026, produced no discernible positive impact on units like Sai Tex.

**The Energy Squeeze.** Electricity costs in Maharashtra had been rising at 10 to 15 per cent every two years.¹⁶ Sai Tex's monthly energy bill had climbed from ₹45,000 in 2023 to ₹90,000 by 2025 — a 100 per cent increase (see Exhibit 1). The Rifa machines offered no energy-efficiency features. Unlike the newer inverter-driven looms that competitors were installing, the rapier machines had no idle-state power management and no variable-speed drives.

**The Labour Drain.** Workers left Sai Tex not because of poor conditions, but because newer machines at other units offered approximately 10 per cent higher salaries. Patil was operating at roughly 50 per cent capacity — production had swung wildly through 2025, from a high of 45,000 metres in January and August to a low of 25,000 metres in October (see Exhibit 2). Labour costs had doubled from ₹150,000 per month in 2023 to ₹300,000 in 2025, even as the workforce shrank — a reflection of the wage inflation required to retain even a skeleton crew.

The combined effect was a vicious cycle. Lower capacity meant higher per-unit fixed costs. Higher costs meant lower margins. Lower margins meant an inability to raise wages. An inability to raise wages meant further worker attrition. Patil was running as fast as he could just to stay in the same place — and falling behind nonetheless.¹⁷

The financial picture was stark (see Exhibit 1). In 2023, Sai Tex had generated monthly sales of ₹675,000 against total expenses of ₹207,000, yielding a net profit of ₹468,000. By 2025, monthly sales had collapsed to ₹486,000 while total expenses had ballooned to ₹510,000 — yielding a net loss of ₹24,000 per month. Interest payments alone had risen tenfold, from ₹12,000 to ₹120,000 per month, consuming nearly 25 per cent of total revenue.

Adding to the pressure, Sai Tex had received a notice from the central government requiring the unit to complete exports worth ₹10 lakh or face a penalty fine of ₹6 lakh — a mandate nearly impossible to fulfil given that the export market had contracted by 60 per cent.¹⁸

## THE SALE-PURCHASE QUESTION

The job-work model that had sustained Sai Tex for 15 years was, by its very design, a low-margin, low-control arrangement. The weaver bore all the production costs but had no say in the price of the output. The alternative was the sale-purchase model: buy yarn directly, weave it into fabric, and sell the finished product independently. Patil estimated that the initial working capital required — covering two months of yarn inventory and storage space — would be approximately ₹25 lakh.¹⁹

The shift was not merely financial; it was a return to skills Patil had not exercised in fifteen years. Before founding Sai Tex, he had worked in larger textile operations where he had gained direct exposure to yarn procurement, buyer negotiations, and the sale-purchase model's economics. He understood the mechanics but had deliberately set those skills aside in 2010. Now, market conditions were forcing the question: could he reactivate capabilities that had lain dormant for a decade and a half? He would need to manage inventory risk, bear the cost of unsold fabric, and compete not just with other units making the same pivot but with the large export-oriented companies that had recently flooded the domestic market. Several other units in Ichhalkaranji were also contemplating the same transition, which meant that the sale-purchase market itself was becoming more competitive even as units entered it.

## THREE PATHS FORWARD

By late 2025, Patil had distilled his options into three distinct paths. Each was mutually exclusive in practice — the financial and operational commitments of any one path would preclude the others.

**Option A: The Green Technical Pivot.** Replace all ten Rifa machines with twelve high-speed air-jet looms at a cost of ₹3.6 crore, install a rooftop solar plant for ₹50 lakh to eliminate the electricity bill, and simultaneously pivot to the sale-purchase model with ₹25 lakh in working capital. Total new investment: approximately ₹4.35 crore. Combined with the existing ₹1.2 crore debt, total obligations would reach ₹5.55 crore. At 14 per cent interest, the annual debt service alone would approach ₹77.7 lakh (see Exhibit 3).

The air-jet looms operated at 1,200 RPM — four times the speed of the Rifa machines — and each could produce 400 metres per day compared to the Rifa's 100 metres (see Exhibit 4). With twelve machines, the unit's daily capacity would leap from 1,000 metres to 4,800 metres. Under the sale-purchase model, Patil would capture the full product margin rather than the shrinking per-pick fee. If the assumptions held, monthly production could reach approximately 144,000 metres. Even a modest margin of ₹12–15 per metre would translate to monthly revenue of ₹17–22 lakh. After debt service of approximately ₹6.5 lakh per month, net profit could range from ₹10–15 lakh — a figure that would transform Sai Tex from a loss-making unit into one of the most profitable operations in the Park. The new machines would also reverse the labour drain: air-jet looms were precisely the technology that attracted migrant workers to competing units. The rooftop solar plant would make Sai Tex one of the few energy-independent units in Ichhalkaranji.

The Maharashtra Textile Policy 2023–28 offered capital subsidies of up to 25 per cent for eligible modernization investments under MAHA-TUFS, potentially reducing the effective investment by up to ₹1 crore. However, Patil had not been aware of this subsidy until the case researcher informed him — and his lack of formal financial records made the application process daunting.²⁰

The risk was existential. The bank required Patil's personal bungalow as additional collateral. If the domestic fabric market softened further, if the subsidy was delayed, or if the air-jet looms could not run at capacity from day one, the interest on ₹5.55 crore at 14 per cent would consume not just the business but the family home. Two competitors within the Pride Co-operative Park — M.F. Rathi Textiles and P.A. Patil Textiles — had already made the leap. Their success was both an inspiration and a source of anxiety: it proved the upgrade could work, but it also meant that every month Sai Tex delayed, its competitive position eroded further.

**Option B: The Hybrid Lean Model.** Instead of a wholesale replacement, retrofit the existing Rifa machines with energy-efficient inverter motors and acoustic shielding. Estimated cost: ₹40–50 lakh. This would reduce the energy bill by 20–30 per cent — a saving of approximately ₹18,000–27,000 per month — and extend the operational life of the current machines by several years. The acoustic shielding would improve working conditions on the factory floor, which could slow the rate of worker attrition even if it could not reverse it entirely. The ₹40–50 lakh investment was serviceable from current cash flow and existing credit lines without requiring additional collateral — a critical distinction from Option A.

A pro-forma monthly analysis illustrated the potential. Current monthly expenses stood at ₹510,000. With inverter-driven energy savings of ₹18,000–27,000, the adjusted expense base would fall to approximately ₹483,000–492,000. Against average monthly revenue of ₹350,000, the unit would still operate at a loss — but the monthly deficit would narrow from ₹24,000 to near break-even or a small surplus in stronger months when production exceeded 40,000 metres. The retrofit would not eliminate the bleeding, but it would slow it to a manageable rate.

The retrofit could be completed in four to six weeks without halting production, unlike Option A which would require a two- to three-month shutdown for installation, commissioning, and worker retraining on the new air-jet technology. During that transition period, Sai Tex would generate zero revenue while continuing to service its existing debt — a cash-flow gap that Option B avoided entirely.

Option B also preserved optionality. If the MAHA-TUFS subsidy became accessible within two years — particularly if the government simplified the documentation requirements for units without formal audited accounts — Patil could apply for the full modernization from a position of reduced losses rather than desperation. A unit that was nearly breaking even would be a more credible applicant than one haemorrhaging cash. The retrofit would buy Sai Tex two to three years: time to accumulate savings toward a future full upgrade, time for the export market to recover, and time for the subsidy bureaucracy to become navigable. It was the "live to fight another day" option.

It would not, however, solve the fundamental problem: Sai Tex would still be weaving low-value fabric at ₹6.40–₹9.00 per metre on machines that could not match the throughput of competitors. The job-work rate would remain at the mercy of market forces that Patil could not influence. Option B stabilized the patient but did not cure the disease.

**Option C: Asset Liquidation and Exit.** Sell the ten Rifa machines at their current resale value of ₹2 lakh each — ₹20 lakh total — use the proceeds to partially retire the ₹1.2 crore debt, and close the factory (see Exhibit 3). This would immediately stop the monthly losses and preserve whatever equity remained in the family's land and personal assets.

The arithmetic, however, was sobering. The ₹20 lakh from machine sales would cover less than 17 per cent of the ₹1.2 crore outstanding debt. The remaining ₹1 crore would need to be restructured over three to five years — meaning Patil would continue making payments on machines he no longer owned. At 14 per cent interest, even a restructured repayment schedule would consume ₹12–14 lakh per year in interest alone, a burden that would persist long after the factory floor fell silent.

The human cost was equally stark. Closure would displace the twelve permanent employees and their families, plus the temporary workers who depended on peak-season work, in a town where alternative employment was scarce and shrinking. In Ichhalkaranji, where a mill owner's identity was inseparable from his factory, closure carried a stigma that extended beyond business failure. The weaving community was tight-knit; a shuttered unit was not merely a commercial event but a social one — a signal that the owner had been unable to fulfil the obligations that came with running a mill.

Patil had considered redirecting his energy into trading or consulting for other mill owners — leveraging his 15 years of operational knowledge without the capital burden of ownership. He understood yarn markets, production scheduling, and the mechanics of job-work negotiations better than most. Several retired mill owners in the cluster had made similar transitions, earning modest but stable incomes as intermediaries between merchants and active weavers. But trading required working capital he did not have, and consulting fees in Ichhalkaranji were modest — unlikely to service the residual debt while supporting his family.

His family was supportive — they wanted him to turn the business around — but not at the cost of additional stress and debt that could jeopardize the household.²¹ There was no internal resistance to the idea of upgrading; everyone agreed that new machines were the answer. The question was not whether to modernize but how to finance it — and whether the business, already haemorrhaging cash, could survive the transition.

## THE DECISION

On a Sunday evening in April 2026, Patil sat in his small office adjacent to the factory floor. On one side of his desk lay the bank's loan application for the ₹3.6 crore modernization, with a yellow sticky note marking the page where he would need to sign over the bungalow. On the other side lay a draft "For Sale" listing for the ten Rifa machines, prepared by a broker who had already found a buyer in a smaller town where rapier looms were still considered modern.

Outside, the five running looms clattered on. The five idle ones stood silent. Somewhere in the workers' quarters, Shivmani Mishra was probably wondering whether to start looking for work at one of the upgraded mills down the road. The export penalty notice from the central government — ₹6 lakh in fines if Sai Tex could not complete ₹10 lakh in exports — sat in a drawer, unanswered.

Growing up, Patil had watched his father — a politician and a man known across the community for his tireless social work — treat every person's livelihood as a personal obligation. That example had shaped Avinash's own conviction that running a factory was not just a commercial act but a social one. But the lives that depended on Sai Tex were now threatened by the very loyalty that kept the doors open — and the thread was running out.

The phone rang. Patil stared at the screen — it was the bank manager. He took a breath and answered.

"Avinash-ji, I need your answer by Monday. Are we processing the MAHA-TUFS subsidy application for the full modernization, or are we just renewing the existing cash credit limit?"²²

The air-jet looms could produce four times the output. The sale-purchase model could transform his revenue per metre. The solar plant could eliminate his largest variable cost. The subsidy could offset a quarter of the capital expenditure. But the math also assumed that the domestic fabric market would hold, that the subsidy would be disbursed on time, that the air-jet looms would run at capacity from day one, and that the migrant workers would return to a factory that was, for now, still running 15-year-old machines at half capacity. If any of those assumptions failed, the interest on ₹5.55 crore at 14 per cent would consume not just the business but the family home.
