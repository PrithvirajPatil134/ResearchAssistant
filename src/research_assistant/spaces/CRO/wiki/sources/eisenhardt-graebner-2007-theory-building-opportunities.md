## Summary

Eisenhardt and Graebner address the opportunities and challenges of theory building from cases, particularly multiple cases. The paper defines the research strategy as "using one or more cases to create theoretical constructs, propositions and/or midrange theory from case-based, empirical evidence" (p. 25, citing Eisenhardt 1989b). It provides practical guidance on justifying inductive research, theoretical sampling, managing interview data bias, presenting empirical evidence, and writing emergent theory.

[Source: Session 4_Theory building from cases opportunities and challenges.pdf, pp. 25-32]

## Key Arguments

### Definition and Core Logic

Building theory from cases involves "recursive cycling among the case data, emerging theory, and later, extant literature" (p. 25). Central to the approach is **replication logic**: "each case serves as a distinct experiment that stands on its own as an analytic unit. Like a series of related laboratory experiments, multiple cases are discrete experiments that serve as replications, contrasts, and extensions to the emerging theory" (p. 25, citing Yin 1994).

The method produces theory that is "accurate, interesting, and testable" because it is "deeply embedded in rich empirical data" (p. 26). It bridges qualitative evidence to mainstream deductive research.

[Source: pp. 25-26]

### Justifying Theory Building

Researchers must justify why a research question is "better addressed by theory-building rather than theory-testing research" (p. 26). Two justification approaches:

- **Theory-driven questions**: frame within existing theory, show how inductive methods are necessary. Justification rests on "the ability of qualitative data to offer insight into complex social processes that quantitative data cannot easily reveal" (p. 27).
- **Phenomenon-driven questions**: frame in terms of the phenomenon's importance and "the lack of plausible existing theory" (p. 27). Conflicting theories alone are not enough; the researcher must demonstrate existing research "does not address the research question at all, or does so in a way that is inadequate or likely to be untrue" (p. 26).

Theory-building research answers "how" and "why" questions well but is "ill-equipped to address the questions 'how often,' and 'how many'" (p. 27).

[Source: pp. 26-27]

### Theoretical Sampling of Cases

Cases are "selected because they are particularly suitable for illuminating and extending relationships and logic among constructs" (p. 27). Not random or stratified sampling. Reasons for selection include: "revelation of an unusual phenomenon, replication of findings from other cases, contrary replication, elimination of alternative explanations, and elaboration of the emergent theory" (p. 27).

**Single cases**: chosen because "unusually revelatory, extreme exemplars, or opportunities for unusual research access" (p. 27).

**Multiple cases**: provide "a stronger base for theory building" (p. 27). They "enable comparisons that clarify whether an emergent finding is simply idiosyncratic to a single case or consistently replicated by several cases" (p. 27). A key approach is **polar types**: "a researcher samples extreme (e.g., very high and very low performing) cases in order to more easily observe contrasting patterns in the data" (p. 28).

Adding cases has outsize impact: "adding three cases to a single-case study is modest in terms of numbers, but offers four times the analytic power" (p. 28).

[Source: pp. 27-28]

### Managing Interview Data Bias

Key mitigations:

1. **Numerous and diverse informants**: "organizational actors from different hierarchical levels, functional areas, groups, and geographies, as well as actors from other relevant organizations and outside observers" (p. 28). "It is unlikely that these varied informants will engage in convergent retrospective sensemaking and/or impression management" (p. 28).

2. **Combine retrospective and real-time cases** (p. 28, citing Leonard-Barton 1990). Retrospective cases build breadth efficiently; real-time cases "help to mitigate retrospective sensemaking and impression management" (p. 28).

[Source: pp. 28-29]

### Presenting Empirical Evidence

For multiple-case research, the challenge is staying within spatial constraints while conveying both theory and evidence. The recommended approach:

- Organize the paper around the theory, with "each part of the theory demonstrated by evidence from at least some of the cases" (p. 29).
- Use **construct tables** that "summarize the case evidence and indicate how the focal construct is 'measured,' thus increasing the 'testability' of the theory" (p. 29).
- Tables and visual aids complement selective text descriptions and "further emphasize the rigor and depth of the empirical grounding of the theory" (p. 29).

[Source: pp. 29-30]

### Writing Emergent Theory

Write theory in multiple ways within a single paper (p. 30):

1. Sketch emergent theory in the introduction.
2. Write each proposition linked to supporting empirical evidence for each construct and relationship.
3. Write the underlying theoretical arguments providing "the logical link between the constructs within a proposition" (p. 30).
4. Provide a visual theory summary (diagram or summary table).

Multiple-case theory tends to be more parsimonious than single-case theory because researchers "retain only the relationships that are replicated across most or all of the cases" (p. 30). This parsimony is a strength: "parsimony, robustness, and generalizability characterize superior theory" (p. 30).

[Source: p. 30]

### Recapitulation of Eisenhardt (1989b)

The paper references and builds on Eisenhardt's (1989b) "Building theories from case study research" (AMR, 14: 532-550), which established the foundational process including: getting started (research question), selecting cases, crafting instruments, entering the field, analyzing within-case data, searching for cross-case patterns, shaping hypotheses, enfolding literature, and reaching closure.

[Source: pp. 25, 27, references p. 31]

## Methodology

Commentary/editorial format published in Academy of Management Journal. Draws on the authors' extensive experience with case-based research projects. No primary data collection.

[Source: p. 25]

## Findings

Not applicable (conceptual/methodological paper). The paper's contribution is prescriptive guidance for researchers using theory-building from cases.

## Relevance to Research

For the agentic AI governance research using exploratory sequential mixed methods:

- Justifies the qualitative Phase 1 as appropriate when "existing research either does not address the research question at all, or does so in a way that is inadequate" (p. 26). The agentic AI governance research question meets this criterion since no existing theory specifically addresses governance of goal-directed AI agents.
- The polar types sampling strategy applies: selecting firms with very mature vs. very immature agentic AI governance for maximum construct clarity.
- Construct tables provide the bridge between qualitative Phase 1 constructs and quantitative Phase 2 operationalization.
- Replication logic validates the multi-case approach for building governance maturity constructs.

[Source: pp. 25-32]

## Limitations

- Brief commentary format (8 pages) limits depth on any single topic.
- Assumes readers already know Eisenhardt (1989b); without that foundation, some prescriptions lack full context.
- Does not address mixed-methods designs where case research feeds quantitative testing (the sequential approach used in the agentic AI governance research).

[Source: pp. 25-32]
