## Summary

Sutton and Staw identify five elements commonly mistaken for theory in organizational research: references, data, variables/constructs, diagrams, and hypotheses. Drawing on editorial experience at ASQ and Research in Organizational Behavior, they argue that none of these elements alone constitutes theory because they do not explain *why* empirical patterns occur. The paper also addresses structural problems in how journals handle the theory-method tradeoff and recommends that journals relax empirical standards when theoretical contributions are strong.

[Source: Session 3_what theory is not_complete_20260425.pdf, pp. 371-384]

## Key Arguments

### Five Things That Are Not Theory

1. **References are not theory** (pp. 371-372). Listing citations to prior theoretical work does not constitute theory. Authors must explicate "which concepts and causal arguments are adopted from cited sources and how they are linked to the theory being developed or tested" (p. 372). References can serve as "a smoke screen to hide the absence of theory" (p. 372).

2. **Data are not theory** (pp. 373-374). Empirical patterns (beta weights, factor loadings, informant statements) describe *which* patterns were observed; theory explains *why* they were observed (p. 373, citing Kaplan 1964). "Prior findings cannot by themselves motivate hypotheses, and the reporting of results cannot substitute for causal reasoning" (p. 374). Applies equally to qualitative data: "The data do not generate theory-only researchers do that" (p. 374, citing Mintzberg 1979: 584).

3. **Lists of variables or constructs are not theory** (pp. 375-376). A theory must explain "why variables or constructs come about or why they are connected" (p. 375, citing Weick 1989: 517 quoting Homans). Listing demographic characteristics, categorizing determinants, or ordering variables in LISREL models does not make a theoretical argument.

4. **Diagrams are not theory** (pp. 376-377). Figures "should be considered as stage props rather than the performance itself" (p. 376, citing Whetten 1989). Diagrams rarely explain *why* proposed connections will be observed. One indication of strong theory: "it is possible to discern conditions in which the major proposition or hypothesis is most and least likely to hold" (p. 377).

5. **Hypotheses (or predictions) are not theory** (pp. 376-378). "Hypotheses are concise statements about what is expected to occur, not why it is expected to occur" (p. 377). Two telltale signs of hypotheses substituting for theory: too many hypotheses for any to be adequately explained, and introductions ending with long lists of predictions "only tenuously linked to causal explanations" (p. 378).

### What Strong Theory Is

Theory is "about the connections among phenomena, a story about why acts, events, structure, and thoughts occur" (p. 378). Strong theory "delves into underlying processes so as to understand the systematic reasons for a particular occurrence or nonoccurrence" (p. 378). It "burrows deeply into microprocesses, laterally into neighboring concepts, or in an upward direction, tying itself to broader social phenomena" (p. 378). As Weick (1995) put it: "a good theory explains, predicts, and delights" (p. 378).

[Source: pp. 378-379]

### Structural Problems in Journals

The paper identifies a double bind: journals demand both strong theory and precise measurement, but reviewers reject papers where theoretical variables are not fully operationalized (pp. 380-381). This produces "hypocritical writing" where "theory is crafted around the data" and peripheral processes are omitted to avoid exposing gaps in empirical design (p. 381).

[Source: pp. 380-381]

### Recommendations

- Journals should relax empirical standards when theories are "particularly interesting" (p. 382).
- Data can be "more illustrative than definitive" when theory is strong (p. 382).
- Not everything in the theoretical introduction needs to be operationalized (p. 382).
- Authors should be "rewarded rather than punished for developing strong conceptual arguments that dig deeper and extend more broadly than the data will justify" (p. 382).

[Source: pp. 381-383]

## Methodology

Conceptual essay drawing on editorial experiences at ASQ and Research in Organizational Behavior. No empirical data collection. The paper synthesizes consensus among reviewers, editors, and readers about what does not constitute theory.

[Source: p. 371]

## Relevance to Research

This paper serves as a foundational reference for crafting the theoretical sections of any research paper. For the agentic AI governance research:

- Provides the standard against which theoretical arguments must be evaluated: every claim about governance-risk relationships must explain the causal mechanism, not just list variables or cite prior findings.
- The five "wrong way" signs apply directly to literature review writing: avoid listing theories by name without explicating their logic; avoid using empirical results from prior studies as substitutes for theoretical reasoning.
- The recommendations about journals accepting illustrative data support the exploratory sequential design where qualitative Phase 1 produces theory that Phase 2 tests.

[Source: pp. 371-384]

## Limitations

- Written in 1995; the journal landscape has shifted, though the core arguments about theory remain widely cited.
- Focuses on organizational research; applicability to IS research requires recognizing that IS journals have their own theory norms (e.g., Gregor 2006 taxonomy of theory types).
- Does not address what makes theory *strong* beyond identifying what theory is *not*; the authors acknowledge this more modest aim (p. 372).

[Source: pp. 371-372]
