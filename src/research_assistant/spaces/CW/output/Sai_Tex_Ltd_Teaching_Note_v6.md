## 1. SYNOPSIS

In April 2026, Avinash Kisanrao Patil, founder of Sai Tex Ltd. — a small powerloom unit in Ichhalkaranji, Maharashtra — faced an existential decision. His ten Rifa Electronic Rapier looms, purchased in 2010 for ₹1.2 crore (US$144,600),¹ were running at half capacity. A United States tariff shock had collapsed job-work rates below break-even, migrant workers were leaving for competitors with air-jet looms, and energy costs had doubled. The unit was losing approximately ₹24,000 every month (case Exhibit 1). Patil had three options: a ₹4.35 crore green technical pivot to air-jet looms with solar power and a sale-purchase business model, a ₹40–50 lakh hybrid retrofit of existing machines, or asset liquidation and exit. The bank would finance the full modernization — but only if Patil pledged his family's bungalow as additional collateral. Twelve permanent employees and up to ten temporary workers depended on Sai Tex for their livelihoods. The case requires students to integrate competitive analysis, financial modelling, and socio-emotional wealth considerations to recommend a course of action for a resource-constrained MSME.

A downloadable spreadsheet containing the case exhibit data is available for instructors who wish to have students build their own financial models during pre-class preparation or in-class exercises.

## 2. LEARNING OBJECTIVES

This case is set in the Indian textile MSME sector, a market undergoing rapid technological disruption while thousands of family-owned firms struggle to adapt. It presents students with the task of evaluating strategic alternatives for a resource-constrained firm facing simultaneous competitive, financial, and social pressures.

After reading and analysing this case, students will be able to do the following:

1. Analyse how macro-level disruptions (trade policy, energy inflation, labour mobility) cascade into a micro-level MSME crisis, using PESTEL and Porter's Five Forces frameworks.
2. Evaluate three strategic alternatives (full modernization, hybrid retrofit, and exit) by conducting break-even analysis, debt-service calculations, and scenario modelling under resource constraints.
3. Assess the role of non-financial factors — specifically socio-emotional wealth (SEW), founder identity, and worker welfare — in shaping strategic decisions that deviate from purely financial optimization.
4. Design a phased implementation plan that sequences investments to manage risk, rather than treating modernization as a single binary decision.

These objectives guide students through a learning path progressing from environmental analysis (LO1) to financial evaluation (LO2), then to stakeholder integration (LO3), and finally to actionable strategic design (LO4).

## 3. POSITION IN COURSE

The case is designed for graduate and executive management programmes, particularly for courses in strategic management, operations management, entrepreneurship, and family business. It is also suitable for undergraduate capstone courses in business strategy.

| Course | Module | Positioning |
|:-------|:-------|:------------|
| Strategic Management (MBA/PGDM) | Competitive dynamics, technology disruption, MSME strategy | Second half of course, after students have been introduced to PESTEL, Porter's Five Forces, and basic financial analysis |
| Operations Management | Capacity planning, throughput analysis, Theory of Constraints | Mid-course, when students can apply quantitative tools to real-world operational data |
| Entrepreneurship / Family Business | Socio-emotional wealth, founder identity, growth vs. survival | Late in course, as an integrative case requiring synthesis across financial and non-financial dimensions |
| Executive Education | MSME competitiveness, cluster development, Make in India policy | Standalone session or paired with a policy case |

Prerequisites: Before beginning the case, students should have a theoretical understanding of industry analysis frameworks (PESTEL, Five Forces), basic financial analysis (break-even, debt service), and capacity utilization concepts. Familiarity with the socio-emotional wealth construct is helpful but not required — the case can serve as the introduction to SEW.

Expected Outcomes: The case works well as a capstone integrative exercise that requires students to synthesize across strategy, finance, operations, and organizational behaviour. Students should leave the session understanding that MSME strategy cannot be reduced to financial optimization alone, and that implementation sequencing is itself a strategic variable.

Session Duration: A 75-minute session is recommended. A 90-minute session allows for the optional role-play extension described in the teaching plan.

## 5. ASSIGNMENT QUESTIONS

The following questions should be distributed to students before class. They are designed to guide pre-class preparation and structure the in-class discussion. Each question maps to one or more learning objectives.

1. Break-Even Analysis (LO2): Using the data in case Exhibits 1, 2, and 7, calculate Sai Tex's monthly break-even volume under the current job-work model. Is break-even achievable given the constraints described in the case? What does this tell you about the viability of the status quo?

2. Environmental Analysis (LO1): Conduct a PESTEL analysis of the Ichhalkaranji textile cluster as of April 2026. Which factor represents the most significant threat to Sai Tex, and which represents the most significant opportunity?

3. Option Evaluation (LO2, LO3): Evaluate the three strategic options available to Patil (case Exhibit 4). For each option, assess the financial viability, the risk to the family, and the impact on the workers who depend on Sai Tex. Which option would you recommend, and why?

4. Implementation Design (LO4): Patil's family wants him to "turn this into a profitable business without adding more stress and debt." Is this possible? Design a phased implementation plan that addresses the family's concerns while moving toward long-term viability.

The board plan is organized into three panels corresponding to the major discussion segments. The instructor builds each panel progressively as the discussion unfolds.

        ↓
Export orders collapse (−40%)
        ↓
Large export-oriented units divert surplus → domestic market
        ↓
Domestic oversupply → job-work rates fall (25 → 17–18 paisa/pick)
        ↓
Revenue per metre collapses (₹15 → ₹6.40–₹9.00)
        ↓
Revenue below break-even → monthly loss of ₹24,000 (case Exhibit 1)
        ↓
Cannot match competitor wages → workers leave for air-jet units
        ↓
Capacity drops to 50% → fixed costs spread over fewer metres
        ↓
Per-unit cost rises → losses deepen → THE RED QUEEN TRAP


Instructor note: Ask students to identify where Patil could have broken the chain. Common responses: "He should have upgraded earlier" (hindsight bias — useful teaching moment); "He should have diversified customers" (limited agency under job-work model); "He should have built financial reserves" (valid, but MSME cash constraints are real). The most insightful response is that the chain has no single breakable link for a firm of Sai Tex's size — the vulnerability is structural, not managerial.

### Question 1: Break-Even and the Viability of the Status Quo

Assignment Question: Using the data in case Exhibits 1, 2, and 7, calculate Sai Tex's monthly break-even volume under the current job-work model. Is break-even achievable given the constraints described in the case? What does this tell you about the viability of the status quo?

Theoretical Framework: Theory of Constraints (Goldratt & Cox, 1984). The break-even calculation reveals that the binding constraint is not production capacity but the structural economics of the job-work model itself. When the contribution margin is negligible or negative, no realistic volume of production can cover fixed costs — the system is fundamentally broken, not merely underperforming.

Analysis:

The break-even calculation requires students to separate fixed and variable costs from the case exhibits.

From case Exhibit 7, at a production volume of 45,000 metres (near-peak single-shift capacity), total production expenses excluding interest are ₹287,900, yielding a cost per metre of ₹6.40 and a selling price per metre of ₹7.68. The gross margin per metre is ₹1.28. However, the monthly interest burden of ₹120,000 (case Exhibit 1) must also be covered. When interest is included, total monthly cost rises to ₹407,900 against revenue of ₹345,600, yielding a net loss of ₹62,300 even at near-peak production.

Students should recognize that the ₹1.28 gross margin per metre (case Exhibit 7) must cover not only interest but also the gap between the base payroll of ₹200,900 (case Exhibit 6) and the total labour cost of ₹300,000 (case Exhibit 1), which reflects overtime, temporary workers, and variable labour expenses.

Simplified Break-Even Model (see Exhibit TN-3 for full scenario analysis):

| Component | Value | Source |
|:----------|:------|:-------|
| Revenue per metre (range) | ₹6.40–₹9.00 | Case Exhibit 3 (varies by pick quality and month) |
| Revenue per metre (annual average) | ₹8.04 | Case Exhibit 3: ₹3,650,860 ÷ 454,000 metres |
| Variable cost per metre (labour + energy) | ₹8.66 | Case Exhibit 2: labour ₹6.66/m + energy ₹2.00/m |
| Contribution margin per metre (at average) | (₹0.62) | Revenue ₹8.04 minus variable cost ₹8.66 |
| Contribution margin per metre (at best rate) | ₹0.34 | Revenue ₹9.00 minus variable cost ₹8.66 |
| Fixed costs per month | ~₹155,000 | Interest ₹120,000 + manager ₹20,000 + spare parts ₹12,000 + other ₹3,000 |
| Break-even volume (at ₹0.34 margin) | ~455,900 metres/month | Fixed costs ÷ contribution margin |
| Break-even volume (at negative margin) | Not achievable | Negative contribution margin |

The maximum single-shift capacity of the unit is approximately 45,000 metres per month (case Exhibit 3, January and August peaks). Even at the most favourable contribution margin (₹0.34, corresponding to the highest-quality picks at ₹9.00/metre), break-even requires approximately 455,900 metres — more than ten times the unit's maximum observed output. At the average selling price of ₹8.04 per metre, the contribution margin turns negative: every additional metre produced increases the loss. The status quo is structurally unviable.

A note on the two financial perspectives in the case: Case Exhibit 7 presents a production-cost view at 45,000 metres showing a gross margin of ₹1.28/metre before interest. Case Exhibit 2 presents a unit-cost view showing labour at ₹6.66/metre and energy at ₹2.00/metre. The difference arises because Exhibit 7 uses the base payroll of ₹200,900 (case Exhibit 6), while Exhibit 2 uses the total labour cost of ₹300,000 (case Exhibit 1), which includes overtime, temporary workers, and variable labour expenses. Students should be encouraged to work with both perspectives and reconcile them — this mirrors the real-world challenge of analysing a firm that lacks formal financial statements.

Expected Student Responses:

- *Quantitatively oriented students* will build the break-even model and arrive at the conclusion that the current model cannot reach break-even under any realistic scenario. They may debate whether certain costs (e.g., the manager's salary of ₹20,000) are truly fixed or could be reduced. This is a productive discussion — it highlights the difference between accounting flexibility and operational reality in a small firm where the manager is also the coordinator, purchaser, and delivery supervisor (case Exhibit 6).

- *Qualitatively oriented students* may skip the calculation and argue directly that "the numbers don't work." The instructor should push these students to show their work: "How do you know the numbers don't work? Show me the break-even point." This reinforces the discipline of grounding strategic arguments in data.

- *A common error* is to use the 2023 figures (when the unit was profitable at ₹15/metre and ₹468,000 monthly profit) as the baseline. The instructor should redirect: "The question asks about the current model. What has changed between 2023 and 2025 that makes the old math irrelevant?" This leads naturally into the PESTEL discussion.

- *A sophisticated response* will note the production volatility in case Exhibit 3 — output swung from 45,000 metres in August to 25,000 metres in October — and argue that the break-even problem is compounded by demand unpredictability. At lower volumes, fixed costs per metre rise sharply, as case Exhibit 7 notes.

Key Teaching Takeaway: The status quo is not an option. This is not a case about whether to change, but about which change to pursue. Establishing this early prevents students from spending class time defending inaction.

### Question 2: PESTEL Analysis of the Ichhalkaranji Cluster

Assignment Question: Conduct a PESTEL analysis of the Ichhalkaranji textile cluster as of April 2026. Which factor represents the most significant threat to Sai Tex, and which represents the most significant opportunity?

Theoretical Framework: PESTEL analysis (macro-environmental scanning) combined with Porter's Five Forces (industry-level competitive dynamics). The value of this question lies not in the checklist exercise but in identifying interactions between factors and distinguishing between structural vulnerabilities and triggering events.

Analysis:

| Factor | Key Forces | Impact on Sai Tex |
|:-------|:-----------|:------------------|
| Political | US tariff on Indian fabric exports (August 2024); Maharashtra Textile Policy 2023–28; MAHA-TUFS subsidy (up to 25% capital subsidy); export compliance notice requiring ₹10 lakh in exports or ₹6 lakh penalty (case Exhibit endnote 41) | The tariff is the proximate cause of the crisis — it triggered the domestic oversupply that collapsed job-work rates. The MAHA-TUFS subsidy is a potential lifeline, but Patil lacks the formal financial records to apply easily. The export penalty adds a further compliance burden on a unit already unable to export. |
| Economic | 14% interest rates for MSMEs; energy cost inflation (₹3.50 to ₹6.50 per unit, +86%, case Exhibit 2); yarn price inflation (20–47% across grades, case Exhibit 2); domestic oversupply from diverted exports | Every economic factor is moving against Sai Tex. The interest rate makes large capital investments extremely risky for a firm with no formal balance sheet. Yarn price inflation is particularly relevant if Patil pivots to the sale-purchase model. |
| Social | Migrant labour dependency (workforce entirely from UP and Bihar); worker mobility toward modern mills offering 10% higher wages; founder's social obligations to twelve permanent employees and their families; COVID-19 aftermath (factory shut during lockdown, took three years to recover to full capacity) | Labour is the binding constraint. Workers are rational economic actors who compare machinery across units. The social dimension — socio-emotional wealth — prevents Patil from making a purely financial exit decision. |
| Technological | Air-jet looms (1,200 RPM) replacing rapier looms (300 RPM); 4× production speed differential (case Exhibit 5); solar energy viability (₹50 lakh investment to eliminate ₹65,000–₹90,000/month energy bill) | Technology is both the threat (competitors upgrading) and the opportunity (Option A). The 4× speed differential is the core of the Red Queen dynamic. |
| Environmental | Rising energy costs (₹45,000 to ₹90,000/month, +100%, case Exhibit 1); solar energy as a hedge; no direct pollution compliance burden (Sai Tex does not process or dye fabric) | Solar investment has the clearest and most immediate return of any single investment — it eliminates the energy cost that case Exhibit 7 shows consuming ₹65,000/month even at reduced production. |
| Legal | MAHA-TUFS eligibility requirements; cooperative park regulations; lack of formal financial documentation; export compliance notice (₹10 lakh obligation or ₹6 lakh penalty) | Patil's informal record-keeping is a barrier to accessing the very subsidies designed to help MSMEs like Sai Tex. He was unaware of MAHA-TUFS until the case researcher informed him — a detail that underscores the information asymmetry facing MSMEs. |

Porter's Five Forces Overlay (see Exhibit TN-2):

Students should be encouraged to layer the Five Forces analysis onto the PESTEL to show how macro factors translate into industry-level competitive pressure. The key insight is that the tariff shock (Political) did not create the competitive vulnerability — it activated a latent technology gap (Technological) that had been building for years. Before August 2024, Patil was surviving with the same old machines. The tariff made the technology gap lethal.

Expected Student Responses:

- Students will likely identify the technological factor as the most significant threat, citing the 4× speed differential between Rifa and air-jet looms (case Exhibit 5). This is correct but incomplete. The instructor should probe: "Is technology the threat, or is it the tariff that made the technology gap lethal? Patil survived for fifteen years with the same machines."

- On the opportunity side, students will split between the MAHA-TUFS subsidy (political) and solar energy (environmental). Both are valid. The instructor can use this to illustrate that PESTEL factors interact: the subsidy reduces the financial barrier to the technological upgrade, while solar reduces the economic burden of energy costs.

- Some students may argue that the social factor (labour mobility) is the most significant threat because it is the binding constraint on current operations — production swung from 45,000 metres in August to 25,000 metres in October 2025 (case Exhibit 3), driven by worker departures. This is a sophisticated response that connects PESTEL to the Theory of Constraints.

- A common oversight is the legal factor — students often miss the export compliance notice (₹10 lakh export obligation or ₹6 lakh penalty) mentioned in the case. The instructor can use this to illustrate how regulatory burdens compound financial distress for MSMEs.

- Executive education participants may note the COVID-19 aftermath as a compounding social/economic factor — the case notes that the factory took three years to recover to full capacity after the lockdown, only for the tariff shock to knock it back down. This illustrates the concept of "stacked shocks" that MSMEs face.

Key Teaching Takeaway: PESTEL is not a checklist exercise. The value lies in identifying interactions between factors and distinguishing between structural vulnerabilities (technology gap, building since 2010) and triggering events (tariff shock, August 2024).

### Question 3: Evaluating the Three Strategic Options

Assignment Question: Evaluate the three strategic options available to Patil (case Exhibit 4). For each option, assess the financial viability, the risk to the family, and the impact on the workers who depend on Sai Tex. Which option would you recommend, and why?

Theoretical Frameworks: Red Queen Effect (Barnett & Hansen, 1996); Socio-Emotional Wealth (Gómez-Mejía et al., 2007; Berrone, Cruz, & Gómez-Mejía, 2012); Theory of Constraints (Goldratt & Cox, 1984).

This is the central question of the case and should consume the largest portion of class time. The analysis integrates financial modelling, competitive positioning, and socio-emotional wealth considerations.

Option A: The Green Technical Pivot (₹4.35 Crore New Investment)

Financial Analysis:

| Metric | Current State (Case Data) | Option A (Projected) |
|:-------|:--------------------------|:--------------------|
| Machines | 10 Rifa rapier looms, 300 RPM | 12 air-jet looms, 1,200 RPM (case Exhibit 5) |
| Daily capacity | 1,000 m (10 × 100 m/day) | 4,800 m (12 × 400 m/day) (case Exhibit 5) |
| Monthly capacity (single shift) | ~45,000 m (peak observed, case Exhibit 3) | 144,000 m |
| Revenue model | Job-work at 17–18 paisa/pick | Sale-purchase (full product margin) |
| Revenue per metre | ₹6.40–₹9.00 (case Exhibit 3) | ₹15–₹35 (depending on fabric grade) |
| Monthly energy cost | ₹65,000–₹90,000 (case Exhibits 2, 7) | Near zero (with ₹50 lakh solar plant) |
| Total debt | ₹1.2 Cr (existing) | ₹5.55 Cr (case Exhibit 4) |
| Monthly interest (at 14%) | ₹120,000 (case Exhibit 1) | ~₹647,500 (case Exhibit 4) |
| MAHA-TUFS subsidy potential | N/A | Up to ~₹1.09 Cr (25% of ₹4.35 Cr) |

The financial case for Option A is strong on paper. The case notes that at even moderate utilization, monthly revenue could reach ₹17–22 lakh, with net profit of ₹10–15 lakh after debt service (case Exhibit 4 assumptions). Risk to Family: The bank requires the family bungalow as additional collateral. If the modernization fails, the family loses both the business and their home. The wife's statement — "We want this to work, Avinash. But not at the cost of everything else" — signals that the family's risk tolerance has a hard limit.

Worker Impact: Positive. Air-jet looms require fewer workers per machine but the four-fold increase in throughput means the unit would likely need to hire additional staff. Modern machines also attract workers — the case notes that workers leave Sai Tex specifically because competitors have newer equipment offering 10% higher wages.

Financial Analysis: Retrofitting with inverter motors reduces the energy bill by an estimated 20–30% (saving ₹13,000–₹27,000 per month, or ₹2.16–3.24 lakh per year, per case Exhibit 4) but does not increase throughput. The energy savings do not close the ₹24,000/month loss gap (case Exhibit 1), let alone generate profit.

### 5. What Are the Key Takeaways? (Epilogue / Post-Case Wrap-Up)

The teaching note should specify 3–5 learning takeaways that the instructor can reinforce after discussion:

1. Financial literacy for small entrepreneurs: Operating at full capacity does not guarantee profitability. Unit economics (cost per metre vs. market price per metre) determine viability, not volume alone.

2. Capital structure and family risk: When personal assets (the family bungalow) serve as collateral for business debt, the entrepreneur's risk calculus fundamentally changes. This is not a corporate finance decision — it is a family welfare decision.

3. Industry structure analysis: The Surat powerloom sector exhibits classic signs of a fragmented industry undergoing consolidation through technology adoption. Small operators face a "modernize or exit" binary, not a "modernize or maintain" choice.

4. Sunk cost reasoning: Raju's 15 years of experience and his father's legacy create powerful psychological anchors. Students should recognize when emotional attachment to a business distorts rational decision-making.

5. The entrepreneurship paradox: The same persistence that makes entrepreneurs successful (Raju kept the unit running through multiple crises) can become destructive when the external environment has structurally shifted.

## PART III: CROSS-CASE PATTERN ANALYSIS — WHAT MAKES TEACHING NOTES EFFECTIVE

Having analyzed the HBSP guide principles and applied them to the Sai Tex case, the following patterns emerge from the KB's published case collection (C1–C15):

### Pattern 1: The Decision Anchor

Every effective teaching note centers on a specific decision that the protagonist must make. The HBSP guide emphasizes this as the "case problem." In the KB cases:
- **Sai Tex (C14)**: Invest ₹4.5 crore, retrofit for ₹40–50 lakh, or exit
- Cases that lack a clear decision point produce diffuse classroom discussions

### Pattern 2: Quantitative Scaffolding

The strongest teaching notes provide numerical frameworks that students can use to evaluate options. Exhibits with financial data (revenue, cost breakdowns, loan terms) are not decorative — they are the analytical backbone of the discussion. The Sai Tex case provides this through Exhibits 1–8.

### Pattern 3: The Emotional Dimension

The HBSP guide notes that cases work best when students feel the weight of the decision. Teaching notes should explicitly flag where emotional and rational analysis diverge. In Sai Tex, the family bungalow as collateral creates this tension — the financially optimal choice (exit) is emotionally the hardest.

### Pattern 4: Structured Ambiguity

Good teaching notes do not prescribe a "correct" answer. They provide analysis that makes each option defensible while highlighting the trade-offs. The instructor's role is to ensure all perspectives receive rigorous examination, not to reveal a hidden right answer.

### Pattern 5: Time-Phased Discussion Architecture

The HBSP guide's emphasis on "planning the discussion" translates to a temporal structure: opening (hook) → analysis (deep dive) → debate (options) → synthesis (takeaways). Teaching notes that specify timing for each phase produce more disciplined classroom experiences.

## 1. OPENING HOOK CONVENTIONS

Four distinct hook types emerge from this 5-case sample, consistent with the v4 corpus:

| Hook Type | Cases | Opening Line Pattern |
|-----------|-------|---------------------|
| Time-anchored protagonist | C4, C8 | "In [Month Year], [Full Name], the [title] of [Company], was [verb]…" |
| Industry-context-first | C2 | Macro trend paragraph → protagonist enters in paragraph 2 |
| Epigraph + narrative | C5 | Founder quote set apart → "By 2024, Café Greenr…" |
| Emotional sensory moment | C11 | "When Jamana Mahajan received the Digital Women Award… her heart filled with compassion, gratitude, and pride. Her eyes moistened…" |

The dominant formula (4/5 cases): protagonist named with full designation within the first two narrative sentences, paired with an emotional or cognitive verb signaling tension.

Emotional verbs observed in this batch: was considering (C4), hoped to gain (C8), moistened remembering (C11), had undergone a significant evolution (C5).

Key finding: C2 is the only case that delays protagonist introduction — it opens with an industry trend paragraph. This works because the "protagonist" is effectively Google's cross-functional team, not a single individual. All person-centered cases name the protagonist immediately.

All 5 TNs follow an identical section sequence (Ivey standard):

| Section | Present | Purpose |
|---------|---------|---------|

## 8. PUBLICATION READINESS CHECKLIST (Refined)

### A. Case Structure (mandatory — 5/5 compliance)

- [ ] Protagonist named with full name + title + company in first two narrative sentences
- [ ] Opening hook uses date/moment OR epigraph OR sensory detail
- [ ] Dilemma foreshadowed in paragraph 1, fully developed in closing section
- [ ] 5–9 ALL-CAPS section headings following: Origin → Context → Problem → Decision
- [ ] 3,500–5,500 words narrative + 1–5 exhibits (target 8–11 total pages)
- [ ] Ends with open questions, reflective pause, or decision imperative — never a solution

### B. Narrative Voice (mandatory — 5/5 compliance)

- [ ] Third-person past tense for protagonist actions
- [ ] Present tense only for current industry facts
- [ ] No theoretical framework names in case text (reserve for TN)
- [ ] Neutral tone — no advocacy, no editorializing
- [ ] Direct quotes: 0–2, always attributed
- [ ] Currency defined with exchange rate footnote on first use
- [ ] Numbers stated factually without adjectives

### C. Exhibits (mandatory — 5/5 compliance)

- [ ] All exhibits placed after narrative, never inline
- [ ] Numbered sequentially, ALL-CAPS titles
- [ ] Source attribution below every exhibit
- [ ] In-text (see Exhibit N) references (minimum 2)
- [ ] At least one financial/quantitative exhibit

### D. Teaching Note (mandatory — 5/5 compliance)

- [ ] Sections in order: Synopsis → Learning Objectives → Position in Course → Relevant Readings → Assignment Questions → Teaching Plan → Analysis → What Happened
- [ ] 4–6 learning objectives, verb-led, escalating Bloom's levels
- [ ] 4–5 assignment questions escalating from descriptive → analytical → prescriptive
- [ ] Teaching plan with minute-by-minute breakdown
- [ ] Analysis section answers each assignment question with framework application
- [ ] "What Happened" epilogue reveals actual outcome

### E. Pedagogical Design (12–14/14 from v4, confirmed 5/5 here)

- [ ] 2–4 explicit strategic alternatives with genuine trade-offs
- [ ] Minimum 3 stakeholders with conflicting interests
- [ ] Time pressure established (deadline, competitive threat, financial constraint)
- [ ] Personal stakes for protagonist (career, family, livelihood, reputation)
- [ ] Sufficient data for both quantitative and qualitative classroom discussion
- [ ] Decision point genuinely ambiguous — reasonable students will disagree

## 1. SYNOPSIS

In April 2026, Avinash Kisanrao Patil, founder of Sai Tex Ltd. — a small powerloom unit in Ichhalkaranji, Maharashtra — faced an existential decision. His ten Rifa Electronic Rapier looms, purchased in 2010 for ₹1.2 crore (US$144,600),¹ were running at half capacity. A United States tariff shock had collapsed job-work rates from 25 to 17–18 paisa per pick, migrant workers were leaving for competitors with newer air-jet looms, and energy costs had doubled. The unit was losing ₹24,000 every month (case Exhibit 1). Patil had three options: a ₹4.35 crore green technical pivot to air-jet looms with solar power and a sale-purchase business model, a ₹40–50 lakh hybrid retrofit of existing machines, or asset liquidation and exit. The bank would finance the full modernization — but only if Patil pledged his family's bungalow as additional collateral. Twelve permanent employees and up to ten temporary workers depended on Sai Tex for their livelihoods. The case presents a classic MSME survival dilemma in which financial analysis alone is insufficient — students must weigh competitive dynamics, family stakes, and socio-emotional wealth to arrive at a recommendation.

## 2. LEARNING OBJECTIVES

This case is set in the Indian textile MSME sector, a market undergoing rapid technological disruption while thousands of family-owned firms struggle to adapt. It presents students with the task of evaluating strategic alternatives for a resource-constrained firm facing simultaneous competitive, financial, and social pressures.

After reading and analysing this case, students will be able to do the following:

1. Analyse how macro-level disruptions (trade policy, energy inflation, labour mobility) cascade into a micro-level MSME crisis, using PESTEL and Porter's Five Forces frameworks.
2. Evaluate three strategic alternatives (full modernization, hybrid retrofit, and exit) by conducting break-even analysis, debt-service calculations, and scenario modelling under resource constraints.
3. Assess the role of non-financial factors — specifically socio-emotional wealth (SEW), founder identity, and worker welfare — in shaping strategic decisions that deviate from purely financial optimization.
4. Design a phased implementation plan that sequences investments to manage risk, rather than treating modernization as a single binary decision.

These objectives guide students through a learning path progressing from environmental analysis (LO1) to financial evaluation (LO2), then to stakeholder integration (LO3), and finally to actionable strategic design (LO4).

## 9. ANALYSIS

The analysis below presents planned discussion questions and expectations of how students may respond. The case has not yet been classroom-tested; instructors should treat the anticipated student responses as indicative rather than definitive.²

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━


### Question 1: Break-Even and the Viability of the Status Quo

Assignment Question: Using the data in case Exhibits 1, 2, and 7, calculate Sai Tex's monthly break-even volume under the current job-work model. Is break-even achievable given the constraints described in the case? What does this tell you about the viability of the status quo?

Theoretical Framework: Theory of Constraints (Goldratt & Cox, 1984). The break-even calculation reveals that the binding constraint is not production capacity but the structural economics of the job-work model itself. When the contribution margin is negative or negligible, no volume of production can cover fixed costs — the system is fundamentally broken, not merely underperforming.

Analysis:

The break-even calculation requires students to separate fixed and variable costs from the case exhibits.

From case Exhibit 7, at a production volume of 45,000 metres (near-peak single-shift capacity), total production expenses excluding interest are ₹287,900, the cost per metre is ₹6.40, and the selling price per metre is ₹7.68. The gross margin per metre is ₹1.28. However, the monthly interest burden of ₹120,000 (case Exhibit 1) must also be covered. When interest is included, total monthly cost rises to ₹407,900 against revenue of ₹345,600, yielding a net loss of ₹62,300 even at near-peak production.

Simplified Break-Even Model (see Exhibit TN-3 for full scenario analysis):

| Component | Value | Source |
|:----------|:------|:-------|
| Revenue per metre (range) | ₹6.40–₹9.00 | Case Exhibit 3 (varies by pick quality and month) |
| Revenue per metre (annual average) | ₹8.04 | Case Exhibit 3: ₹3,650,860 ÷ 454,000 metres |
| Variable cost per metre (labour + energy) | ₹8.66 | Case Exhibit 2: labour ₹6.66/m + energy ₹2.00/m |
| Contribution margin per metre (at average) | (₹0.62) | Revenue ₹8.04 minus variable cost ₹8.66 |
| Contribution margin per metre (at best rate) | ₹0.34 | Revenue ₹9.00 minus variable cost ₹8.66 |
| Fixed costs per month | ~₹155,000 | Interest ₹120,000 + spare parts ₹12,000 + manager ₹20,000 + other ₹3,000 |
| Break-even volume (at ₹0.34 margin) | ~455,900 metres/month | Fixed costs ÷ contribution margin |
| Break-even volume (at negative margin) | Not achievable | Negative contribution margin |

Students should note the critical distinction between the Exhibit 7 cost structure (which shows a positive gross margin of ₹1.28/metre at 45,000 metres, excluding interest) and the full-cost picture (which turns negative once interest is included). The maximum single-shift capacity of the unit is approximately 45,000 metres per month (case Exhibit 3, January and August peaks). Even at the most favourable contribution margin (₹0.34, corresponding to the highest-quality picks at ₹9.00/metre), break-even requires approximately 455,900 metres — more than ten times the unit's maximum observed output. At the average selling price of ₹8.04 per metre, the contribution margin turns negative: every additional metre produced increases the loss. The status quo is structurally unviable.

Expected Student Responses:

- *Quantitatively oriented students* will build the break-even model and arrive at the conclusion that the current model cannot reach break-even under any realistic scenario. They may debate whether certain costs (e.g., the manager's salary of ₹20,000) are truly fixed or could be reduced. This is a productive discussion — it highlights the difference between accounting flexibility and operational reality in a small firm where the manager is also the coordinator, purchaser, and delivery supervisor (case Exhibit 6).

- *Qualitatively oriented students* may skip the calculation and argue directly that "the numbers don't work." The instructor should push these students to show their work: "How do you know the numbers don't work? Show me the break-even point." This reinforces the discipline of grounding strategic arguments in data.

- *A common error* is to use the 2023 figures (when the unit was profitable at ₹15/metre and ₹468,000 monthly profit) as the baseline. The instructor should redirect: "The question asks about the current model. What has changed between 2023 and 2025 that makes the old math irrelevant?" This leads naturally into the PESTEL discussion.

- *A sophisticated response* will note the Exhibit 7 data showing a positive gross margin of ₹1.28/metre at 45,000 metres — and then demonstrate that this margin evaporates once interest is included. This distinction between production profitability and total profitability is a key teaching moment.

Key Teaching Takeaway: The status quo is not an option. This is not a case about whether to change, but about which change to pursue. Establishing this early prevents students from spending class time defending inaction.

Theoretical Framework: PESTEL analysis (macro-environmental scanning) combined with Porter's Five Forces (industry-level competitive dynamics). The value of this question lies not in the checklist exercise but in identifying interactions between factors and distinguishing between structural vulnerabilities and triggering events.

Analysis:

| Factor | Key Forces | Impact on Sai Tex |
|:-------|:-----------|:------------------|
| Political | US tariff on Indian fabric exports (August 2024); Maharashtra Textile Policy 2023–28; MAHA-TUFS subsidy (up to 25% capital subsidy); export compliance notice (₹10 lakh export obligation or ₹6 lakh penalty — case Exhibit 4 notes) | The tariff is the proximate cause of the crisis — it triggered the domestic oversupply that collapsed job-work rates. Porter's Five Forces Overlay (see Exhibit TN-2):

Students should be encouraged to layer the Five Forces analysis onto the PESTEL to show how macro factors translate into industry-level competitive pressure. The key insight is that the tariff shock (Political) did not create the competitive vulnerability — it activated a latent technology gap (Technological) that had been building for years. Before August 2024, Patil was surviving with the same old machines. The tariff made the technology gap lethal.

Expected Student Responses:

- Students will likely identify the technological factor as the most significant threat, citing the 4× speed differential between Rifa and air-jet looms (case Exhibit 5). This is correct but incomplete. The instructor should probe: "Is technology the threat, or is it the tariff that made the technology gap lethal? Patil survived for fifteen years with the same machines."

- On the opportunity side, students will split between the MAHA-TUFS subsidy (political) and solar energy (environmental). Both are valid. The instructor can use this to illustrate that PESTEL factors interact: the subsidy reduces the financial barrier to the technological upgrade, while solar reduces the economic burden of energy costs.

- Some students may argue that the social factor (labour mobility) is the most significant threat because it is the binding constraint on current operations — production swung from 45,000 metres in August to 25,000 metres in October 2025 (case Exhibit 3), driven by worker departures during the festival season. This is a sophisticated response that connects PESTEL to the Theory of Constraints.

- A common oversight is the legal factor — students often miss the export compliance notice (₹10 lakh export obligation or ₹6 lakh penalty) described in the case. The instructor can use this to illustrate how regulatory burdens compound financial distress for MSMEs.

- Executive education participants may raise the COVID-19 lockdown (Social) as a compounding factor — the case notes that the factory was shut during the pandemic, workers left, and it took three years to return to full capacity, only for the tariff shock to knock the unit back down. This illustrates how sequential shocks erode MSME resilience.

Key Teaching Takeaway: PESTEL is not a checklist exercise. The value lies in identifying interactions between factors and distinguishing between structural vulnerabilities (technology gap, building since 2010) and triggering events (tariff shock, August 2024).

## SYNOPSIS

Avinash Kisanrao Patil, founder and sole proprietor of Sai Tex Ltd., a job-work powerloom unit in Ichhalkaranji, Maharashtra, faces an existential decision in April 2026. His ten Rifa Electronic Rapier looms — purchased in 2010 for ₹1.2 crore (US$144,600) — are now 15 years old, operating at 300 RPM against the 1,200 RPM of modern air-jet looms that competitors have adopted. Three converging forces — a 40 per cent collapse in fabric exports following US tariffs, a doubling of electricity costs, and accelerating migrant labour attrition to upgraded mills — have pushed Sai Tex from a monthly profit of ₹468,000 in 2023 to a monthly loss of ₹24,000 in 2025. Patil must choose among three options: (A) a ₹4.35 crore "Green Technical Pivot" involving twelve new air-jet looms, a rooftop solar plant, and a shift to the sale-purchase business model — requiring his family bungalow as collateral; (B) a ₹40–50 lakh retrofit of existing machines to buy two to three years of survival; or (C) liquidation and exit. The case provides detailed financial, production, and technology data across seven exhibits, enabling quantitative analysis of each option. It is designed for courses in entrepreneurship, operations strategy, MSME management, and family business, and is suitable for MBA, executive education, and doctoral-level instruction. The case is accompanied by seven exhibits and 64 endnotes.

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After reading and analyzing this case study, students will be able to do the following:

1. Evaluate strategic options under resource constraints — Apply decision-analysis frameworks to compare mutually exclusive investment alternatives (full modernization, incremental retrofit, and exit) using the financial and operational data provided in the case exhibits.

2. Analyze the dynamics of technology-driven competitive decline — Identify how the Red Queen Effect operates in a traditional manufacturing cluster, where firms must continuously invest in technology merely to maintain their competitive position.

3. Assess the interaction between business model choice and competitive positioning — Compare the job-work model (low risk, low margin, no pricing power) with the sale-purchase model (higher margin, inventory risk, market access requirements) and evaluate the conditions under which a business model pivot is warranted.

5. Examine the role of socio-emotional wealth (SEW) in entrepreneurial decision-making — Analyze how non-financial considerations — family legacy, worker welfare, community identity, and the founder's sense of social obligation — shape strategic choices in ways that diverge from purely financial optimization.

## ANALYSIS

### 1. Break-Even Analysis (Assignment Question 1)

Data extraction from exhibits:

From Exhibit 7, at 45,000 metres production (near-peak single-shift capacity):
- Total production expenses (excluding interest): ₹287,900/month
- Bank interest: ₹120,000/month
- **Total monthly cost: ₹407,900**
- Selling price per metre: ₹7.68
- Cost per metre (production only): ₹6.40
- Gross margin per metre: ₹1.28

From Exhibit 1 (2025 averages):
- Total monthly expenses: ₹510,000
- Average monthly revenue: ₹486,000 (from "Financial Performance Stats")
- Average realized rate: ₹8.07/metre (from Exhibit 3 full-year average)

Break-even volume calculation:

Monthly fixed costs must be estimated by separating fixed and variable components. From the case data:

| Cost Component | Nature | Monthly Amount (₹) |
|:---------------|:-------|-------------------:|
| Bank interest | Fixed | 120,000 |
| Spare parts & mill store | Semi-fixed | 12,000 |
| Transport & hamali | Variable | 5,000 |
| Other expenses | Fixed | 5,000 |
| Power bill | Semi-variable | 65,000 |
| Labour (base payroll) | Fixed (in short run) | 200,900 |

Approximate fixed costs: ₹120,000 + ₹12,000 + ₹5,000 + ₹200,900 = ₹337,900/month

Variable cost per metre (power + transport, scaled): approximately ₹1.56/metre

At the 2025 average rate of ₹8.07/metre:
- Contribution margin per metre = ₹8.07 − ₹1.56 = ₹6.51/metre
- **Break-even volume = ₹337,900 ÷ ₹6.51 ≈ 51,900 metres/month**

This is critical: Sai Tex's peak single-shift production in 2025 was only 45,000 metres (January and August). The break-even volume exceeds the unit's maximum observed output. At the current cost structure and rate, Sai Tex cannot break even under any achievable production scenario.

Break-even rate calculation:

At the 2025 average production of ~37,800 metres/month (454,000 ÷ 12):
- Total cost = ₹337,900 + (₹1.56 × 37,800) = ₹337,900 + ₹58,968 = ₹396,868
- Required rate per metre = ₹396,868 ÷ 37,800 = ₹10.50/metre
- Required pick rate (at average 47 picks/cm) = approximately 22 paisa/pick

The 2023 rate was 25 paisa/pick. The 2025 rate averaged 17.5 paisa. Sai Tex would need a rate recovery of approximately 26 per cent from 2025 levels — a recovery that the case provides no evidence to expect, given the structural oversupply from diverted exports.

Key teaching point: The break-even analysis demonstrates that Sai Tex's problem is not merely cyclical (a temporary rate decline) but structural (the cost base has permanently outgrown the revenue capacity of the current technology). This is the quantitative foundation for the Red Queen argument.

### 2. Strategic Option Evaluation (Assignment Question 2)

Students should construct a decision matrix using the data from Exhibit 4. A sample framework:

| Criterion (Weight) | Option A: Green Pivot | Option B: Hybrid Lean | Option C: Exit |
|:--------------------|:---------------------:|:---------------------:|:--------------:|
| Financial viability (30%) | Medium-High: Transformative upside (₹10–15L/mo net profit) but extreme downside risk (₹5.55 cr debt at 14%) | Low: Does not reach break-even; slows the bleed but does not stop it | Medium: Stops losses immediately; ₹1 cr residual debt remains |
| Operational risk (25%) | High: Assumes full capacity from day one, labour returns, market holds, subsidy disbursed on time | Low: Proven technology; incremental change | None: Operations cease |
| Stakeholder impact (25%) | Positive if successful: retains and attracts workers; transforms community standing | Neutral-Negative: workers continue to leave; slow decline | Highly negative: 12+ permanent workers displaced; community loss |
| Strategic positioning (20%) | Strong: Matches competitors; enables sale-purchase pivot; energy independence | Weak: Remains in commodity trap with obsolete technology | None: Exits the industry |

Expected student debate:

Students will likely split into three camps:

- **Option A advocates** will argue that the break-even analysis (Q1) proves that incremental measures cannot save Sai Tex. The only path to viability is a step-change in capacity and business model. The MAHA-TUFS subsidy (up to 25%) and solar energy independence reduce the effective risk. Two competitors in the same Park have already succeeded with this path.

- **Option B advocates** will argue that Option A's assumptions are heroic: full capacity from day one, stable domestic market, timely subsidy disbursement, and labour returning to a unit that is still under construction. Option B preserves optionality — it buys 2–3 years during which market conditions may improve, subsidy access may become easier, or Patil may accumulate savings toward a future upgrade without risking the family home.

- **Option C advocates** (likely a minority) will argue that the rational economic decision is to cut losses. The ₹20 lakh resale value against ₹1.2 crore debt means Patil is already deeply underwater. Adding ₹4.35 crore of new debt on a 14% interest rate in a declining market is a gamble, not a strategy. Preserving the family's remaining assets is the responsible choice.

Instructor guidance: Push students to articulate the assumptions underlying each option. Option A's attractiveness depends entirely on four assumptions holding simultaneously: (1) domestic fabric market holds, (2) MAHA-TUFS subsidy is disbursed on time, (3) air-jet looms run at capacity from day one, and (4) migrant workers return. If any one fails, the interest on ₹5.55 crore at 14% consumes the family home. The case is deliberately designed so that no option is clearly dominant — the "right" answer depends on the student's risk tolerance, discount rate, and weighting of non-financial factors.

### 3. The Red Queen Effect (Assignment Question 3)

The Red Queen Effect (Barnett and Hansen, 1996) describes a competitive dynamic in which firms must continuously adapt merely to maintain their relative position. The case title directly invokes this metaphor. Students should identify the following mechanisms:

The self-reinforcing cycle of decline:

Technological stagnation (300 RPM vs. 1,200 RPM)
        ↓
Lower throughput per machine (100 m/day vs. 400 m/day)
        ↓
Higher per-unit fixed costs (fixed costs spread over fewer metres)
        ↓
Inability to offer competitive wages
        ↓
Migrant labour attrition to upgraded mills
        ↓
Further capacity reduction (50% utilization)
        ↓
Even higher per-unit costs → even lower margins
        ↓
Inability to invest in upgrades → back to start


Cluster-level dynamics: The case describes how Ichhalkaranji's historical strength — flexibility and dense local networks — has become a liability. The same network that once allowed a weaver to find a new customer within days now allows workers to find a better employer within days. The cluster's competitive advantage has shifted from flexibility to technology, and units that have not upgraded are being selected out.

The tariff shock as an accelerant, not a cause: Students should recognize that the US tariff (August 2024) did not create Sai Tex's problem — it accelerated a pre-existing decline. The technology gap existed before the tariff. The tariff's effect was to flood the domestic market with surplus fabric from export-oriented units, compressing job-work rates below break-even. This is a classic example of how external shocks disproportionately harm firms that are already competitively weakened.

Key teaching point: The Red Queen Effect explains why Option B (retrofit) is strategically insufficient even if it is financially safer. Retrofitting 15-year-old machines does not change Sai Tex's position on the technology curve — it merely slows the rate of decline. The competitors who have already upgraded will continue to attract workers, capture clients, and drive down rates. Option B is running faster on the same treadmill; Option A is switching to a faster treadmill.

### 4. Theory of Constraints Analysis (Assignment Question 4)

Goldratt's Theory of Constraints (TOC) asks: What is the one constraint that limits the system's throughput? Students should apply the five focusing steps:

Step 1: Identify the constraint.

Students will debate which of four candidates is the binding constraint:

| Candidate Constraint | Evidence For | Evidence Against |
|:---------------------|:-------------|:-----------------|
| Technology | 300 RPM vs. 1,200 RPM; 100 m/day vs. 400 m/day; 15-year-old machines | Even current machines are running at only 50% capacity — the technology is not fully utilized |
| Labour | 50% capacity utilization; workers leaving for upgraded mills; 10% wage premium at competitors | Labour is leaving because of the technology — it is a symptom, not the root cause |
| Market access | Job-work rate below break-even; no pricing power; export market collapsed 40% | Under the sale-purchase model, market access changes entirely — this constraint is model-dependent |
| Capital | ₹1.2 cr existing debt; 14% interest rate; bank requires bungalow as collateral; no formal financial records | MAHA-TUFS subsidy could offset 25%; capital is available if collateral is pledged |

Step 2: Decide how to exploit the constraint.

If technology is the binding constraint: maximize throughput on existing machines (run all 10 looms, not 5). But this requires labour — which is leaving because of the technology. The constraint is interactive.

If capital is the binding constraint: the MAHA-TUFS subsidy, the solar plant (eliminating the electricity bill), and the sale-purchase model (capturing full margin) are all mechanisms to relax the capital constraint.

Step 3: Subordinate everything else to the constraint.

This is where the analysis becomes most productive. If students identify technology as the root constraint (with labour as a derived constraint), then all other decisions — business model, energy, workforce — should be subordinated to resolving the technology gap. This points toward Option A.

If students identify capital as the root constraint, then the priority is to find the lowest-capital path to viability — which points toward Option B as a bridge, or toward creative financing (phased upgrade, leasing, cooperative investment) that the case does not explicitly offer but that students may propose.

Key teaching point: The TOC analysis reveals that Sai Tex's constraints are nested and interactive. Technology drives labour attrition, which drives capacity underutilization, which drives per-unit cost inflation, which drives the inability to accumulate capital for upgrades. Breaking the cycle requires addressing the root constraint (technology), not the symptoms (labour, costs). This is why Option B — which addresses symptoms — is strategically insufficient.

### 5. Socio-Emotional Wealth and Non-Financial Decision Criteria (Assignment Question 5)

The SEW framework (Gómez-Mejía et al., 2007; Berrone et al., 2012) identifies five dimensions of non-financial value that family firm owners seek to preserve:

| SEW Dimension | Manifestation in Sai Tex |
|:--------------|:-------------------------|
| Family control and influence | Patil is sole proprietor; all decisions are his. Option A requires pledging the family bungalow — transferring control to the bank. |
| Family members' identification with the firm | "The mill was not merely a business but an extension of Patil's identity and social obligations." |
| Binding social ties | 12 permanent employees; Shivmani Mishra has been with the firm since founding; Patil supports local schools. |
| Emotional attachment | Father's legacy: "If you have the means to give someone a livelihood, you have the duty to do it." |
| Renewal of family bonds through dynastic succession | Not explicitly addressed — Patil is first-generation. But the bungalow represents intergenerational family wealth. |

How SEW changes the calculus:

A purely financial analysis might favour Option C (exit) — it stops the bleeding and preserves remaining family assets. But SEW theory predicts that family firm owners will accept greater financial risk to preserve non-financial value. Patil's identification with the firm, his binding social ties to workers, and his father's legacy of social obligation all create a strong SEW-preservation motive that makes Option C psychologically and socially unacceptable — even if it is financially rational.

This creates a tension: SEW preservation pushes Patil toward Option A (the only option that preserves the firm and its social role), but Option A also poses the greatest threat to SEW if it fails (loss of the family bungalow, the ultimate symbol of family wealth and stability).

Expected student debate:

- Some students will argue that SEW considerations are a form of cognitive bias — Patil is letting emotional attachment cloud his judgment. The "duty to provide livelihoods" is admirable but not a sound basis for risking the family home.
- Others will argue that SEW is not irrational — it reflects a different utility function. Patil's social capital, community standing, and personal identity are real assets that have economic value (e.g., worker loyalty, supplier trust, cooperative membership). Destroying them through exit has costs that a financial model does not capture.
- The most sophisticated students will argue that the relevant question is not "should SEW matter?" but "how much risk is SEW worth?" — and that the answer depends on the probability-weighted outcomes of Option A.

Key teaching point: The SEW framework explains why Patil's decision is genuinely difficult — not because he lacks information, but because his financial and non-financial objectives are in tension. This is the central pedagogical insight of the case: real-world strategic decisions in MSMEs and family firms are never purely financial.

## WHAT HAPPENED

As of April 2026, Patil had not yet made a final decision. He was in active discussions with his bank regarding the MAHA-TUFS subsidy application for the full modernization (Option A). The bank had indicated willingness to finance the upgrade but required the family bungalow as additional collateral. Patil's response to the bank manager's question — "Are we processing the MAHA-TUFS subsidy application for the full modernization, or are we just renewing the existing cash credit limit?" — was: "We are applying for MAHA-TUFS subsidy. Without subsidy there will be huge financial burden."

This response suggests that Patil was leaning toward Option A but recognized that the subsidy was a precondition for its viability. The case was written during this decision window — the outcome remained uncertain at the time of publication.

Several contextual developments were relevant:

- Two competitors in the Pride Co-operative Textile Park — M.F. Rathi Textiles and P.A. Patil Textiles — had already completed air-jet upgrades and were reportedly capturing Sai Tex's former clients.
- The domestic fabric market remained oversupplied due to diverted exports, with no recovery in job-work rates visible as of early 2026.
- The China+1 sourcing strategy that some industry observers had predicted would benefit Indian textile MSMEs had, as of early 2026, produced no discernible positive impact on units like Sai Tex.
- Patil had not been aware of the MAHA-TUFS subsidy until the case researcher informed him — highlighting the information asymmetry that small-unit owners face in accessing government support programs.

## A. CASE SYNOPSIS

In April 2026, Avinash Kisanrao Patil (AKP), founder and sole proprietor of Sai Tex Ltd. — a small powerloom unit in the Pride Co-operative Textile Park in Ichhalkaranji, Maharashtra, India's "Manchester of Maharashtra" — faced an existential decision. His ten Rifa Electronic Rapier looms, purchased in 2010 for ₹1.2 crore (US$144,600), were running at 50 per cent capacity. Five of the ten machines stood idle. A United States tariff shock in August 2024 had collapsed job-work rates from 25 to 17–18 paisa per pick, migrant workers were leaving for competitors with newer air-jet looms offering 10 per cent higher wages, and energy costs had doubled from ₹45,000 to ₹90,000 per month. The unit was losing ₹24,000 every month — a ₹492,000 deterioration from the ₹468,000 monthly profit it had earned just two years earlier (case Exhibit 1).

Patil had three options. Option A: a ₹4.35 crore "green technical pivot" — replacing all ten Rifa machines with twelve high-speed air-jet looms, installing rooftop solar, and pivoting from the job-work model to sale-purchase — which could transform the unit but required pledging the family bungalow as collateral and would push total debt to ₹5.55 crore. Option B: a ₹40–50 lakh "hybrid lean" retrofit of existing machines with inverter motors, buying two to three years of runway without solving the fundamental competitive gap. Option C: asset liquidation and exit — selling the machines for ₹20 lakh, stopping the bleeding, but displacing the twelve permanent employees and their families who depended on Sai Tex for their livelihoods.

The case presents a classic MSME survival dilemma in which financial analysis alone is insufficient. Students must weigh competitive dynamics (the Red Queen Effect), family stakes (socio-emotional wealth), operational constraints (Theory of Constraints), and the cascading impact of macro-level trade policy on a micro-level firm to arrive at a recommendation. The protagonist's father's words — "If you have the means to give someone a livelihood, you have the duty to do it" — anchor the tension between financial rationality and social obligation that runs through every option.

A downloadable spreadsheet containing the case Exhibit 1–7 data is available for instructors who wish to have students build their own financial models during the pre-class assignment or in-class group exercise.

## B. LEARNING OBJECTIVES

This case is set in the Indian textile MSME sector, a market undergoing rapid technological disruption while thousands of family-owned firms struggle to adapt. It presents students with the task of evaluating strategic alternatives for a resource-constrained firm facing simultaneous competitive, financial, and social pressures.

After reading and analysing this case, students will be able to do the following:

1. Analyse how macro-level disruptions (trade policy, energy inflation, labour mobility) cascade into a micro-level MSME crisis, using PESTEL and Porter's Five Forces frameworks. (Enabled by: case sections "The Perfect Storm" and "The Manchester of Maharashtra"; Exhibits 1, 2, 3)

2. Evaluate three strategic alternatives (full modernization, hybrid retrofit, and exit) by conducting break-even analysis, debt-service calculations, and scenario modelling under resource constraints. (Enabled by: case section "Three Paths Forward"; Exhibits 1, 2, 4, 5, 7)

3. Assess the role of non-financial factors — specifically socio-emotional wealth (SEW), founder identity, and worker welfare — in shaping strategic decisions that deviate from purely financial optimization. (Enabled by: case sections "Sai Tex: A Decade of Weaving" and "The Decision"; Exhibit 6)

4. Apply the Theory of Constraints to identify the binding constraint in a manufacturing MSME and evaluate whether each strategic option addresses or ignores it. (Enabled by: case Exhibits 3, 5, 7; the 50% capacity utilization data)

5. Compare the job-work and sale-purchase business models in terms of risk, margin, capital requirements, and strategic positioning within an industrial cluster. (Enabled by: case section "The Sale-Purchase Question"; Exhibits 2, 4)

6. Design a phased implementation plan that sequences investments to manage risk, rather than treating modernization as a single binary decision. (Enabled by: synthesis across all case exhibits and the family's stated constraint — "without adding more stress and debt")

These objectives guide students through a learning path progressing from environmental analysis (LO1) to financial evaluation (LO2), then to stakeholder integration (LO3), operational diagnosis (LO4), business model comparison (LO5), and finally to actionable strategic design (LO6).

## E. ASSIGNMENT QUESTIONS

The following questions should be distributed to students before class. They are designed to guide pre-class preparation and structure the in-class discussion. Each question maps to one or more learning objectives and progresses from descriptive understanding to analytical application to evaluative recommendation.

1. Situational Understanding (LO1)
Describe the causal chain from the August 2024 US tariff shock to Sai Tex's monthly loss of ₹24,000 (case Exhibit 1). How many links are in the chain? At which link, if any, could Patil have intervened? Reference specific data from the case to support each link.

2. Break-Even Analysis (LO2, LO4)
Using the data in case Exhibits 1, 2, 3, and 7, calculate Sai Tex's monthly break-even volume under the current job-work model. Is break-even achievable given the constraints described in the case? What does this tell you about the viability of the status quo?

3. Environmental Analysis (LO1)
Conduct a PESTEL analysis of the Ichhalkaranji textile cluster as of April 2026. Which factor represents the most significant threat to Sai Tex, and which represents the most significant opportunity? How do the factors interact?

4. Option Evaluation (LO2, LO3, LO5)
Evaluate the three strategic options available to Patil (case Exhibit 4). For each option, assess: (a) financial viability using case exhibit data, (b) risk to the family, (c) impact on the twelve permanent employees and their families (case Exhibit 6), and (d) strategic fit with Patil's capabilities and constraints. Which option would you recommend, and why?

5. Business Model Comparison (LO5)
Compare the job-work model and the sale-purchase model on the dimensions of risk, margin, capital requirements, and strategic positioning. Given that Patil has prior experience with the sale-purchase model but has not practised it in fifteen years, what are the key execution risks of the pivot?

6. Socio-Emotional Wealth (LO3)
Patil's father instilled in him the belief that "if you have the means to give someone a livelihood, you have the duty to do it." How does this socio-emotional wealth (SEW) influence Patil's decision-making? Using the five SEW dimensions (Berrone, Cruz, & Gómez-Mejía, 2012), map specific evidence from the case to each dimension. Does SEW help or hinder Patil's strategic choices?

7. Implementation Design (LO6)
Patil's family wants him to "turn this into a profitable business without adding more stress and debt." Is this possible? Design a phased implementation plan that addresses the family's concerns while moving toward long-term viability. Specify decision gates at each phase.

### Facilitation Techniques

- **Cold-Call Opening**: Begin with a student who chose Option C (Exit) in the opening poll — this is typically the least popular choice and forces the class to confront the SEW dimension immediately. Ask: "You chose to shut down. Walk me through your reasoning. What happens to Shivmani Mishra?"

- **Role-Play Scenario** (use during the 35–50 min debate): Select one student and say: "You are Patil's bank manager. He has just walked into your office asking for ₹3.6 crore. His only financial record is a ruled notebook. His machines are worth ₹20 lakh on the resale market. His bungalow is worth [let the student estimate]. Would you approve the loan? Under what conditions?"

- **Small Group Breakout** (20–35 min): Groups of 4–5 students. Assign each group a specific option and a specific stakeholder lens: Group A argues for Option A from Patil's perspective; Group B argues for Option B from the family's perspective; Group C argues for Option C from Shivmani Mishra's perspective. This forces students to inhabit different value systems.

- **Debate Format** (35–50 min): After presentations, allow 2 minutes of cross-examination per group. The instructor moderates but does not resolve — the tension is the point.

### Optional Extension: Stakeholder Role-Play (additional 15 minutes, for 90-minute sessions)

Assign five roles: Patil, his wife, Shivmani Mishra (manager), the bank manager, and a representative of M.F. Rathi Textiles (the competitor who upgraded). Each role-player argues from their stakeholder's perspective. Debrief by asking: "Whose interests should Patil prioritize, and why? Is there a solution that satisfies all five stakeholders?"

#### The Job-Work Model: Why It Is Failing

The job-work model's structural weakness is that the weaver bears all production costs — labour, energy, maintenance — but has no pricing power. The merchant sets the per-pick rate, and the weaver accepts it or loses the order. When the US tariff diverted export surplus into the domestic market, the resulting oversupply gave merchants even more leverage. Rates fell from 25 paisa/pick (₹15/metre) in 2023 to 17–18 paisa/pick (₹6.40–₹9.00/metre) in 2025 (case Exhibits 2, 3). The weaver's only negotiating leverage — the quality and speed of his machines — had eroded as competitors upgraded.

#### The US Tariff Bullwhip Effect

The August 2024 US tariff created a classic bullwhip effect: a demand shock at the export end amplified into a price collapse at the production end. Export volumes from Ichhalkaranji fell by approximately 40%. Large export-oriented units diverted surplus fabric into the domestic market, competing directly with small job-work units for the same customers — but with superior scale and established buyer relationships. The China+1 sourcing strategy that some observers had predicted would benefit Indian textile MSMEs had, as of early 2026, produced no discernible positive impact on units like Sai Tex.
