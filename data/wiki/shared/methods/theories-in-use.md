## Overview

The Theories-in-Use (TIU) approach, formalised by Zeithaml, Jaworski, Kohli, Tuli, Ulaga, and Zaltman (2020), builds "organic" or "home-grown" theory by eliciting practitioners' own if-then beliefs about actions and outcomes and abstracting across them into constructs, propositions, and arguments (Zeithaml et al. 2020, journal pp. 32-34). The term comes from Argyris and Schon (1974), cited on p. 34, who distinguished a person's operative theory-in-use from the espoused theory they claim to hold. It is a method for theory construction, not theory testing (p. 45).

TIU sits alongside case-based theory building rather than replacing it. Where the Eisenhardt protocol treats each organisation as an experiment and reasons across cases (see `eisenhardt-theory-building-protocol.md`), TIU works one knowledgeable informant at a time and abstracts upward from individual mental models. Both must meet the Sutton-Staw standard: the output must explain *why* a relationship holds, not merely assert it (Sutton and Staw 1995, p. 377).

## Steps/Protocol

The process is an iterative loop (Figure 1, p. 37), not a pipeline. Failing a test at any stage returns the researcher to the data or to new conversations.

### Step 1: Define a tentative construct

Introduce the topic and ask what the phenomenon means to the participant; gather examples at varying levels; compare against proximal constructs (p. 38). If the construct is not yet well defined, return to further conversations before proceeding (p. 37).

### Step 2: Select knowledgeable participants in phases

Use 15-25 participants, chosen in successive phases for their knowledgeability and willingness to share, not for representativeness (pp. 34, 36). "Mosaic filling" tracks under-developed components of the emerging theory and selects later participants to fill them (p. 42). Record each conversation, take notes, and write a memo immediately after (p. 38).

### Step 3: Develop propositions

Turn constructs into if-then propositions identifying antecedents, consequences, mediators, and moderators. Put earlier participants' propositions to later participants for reaction (pp. 38-39).

### Step 4: Develop arguments (the "why")

Press participants on the reasons they hold their if-then beliefs, and critically evaluate the soundness of those reasons before accepting them (pp. 39, 41). This step is what turns a pattern into theory under the Sutton-Staw standard.

### Step 5: Code and abstract

Apply grounded-theory coding vocabulary (Corbin and Strauss 2008, cited pp. 39-41): open coding to identify and categorise elements, axial coding to link concepts into antecedents/consequences/moderators, selective coding to unify around a core category. Continue to theoretical saturation, the point where further data yield no new themes (p. 42).

### Step 6: Apply the two gates and pull it together

Test each proposition against the **foundational test** (plausible, aligned with definitions and arguments) and the **advanced test** (interesting, substantively informative, and unified by one or a few common themes) (p. 37). Select the propositions that share those themes for a parsimonious contribution (p. 41).

### Step 7: Demonstrate rigor against five criteria (Table 6, p. 44)

Four from Lincoln and Guba (1985), cited p. 42, plus a fifth added by the authors:

- **Credibility** (≈ internal validity): supporting arguments, range-spanning questions, agreement across participants (p. 43).
- **Transferability** (≈ external validity): similar theory emerging across multiple contexts (p. 43).
- **Dependability** (≈ reliability): multiple researchers converge on the same theory from the same data (p. 44).
- **Confirmability** (≈ objectivity): participant checks and interjudge reliability (p. 44).
- **Distinctiveness** (≈ discriminant validity): constructs and propositions are genuinely new relative to the literature (pp. 44-45).

The authors weight the set toward credibility, transferability, and distinctiveness (p. 45).

## When to Use

Appropriate when (pp. 34-36):
- The phenomenon is new or emerging and validated constructs do not yet exist.
- Existing constructs or the nomological net are ambiguous and need precision.
- The aim is to construct theory that a later quantitative phase will test.

Less useful "when working with well-defined constructs where the nomological net has been mapped out comprehensively" (p. 35). It is not a testing method (p. 45).

## Application to Research

For a qualitative, theory-building Phase 1 on a new phenomenon (for example, organisational change during the GenAI transition in the DBA thesis):

- **Justification**: the phenomenon is emerging and lacks validated measures, which is the condition TIU is built for (p. 35). This parallels the inductive justification used in the Eisenhardt protocol.
- **Elicitation**: interview knowledgeable informants (executives, managers) about their if-then beliefs, then abstract shared constructs across them, rather than mapping a whole organisation.
- **Bridge to Phase 2**: the if-then propositions and their construct definitions feed a quantitative test. See `mixed-methods-sequential-design.md` and `construct-operationalization.md`.
- **Defending the method chapter**: the five rigor criteria (Table 6) provide the standard to cite, and distinctiveness doubles as a prompt for the contribution statement.
- **Worked cautionary example**: the applied assessment of TIU in Kohli and Jaworski (1990) is in `spaces/CRO/output/Session7_TheoriesInUse_Review.md`, which shows what happens when the coding and dependability/confirmability steps are left undocumented.

## Limitations

The authors' own (p. 45): TIU is not for theory testing; it needs interviewing skill researchers often have to acquire; and it works only when informants hold developed theories, so on genuinely new phenomena participants may offer a "guess" rather than an "aha." The 15-25 sample range is loose, and the authors say "more systematic clarity is needed" (p. 48). The advanced test relies on the judgment words "interesting" and "substantively informative" (p. 37). A deeper tension: TIU is partly tacit by definition (p. 34), so eliciting it risks surfacing the espoused theory instead.
