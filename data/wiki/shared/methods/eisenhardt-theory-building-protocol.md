## Overview

A structured approach to building theory from case-based empirical evidence, developed by Kathleen Eisenhardt (1989b) and elaborated in Eisenhardt and Graebner (2007). The method produces theory that is "accurate, interesting, and testable" because it is "deeply embedded in rich empirical data" (Eisenhardt and Graebner 2007, p. 26). It works through "recursive cycling among the case data, emerging theory, and later, extant literature" (p. 25).

The resulting theory must meet the Sutton-Staw standard: it must explain *why* phenomena occur, not merely list variables, present data, draw diagrams, or state hypotheses. "Theory is about the connections among phenomena, a story about why acts, events, structure, and thoughts occur" (Sutton and Staw 1995, p. 378).

[Source: CRO/wiki/sources/eisenhardt-graebner-2007-theory-building-opportunities.md, §Summary; CRO/wiki/sources/sutton-staw-1995-what-theory-is-not.md, §Key Arguments]

## Steps/Protocol

### Step 1: Justify the Inductive Approach

Demonstrate that existing research "does not address the research question at all, or does so in a way that is inadequate or likely to be untrue" (Eisenhardt and Graebner, p. 26). Two justification routes exist:

- **Theory-driven**: frame within existing theory, show how qualitative data offer "insight into complex social processes that quantitative data cannot easily reveal" (p. 27).
- **Phenomenon-driven**: frame in terms of the phenomenon's importance and the absence of plausible theory.

Note that conflicting theories alone are not sufficient justification. The researcher must show that existing work is genuinely inadequate for the question at hand.

[Source: CRO/wiki/sources/eisenhardt-graebner-2007-theory-building-opportunities.md, §Key Arguments, Justifying Theory Building]

### Step 2: Select Cases via Theoretical Sampling

Cases are "selected because they are particularly suitable for illuminating and extending relationships and logic among constructs" (p. 27). This is not random sampling.

Selection reasons include: revelation of an unusual phenomenon, replication of findings, contrary replication, elimination of alternative explanations, and elaboration of emerging theory (p. 27).

**Polar types** are the recommended approach for construct clarity: "a researcher samples extreme (e.g., very high and very low performing) cases in order to more easily observe contrasting patterns in the data" (p. 28).

Adding cases has outsize impact: "adding three cases to a single-case study is modest in terms of numbers, but offers four times the analytic power" (p. 28).

[Source: CRO/wiki/sources/eisenhardt-graebner-2007-theory-building-opportunities.md, §Key Arguments, Theoretical Sampling]

### Step 3: Craft Instruments and Mitigate Bias

Use "numerous and diverse informants" from "different hierarchical levels, functional areas, groups, and geographies" (p. 28). Convergent retrospective sensemaking is unlikely when informants are this varied.

Combine retrospective and real-time cases where possible: retrospective cases build breadth; real-time cases "mitigate retrospective sensemaking and impression management" (p. 28).

[Source: CRO/wiki/sources/eisenhardt-graebner-2007-theory-building-opportunities.md, §Key Arguments, Managing Interview Data Bias]

### Step 4: Enter the Field and Analyze Within-Case

Each case stands as "a distinct experiment that stands on its own as an analytic unit" (p. 25). Analyze each case independently before cross-case comparison. This replication logic means "multiple cases are discrete experiments that serve as replications, contrasts, and extensions to the emerging theory" (p. 25).

[Source: CRO/wiki/sources/eisenhardt-graebner-2007-theory-building-opportunities.md, §Key Arguments, Definition and Core Logic]

### Step 5: Search for Cross-Case Patterns

Retain "only the relationships that are replicated across most or all of the cases" (p. 30). This produces parsimony: "parsimony, robustness, and generalizability characterize superior theory" (p. 30).

[Source: CRO/wiki/sources/eisenhardt-graebner-2007-theory-building-opportunities.md, §Key Arguments, Writing Emergent Theory]

### Step 6: Shape Propositions and Build Construct Tables

Propositions must pass the Sutton-Staw test: they must explain *why* a relationship exists, not merely predict *that* it exists. "Hypotheses are concise statements about what is expected to occur, not why it is expected to occur" (Sutton and Staw, p. 377).

Use **construct tables** that "summarize the case evidence and indicate how the focal construct is 'measured,' thus increasing the 'testability' of the theory" (Eisenhardt and Graebner, p. 29). These tables bridge qualitative evidence to quantitative operationalisation.

Every proposition must include:
- The logical mechanism (why the relationship holds)
- Supporting empirical evidence from the cases
- Boundary conditions (when it most and least likely holds)

One sign of strong theory: "it is possible to discern conditions in which the major proposition or hypothesis is most and least likely to hold" (Sutton and Staw, p. 377).

[Source: CRO/wiki/sources/eisenhardt-graebner-2007-theory-building-opportunities.md, §Key Arguments, Presenting Empirical Evidence; CRO/wiki/sources/sutton-staw-1995-what-theory-is-not.md, §Key Arguments]

### Step 7: Write Emergent Theory

Write theory multiple ways in a single paper (p. 30):
1. Sketch emergent theory in the introduction
2. Write each proposition with supporting empirical evidence
3. Provide the underlying theoretical arguments (the logical link between constructs)
4. Include a visual theory summary

Avoid the five substitutes Sutton and Staw identify: references without explication, data without causal reasoning, variable lists without mechanism, diagrams without explanation, and predictions without logic. "The data do not generate theory; only researchers do that" (Sutton and Staw, p. 374, citing Mintzberg 1979).

[Source: CRO/wiki/sources/eisenhardt-graebner-2007-theory-building-opportunities.md, §Key Arguments, Writing Emergent Theory; CRO/wiki/sources/sutton-staw-1995-what-theory-is-not.md, §Key Arguments, Five Things That Are Not Theory]

## When to Use

Theory-building from cases is appropriate when:
- The research question asks "how" or "why" (not "how often" or "how many")
- Existing theory is absent, inadequate, or untrue for the phenomenon
- The phenomenon is new, poorly understood, or insufficiently operationalised
- Constructs need to be built before they can be tested

It is "ill-equipped to address the questions 'how often,' and 'how many'" (Eisenhardt and Graebner, p. 27). Those questions belong to Phase 2 quantitative work.

[Source: CRO/wiki/sources/eisenhardt-graebner-2007-theory-building-opportunities.md, §Key Arguments, Justifying Theory Building]

## Application to Research

For the Phase 1 qualitative work on agentic AI governance:

**Justification** (Step 1): No existing theory specifically addresses governance of goal-directed AI agents and its relationship to enterprise risk outcomes. Kolt (2025) provides normative governance principles without operational definitions or measurement frameworks. Vu et al. (2025) surface practitioner anticipations but no participant had actual agentic AI experience. The research question meets the "inadequate or likely untrue" threshold.

**Theoretical sampling** (Step 2): Select firms using polar types. Mature governance: firms with documented agent oversight policies, established audit trails, defined human-agent authority boundaries. Immature governance: firms deploying agentic AI with ad hoc oversight, no formal governance protocols, autonomous decision-making without monitoring. The contrast maximises construct clarity for "governance maturity."

**Diverse informants** (Step 3): Interview CIOs/CTOs (technical perspective), Chief Risk Officers (risk perspective), compliance officers (regulatory perspective), and line managers who interact with AI agents (operational perspective). This cross-functional spread prevents convergent sensemaking.

**Construct tables** (Step 6): The key deliverable from Phase 1. Each row represents a governance practice dimension (structural, relational, procedural). Columns show: construct name, definition, case evidence (with firm identifiers), and proposed operationalisation for Phase 2 survey items. This table is the bridge to formative measurement development.

**Sutton-Staw check**: Every proposition from Phase 1 must explain the causal mechanism through which governance maturity reduces enterprise risk. The mechanism (not just the correlation) is what makes it theory.

## Limitations

- Brief commentary format (8 pages) in Eisenhardt and Graebner limits depth; assumes familiarity with Eisenhardt (1989b).
- Does not address mixed-methods designs where case research feeds quantitative testing (the sequential approach used here).
- The Sutton-Staw criteria define what theory is *not* without fully specifying what makes theory *strong* beyond the negative (acknowledged at p. 372).
- Neither paper addresses formative construct development, which requires additional methodological guidance from Diamantopoulos (2011) and MacKenzie et al. (2011).

[Source: CRO/wiki/sources/eisenhardt-graebner-2007-theory-building-opportunities.md, §Limitations; CRO/wiki/sources/sutton-staw-1995-what-theory-is-not.md, §Limitations]
