# Literature Review: 7-Paper Analysis for "Agentic AI Governance and Its Impact on Enterprise Risk and Business Performance"

---

## Paper 1: Berente, Gu, Recker, and Santhanam (2021)

### 1. Full Citation
Berente, N., Gu, B., Recker, J., and Santhanam, R. (2021). "Managing Artificial Intelligence." *MIS Quarterly*, 45(3), pp. 1433-1450.

### 2. Core Argument
The paper defines AI as "the frontier of computational advancements that references human intelligence in addressing ever more complex decision-making problems" (p. 1434). It argues that managing AI requires attending to three interrelated facets: autonomy, learning, and inscrutability (p. 1437). The authors contend that AI is not a discrete technology but a moving frontier, and that managing it is a continuous process of emergence requiring sociotechnical thinking (p. 1438).

### 3. Methodology
This is a special issue editorial/introduction for MIS Quarterly. The method is conceptual synthesis: the authors consolidate insights from seven papers accepted into the special issue, combined with their own literature review and conceptual framing. No empirical data collection was conducted by the authors themselves.

### 4. Key Findings
- AI is defined through three facets: autonomy (acting without human intervention), learning (improving through data and experience), and inscrutability (being unintelligible to specific audiences) (p. 1437, Table 1).
- Managing autonomy involves navigating the tension between automation and augmentation; as AI becomes more autonomous, humans risk losing their own autonomy and effectiveness (pp. 1439-1440).
- Managing learning involves dealing with AI that feeds on data beyond organizational boundaries, raising privacy, trust, and governance issues (p. 1440).
- Managing inscrutability involves four dimensions: opacity, transparency, explainability, and interpretability (pp. 1441-1442).
- Ethical issues (fairness, bias, privacy, accountability) cut across all three facets (pp. 1443-1444).
- Boards must contain members who can scrutinize AI technologies to assess strategic impact (p. 1442, referencing Li et al. 2021).

### 5. Direct Relevance to Term Paper
- **RQ1 (governance practices):** The paper's framework of autonomy, learning, and inscrutability provides a conceptual vocabulary for categorizing governance practices. The discussion of managing inscrutability through opacity, transparency, explainability, and interpretability (pp. 1441-1442) maps directly onto governance mechanisms the term paper seeks to identify.
- **P1 (governance maturity reduces incidents):** The paper argues that managers who understand and address the three facets of AI can avoid negative consequences (p. 1434), supporting the logic that governance maturity reduces risk.
- **P2 (workflow control rigidity):** The automation-augmentation tension (pp. 1439-1440) speaks to the concept of workflow control rigidity: too much automation without human oversight creates risk, but so does too little.
- **Theoretical stream:** The paper's framing of AI as requiring sociotechnical management aligns with the IT governance-to-business-value lineage (Weill and Ross 2004). The discussion of delegation to autonomous agents (p. 1439, referencing Baird and Maruping 2021) connects to principal-agent theory.

### 6. What It Does NOT Do
- Does not provide empirical evidence on governance practices or their outcomes.
- Does not measure risk outcomes (incident frequency, financial loss, compliance failure).
- Does not address agentic AI specifically (focuses on AI broadly).
- Does not test propositions about governance maturity, control rigidity, or shadow AI.
- Does not use quantitative methods or propose measurable constructs.

### 7. Validity for Literature Survey
**Yes.** Published in MIS Quarterly (A* journal, ABDC). Provides the foundational conceptual framework for understanding AI management challenges. Directly relevant as a theoretical anchor for RQ1 and the governance dimensions the term paper investigates.

## Paper 2: Jarrahi and Ritala (2025)

### 1. Full Citation
Jarrahi, M. H. and Ritala, P. (2025). "Rethinking AI Agents: A Principal-Agent Perspective." *California Management Review* (exact volume/pages not found in document; published 2025 based on file metadata and references citing 2025 works).

### 2. Core Argument
The paper argues that viewing AI agents through the lens of principal-agent relationships offers a more actionable perspective for business and organizational contexts than treating them as fully autonomous systems (p. 3). AI agents should be understood as "systems designed to fulfill specific objectives while operating within human-defined constraints" (p. 3), and organizations need governance regimes that go beyond technical capabilities to integrate agents into organizational processes (p. 10).

### 3. Methodology
This is a conceptual/practitioner-oriented article. The method is conceptual argumentation supported by illustrative examples from industry (e.g., Moderna deploying 3,000 tailored GPTs, p. 9; McKinsey's central review team, p. 10). No empirical data collection is reported.

### 4. Key Findings
- AI agents differ from past AI by bridging knowledge and action, proactively orchestrating complex workflows (p. 2).
- Five guiding principles for AI agent deployment: (1) adaptability and environmental sensing (pp. 4-5), (2) guided autonomy with defined boundaries of delegation (p. 5), (3) multi-agent coordination resembling multi-disciplinary teams (pp. 5-6), (4) combining generative AI with rule-based control mechanisms for safety and predictability (p. 6), and (5) alignment/individualization to stakeholder goals (pp. 6-7).
- Four principal-agent challenges are identified with mitigation strategies (Table 1, pp. 7-9): goal misalignment, information asymmetry, moral crumple zones in responsibility allocation, and multi-agent complexity.
- Organization-wide AI governance strategy is needed to build guardrails for agents (p. 10).
- McKinsey example: a central team reviews all developed agents based on risk, legal, and data policies before rollout (p. 10).

### 5. Direct Relevance to Term Paper
- **Theoretical lens (principal-agent theory):** This paper is a direct application of principal-agent theory to AI agents, making it a core theoretical reference. The framing of AI agents as "agents" acting on behalf of human "principals" (p. 3) maps precisely onto Kolt (2025).
- **RQ1 (governance practices):** The five guiding principles (pp. 4-7) and four risk mitigation strategies (Table 1, pp. 7-9) provide concrete governance practices for agentic AI deployments.
- **P2 (workflow control rigidity):** The concept of "guided autonomy" with "defined boundaries of delegation" that "can grow as agents expand their learning" (p. 5) directly operationalizes the workflow control rigidity construct.
- **P3 (shadow AI):** The paper warns about multi-agent complexity where "too many agents with too many tasks can result in overly complex and difficult-to-understand systems" (p. 9), which relates to uncontrolled proliferation.
- **P4 (inverted U-shape):** The tension between autonomy and control is explicit: full autonomy is "not always feasible or desirable" (p. 5), suggesting an optimal middle ground.

### 6. What It Does NOT Do
- Does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative data.
- Does not measure governance outcomes (incident frequency, financial loss).
- Does not test the principal-agent framework with firm-level data.
- Does not address shadow AI directly as a moderating variable.
- Does not propose or test an inverted U-shape relationship.

### 7. Validity for Literature Survey
**Yes.** Published in California Management Review (A journal, ABDC). This is arguably the single most directly relevant paper to the term paper's theoretical framework, as it applies principal-agent theory to agentic AI governance. Essential for the theoretical foundations section.

## Paper 3: Holgersson, Dahlander, Chesbrough, and Bogers (2024)

### 1. Full Citation
Holgersson, M., Dahlander, L., Chesbrough, H. W., and Bogers, M. L. A. M. (2024). "Open Innovation in the Age of AI." *California Management Review*, 67(1), pp. 5-20.

### 2. Core Argument
The paper proposes a framework for how AI changes open innovation (OI) through three mechanisms: enhancing established OI practices, enabling new forms of OI, and replacing some traditional OI practices (p. 5, Table 1). AI is reshaping how organizations search for ideas, evaluate them, find partners, and coordinate innovation, with multi-agent systems offering novel approaches to distributed problem-solving (pp. 10-11).

### 3. Methodology
Conceptual framework paper with illustrative examples. No empirical data collection. The authors draw on OI literature and recent AI developments to construct a typology (Table 1, p. 9).

### 4. Key Findings
- AI enhances OI through: innovation search (NLP, sentiment analysis), partner search, idea evaluation (AI better at screening bad ideas than selecting best ones, p. 10), and resource utilization (pp. 9-11).
- AI enables new OI forms: AI-enabled markets, new business models, federated learning for privacy-preserving collaboration (pp. 11-12).
- AI replaces OI: AI ideation (AI generates ideas rated more creative than those by laypeople, p. 13), synthetic data replacing need for data sharing, multi-agent systems replacing coordination-heavy OI (pp. 13-15).
- Multi-agent systems can "operate autonomously and interact on the basis of predefined rules and protocols" to address OI coordination challenges (p. 10).
- Ethical and IP questions loom large (pp. 15-16).

### 5. Direct Relevance to Term Paper
- **Tangential relevance only.** The paper's focus is on open innovation, not governance or risk management.
- **RQ1:** The multi-agent systems discussion (pp. 10-11) touches on coordination protocols and predefined rules, which are governance-adjacent but framed as innovation mechanisms, not risk controls.
- **P3 (shadow AI):** The paper notes that "the barrier to implementing [multi-agent systems] through multiple autonomous chatbots, is now lower than ever" (p. 10), which indirectly supports the shadow AI concern.
- The paper does not address enterprise risk, governance maturity, incident frequency, or compliance.

### 6. What It Does NOT Do
- Does not address AI governance, risk management, or compliance.
- Does not discuss enterprise risk outcomes.
- Does not engage with principal-agent theory or IT governance frameworks.
- Does not provide empirical data on governance practices or their effects.
- Does not address agentic AI governance specifically.

### 7. Validity for Literature Survey
**No.** While published in California Management Review (A journal, ABDC) and academically rigorous, the paper's focus on open innovation is too distant from the term paper's scope. It could be cited in passing when discussing multi-agent coordination challenges, but it does not belong in the core literature review for a paper on agentic AI governance and enterprise risk.

## Paper 4: Recker, von Briel, Yoo, Nagaraj, and McManus (2023)

### 1. Full Citation
Recker, J., von Briel, F., Yoo, Y., Nagaraj, V., and McManus, M. (2023). "Orchestrating Human-Machine Designer Ensembles During Product Innovation." *California Management Review*, 65(3), pp. 27-47.

### 2. Core Argument
The paper argues that firms must learn to orchestrate ensembles of human and machine designers during product innovation, balancing the strengths of each (p. 29). Machine designers are differentiated along two dimensions: core innovation capability (search/discovery vs. generation) and process sovereignty (supervised vs. unsupervised), yielding four types (Figure 1, p. 30). Successful orchestration requires decisions about ensemble configuration, sequencing, problem domain, and innovation goal (Table 1, p. 36).

### 3. Methodology
Qualitative case research. The authors worked directly with companies including Hackrod, Moderna, Ubisoft, and Starbucks. They conducted semi-structured and unstructured interviews with lead personnel involved in innovation and product development, supplemented by publicly available sources (p. 44). Sample size and exact interview count not specified.

### 4. Key Findings
- Four types of machine designers identified: parameterized construction (Hackrod), solution pattern discovery (Moderna), automated use case identification (Starbucks), product-based learning (Ubisoft) (pp. 31-33).
- Orchestration involves four aspects: ensemble configuration, sequencing, problem domain, and innovation goal (Table 1, p. 36).
- Firms must manage ethical and social implications: sovereignty decisions carry moral implications, human designers must stay in the loop for accountability and legitimacy (pp. 41-42).
- Data privacy tensions: between data privacy and portability, between consent models and data use, between user participation and algorithmic bias (pp. 42-43).
- Governance mechanisms appropriate for machine designers need development (p. 40): "firms must rethink how they govern innovation activities."

### 5. Direct Relevance to Term Paper
- **RQ1 (governance practices):** The paper's discussion of sovereignty (supervised vs. unsupervised machine designers, p. 30) and the need for governance mechanisms (p. 40) provides conceptual grounding for how organizations structure human oversight of autonomous systems.
- **P2 (workflow control rigidity):** The supervised/unsupervised distinction (p. 30) and the sequencing choices (human-first vs. machine-first, Table 1, p. 36) directly relate to workflow control rigidity.
- **Theoretical stream:** The paper's orchestration framework parallels the governance challenge of coordinating human and AI agents, though framed for innovation rather than risk.
- The paper is primarily about product innovation, not enterprise risk or compliance.

### 6. What It Does NOT Do
- Does not address enterprise risk, incident frequency, financial loss, or compliance.
- Does not engage with principal-agent theory.
- Does not measure governance outcomes quantitatively.
- Does not address agentic AI (focuses on machine designers in innovation).
- Does not discuss shadow AI or governance maturity.

### 7. Validity for Literature Survey
**Marginal Yes.** Published in California Management Review (A journal, ABDC). The supervised/unsupervised sovereignty framework and orchestration concepts provide useful analogies for governance of agentic AI. Worth citing in the literature review when discussing human-AI oversight structures, but not a core paper for the term paper's governance-risk focus.

## Paper 5: Eulerich, Waddoups, Wagener, and Wood (2024)

### 1. Full Citation
Eulerich, M., Waddoups, N., Wagener, M., and Wood, D. A. (2024). "The Dark Side of Robotic Process Automation." (Published 2024 based on file metadata; originally circulated as working paper February 2022. Journal publication details not explicitly stated in the extracted text; the SSRN identifier is 4026996.)

### 2. Core Argument
The paper argues that the very features that have driven RPA's widespread adoption (ease of use, low cost, minimal IT integration) are responsible for its largest challenges (p. 3). Through interviews with 26 RPA stakeholders at a Fortune 500 firm, the authors identify five key problems: RPA as a band-aid, control and security issues, misunderstood costs, governance challenges, and loss of process knowledge (p. 3).

### 3. Methodology
Qualitative interview study. 26 semi-structured interviews with RPA stakeholders at a Fortune 500 multinational conglomerate (~300,000 employees, >$60 billion revenue). Interviewees averaged 18.3 years of experience across six functions (top management, accounting, assurance, risk/internal control, cybersecurity, other). Interviews conducted March-April 2021 via video conferencing, average 32 minutes. Coded using MAXQDA software. Findings validated with chief audit executives of 16 corporations and 22 IT auditors at an IIA chapter meeting (pp. 5-7).

### 4. Key Findings
- **RPA as band-aid:** RPA is used to patch suboptimal processes rather than fix root causes, slowing down process improvement (pp. 7-8). "RPA is almost like a drug. It relieves the pain...but it does not address the underlying root cause" (p. 3, Chief Auditor quote).
- **Control and security issues:** Uncontrolled/unknown bots ("dark spots"), failure to assess bot risk, bots as hacking targets due to privileged access, bots providing bad data when underlying processes change (pp. 9-11). "Currently, we have collected 185 [bots]...These are just the robots we know" (p. 10, P-2).
- **Misunderstood costs:** Total cost of ownership understated; ongoing monitoring, assurance, and security costs ignored (pp. 11-12).
- **Governance challenges:** Tension between centralized control (assurance) and decentralized autonomy (motivation). "The biggest challenge...is getting compliance right for all of your bots, especially if you do this decentralized" (p. 12, P-14).
- **Loss of process knowledge:** Domain knowledge erodes as bots take over processes; knowledge of bot functionality lost when creators leave (pp. 13-14).

### 5. Direct Relevance to Term Paper
- **RQ1 (governance practices and risk categories):** This paper is directly relevant. The five problems map onto risk categories the term paper seeks to identify. The governance challenge (centralized vs. decentralized, p. 12) is a concrete governance practice dimension.
- **P1 (governance maturity reduces incidents):** The paper provides evidence that weak governance leads to incidents (unknown bots, security breaches, incorrect data). The external auditor discovering unknown bots (p. 10) is a concrete incident example.
- **P2 (workflow control rigidity):** The centralized vs. decentralized governance tension (p. 12) directly operationalizes control rigidity. Too rigid = kills motivation; too loose = compliance failures.
- **P3 (shadow AI):** The "dark spots" of unknown, unregistered bots (p. 10) are a direct empirical manifestation of shadow AI/shadow IT. "It's easy to generate a robot, and therefore, it's not that easy to know where it was created" (p. 10, P-2).
- **P4 (inverted U-shape):** The governance tension between "heavy governance" killing motivation and "missing governance" creating risk (p. 12, P-6) provides qualitative evidence for the inverted U-shape.
- **RQ2 (quantitative outcomes):** The paper documents incidents (external auditor finding unknown bots, incorrect financial data) but does not quantify them.

### 6. What It Does NOT Do
- Does not address agentic AI specifically (focuses on RPA bots, which are rule-based, not agentic).
- Does not use quantitative methods or measure firm-level risk outcomes statistically.
- Does not engage with principal-agent theory explicitly.
- Does not test propositions about governance maturity or control rigidity.
- Single-firm study limits generalizability.

### 7. Validity for Literature Survey
**Yes.** While the journal publication venue is not explicitly stated in the extracted text (SSRN working paper), the research quality is high (Fortune 500 firm, 26 interviews, validated with external groups). The findings on shadow bots, governance tensions, and risk categories are directly relevant to RQ1, P2, P3, and P4. This paper provides the closest empirical evidence to the term paper's propositions, even though it addresses RPA rather than agentic AI. The gap (RPA vs. agentic AI) is precisely what the term paper addresses.

## Paper 6: Haase, Kremser, Leopold, Mendling, Onnasch, and Plattfaut (2024)

### 1. Full Citation
Haase, J., Kremser, W., Leopold, H., Mendling, J., Onnasch, L., and Plattfaut, R. (2024). "Interdisciplinary Directions for Researching the Effects of Robotic Process Automation and Large Language Models on Business Processes." *Communications of the Association for Information Systems*, 54, Paper 21, pp. 579-604.

### 2. Core Argument
The paper argues that understanding the effects of process automation (RPA and LLMs) on business processes requires an interdisciplinary lens integrating three perspectives: automation technology (IS/BPM), human-automation interaction (HAI from psychology), and routine dynamics (organizational studies) (p. 580). The authors contend that automation does not simply replace human activity but changes it in ways often unintended and unanticipated (p. 585).

### 3. Methodology
Panel report based on a discussion held at the Routines.Research.Community workshop in March 2023 in Berlin, Germany. The paper integrates literature review across three disciplines with panel discussion insights. No primary empirical data collection. An edited transcript of the panel discussion is provided in Appendix A (p. 580).

### 4. Key Findings
- RPA effectiveness depends on human expert availability and skills (p. 581).
- Automation can lead to skill loss if humans do not understand underlying reasoning (p. 581). Example: new employee unable to execute a process when bot fails because "she's never done that" (p. 581).
- Automated decisions can have significant consequences for third parties; EU GDPR requires humans not be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing (p. 581).
- Automation changes job profiles, and employees may react negatively (pp. 581-582).
- From HAI research: automation bias (using automation as heuristic replacement for vigilant information processing), complacency (insufficient monitoring), and skill degradation are key risks (pp. 584-585).
- Higher degrees of automation yield better performance under normal conditions but worse performance during failures due to increased complacency and skill degradation (p. 585).
- Process automation transforms organizational routines, going beyond initial adoption to reshape workflow dynamics (p. 587).
- Research directions proposed include: user-centric design for task automation, understanding individual differences, long-term psychological impact, adaptive tools and training, AI integration in routine tasks, and dynamics in teams with AI-based members (p. 588, Figure 1).

### 5. Direct Relevance to Term Paper
- **RQ1 (risk categories):** The paper identifies risk categories from automation: automation bias, complacency, skill degradation, loss of situation awareness, and automation surprises (pp. 584-585). These are risk categories that emerge from automated/agentic deployments.
- **P1 (governance maturity reduces incidents):** The paper's finding that countermeasures (training on automation fallibility, adaptive automation) reduce automation bias (p. 586) supports the logic that governance practices reduce incidents.
- **P2 (workflow control rigidity):** The degree-of-automation tradeoff (more automation = better normal performance but worse failure performance, p. 585) directly supports the workflow control rigidity construct and the inverted U-shape logic.
- **P4 (inverted U-shape):** "An appropriate function allocation can only meet certain aspects, as higher degrees of automation are always accompanied by positive and negative aspects" (p. 585). This is empirical support from HAI research for the inverted U-shape.
- **Theoretical stream:** The routine dynamics perspective on how automation transforms organizational processes (p. 587) connects to the IT governance-to-business-value lineage.

### 6. What It Does NOT Do
- Does not address agentic AI specifically (focuses on RPA and LLMs in process automation).
- Does not provide firm-level quantitative data on risk outcomes.
- Does not engage with principal-agent theory.
- Does not test governance maturity or shadow AI as variables.
- Panel report format limits depth of empirical evidence.

### 7. Validity for Literature Survey
**Yes.** Published in Communications of the Association for Information Systems (A journal, ABDC). The interdisciplinary framework integrating HAI, routine dynamics, and IS perspectives provides theoretical grounding for the term paper's risk categories and the automation-control tradeoff. Particularly valuable for supporting P2 and P4 with HAI research evidence.

## Paper 7: Taeihagh (2025)

### 1. Full Citation
Taeihagh, A. (2025). "Governance of Generative AI." *Policy and Society*, 44(1), pp. 1-22.

### 2. Core Argument
The paper argues that the governance of generative AI requires a fundamental shift from reactive to proactive and adaptive approaches, with greater public participation and capacity building within the public sector (p. 7). It identifies seven key risk categories of generative AI and proposes a governance framework with eight strategic areas, emphasizing that voluntary self-regulation is insufficient and that comprehensive, systemic governance is needed (pp. 7-10, Table 2).

### 3. Methodology
Conceptual/policy analysis paper. The author synthesizes literature on generative AI risks and governance challenges, introduces a governance framework, and provides an overview of seven articles in the special issue on governance of generative AI. No primary empirical data collection.

### 4. Key Findings
- **Seven key risks of generative AI** (Table 1, pp. 3-5): hallucination/inaccuracies, jailbreaking/unexpected inputs, data training/validation risks, sensitive information risks, opacity risks, control risks, and design/implementation risks.
- **Control risks specific to agentic AI:** "Generative AI systems, particularly with the recent developments in agent-based models, can operate at different levels of autonomy (human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop, and fully autonomous)" (p. 4). Extensive deployment and multi-agent interaction "could result in emergent behavior" (p. 4).
- **Eight governance strategy areas** (Table 2, pp. 10-11): improving data governance/IPR, reducing bias/privacy violations, improving risk management/cybersecurity, reducing negative societal impacts, reducing power imbalances, enhancing public engagement, public sector enhancements, increasing international cooperation.
- Governance should include: regulatory sandboxes, red teaming, impact assessment, internal auditing as routine practices (p. 8).
- Voluntary guidelines alone are insufficient; stronger governance measures including antitrust policies and transparency requirements are needed (p. 6).
- Janssen (2025), cited in the special issue overview, proposes a complex adaptive system (CAS) perspective for AI governance (p. 11).

### 5. Direct Relevance to Term Paper
- **RQ1 (governance practices and risk categories):** The seven risk categories (Table 1, pp. 3-5) and eight governance strategy areas (Table 2, pp. 10-11) provide a taxonomy directly applicable to the term paper's qualitative phase.
- **RQ1 (agentic AI specifically):** The paper explicitly addresses agentic AI control risks: "the ability to detect and prevent misuse of the systems is important. If the systems have continuous learning capabilities that could result in undesirable behavior, the ability to swiftly control the system becomes more important, for example in the use of Agentic AI" (p. 4).
- **P1 (governance maturity reduces incidents):** The paper's argument that proactive governance reduces risks (pp. 7-8) and that reactive governance creates regulatory gaps (p. 7) supports P1.
- **P2 (workflow control rigidity):** The three levels of autonomy (human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop, fully autonomous, p. 4) map onto different levels of workflow control rigidity.
- **Theoretical stream:** The paper's governance framework connects to the IT governance lineage, and the CAS perspective (Janssen 2025, referenced on p. 11) offers a complementary theoretical lens.

### 6. What It Does NOT Do
- Does not provide empirical evidence from organizations.
- Does not measure governance outcomes quantitatively.
- Does not engage with principal-agent theory.
- Does not address enterprise-level risk outcomes (incident frequency, financial loss).
- Focuses on public policy governance rather than organizational/enterprise governance.
- Does not test propositions about shadow AI or control rigidity.

### 7. Validity for Literature Survey
**Yes.** Published in Policy and Society (Q1 journal per file metadata). The paper provides the most current and direct treatment of generative AI governance, including explicit discussion of agentic AI risks. The risk taxonomy and governance framework are directly applicable to RQ1. The public policy focus complements the term paper's enterprise-level focus, and the gap (public policy vs. enterprise governance) is what the term paper addresses.
