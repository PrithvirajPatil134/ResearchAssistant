## 1. What Is Theory?

Sutton & Staw, drawing on Kaplan (1964) and Merton (1967), define theory as **the answer to the question "why."** Theory is a story about why acts, events, structures, and thoughts occur. It is fundamentally about **connections among phenomena**, specifically the nature of causal relationships, including what comes first and the timing of events.

Weick (1995) captures it succinctly: a good theory **explains, predicts, and delights.**

DiMaggio refines this position by identifying three legitimate conceptions of theory:

- **Theory as covering laws.** Generalizations that, taken together, describe the world as we observe or measure it. The focus is on explaining variance. At its limit (e.g., in economics), this view holds that plausibility of assumptions matters less than explanatory power (Friedman, 1953). Sutton & Staw implicitly reject this as insufficient on its own, arguing that the "what" must be accompanied by the "how" and "why."

- **Theory as enlightenment.** Theory as a device of sudden defamiliarization, startling the reader into seeing the world differently. From this perspective, theory is complex, rich in paradox, and functions as a "surprise machine" (Gouldner, 1970). The aim is not to generalize (many generalizations are widely known and rather dull) but to clear away conventional notions and make room for artful insights.

- **Theory as narrative.** Theory as an account of a social process, with emphasis on empirical tests of the plausibility of the narrative and careful attention to scope conditions. The minimalist version (Collins, 1981) requires that hypotheses about variable relationships be accompanied by plausible accounts of how real human actions could produce the associations predicted and observed. More assertive versions argue that theory begins with formal baseline generator models that simulate observed distributions of outcomes (Fararo, 1989).

DiMaggio argues the best theories are often **hybrids** that combine elements of all three approaches. The act of combination requires compromise between competing and mutually incompatible values, which is part of what makes theory construction so difficult.

## 2. Components of Research That Are NOT Theory

Sutton & Staw erect five "Wrong Way" signs, identifying things commonly mistaken for theory but that are not:

### 2.1 References Are Not Theory

Citing prior work or listing the names of theoretical traditions does not constitute theory. Authors must explicate the causal logic from cited sources, not just name-drop. As Sutton & Staw illustrate, a sentence like *"This pattern is consistent with findings that aggression provokes the 'fight' response (Frijda, 1986) and that anger is a contagious emotion (Schacter and Singer, 1962; Baron, 1977)"* lists publications containing conceptual arguments but presents no theory. No logic is offered to explain *why* aggression provokes "fight" or *why* anger is contagious.

References can even serve as a "smoke screen" to hide the absence of genuine theoretical reasoning, especially when citations are made to widely known classics that readers praise but rarely re-read (Mark Twain's definition of a classic). Authors need to explicate which concepts and causal arguments are adopted from cited sources and how they link to the theory being developed or tested.

**Good example:** Weick (1993: 644) acknowledged his debt to Perrow's work and presented the aspects he needed to maintain logical flow: *"Because there is so little communication within the crew and because it operates largely through obtrusive controls like rules and supervision (Perrow, 1986), it acts more like a large formal group with mediated communication than a small informal group with direct communication."* The reader does not need to read Perrow to follow the logic.

### 2.2 Data Are Not Theory

Empirical patterns (beta weights, factor loadings, informant quotes) describe *what* was observed but do not explain *why*. Kaplan (1964) asserted that data and theory play distinct roles: data describe which empirical patterns were observed; theory explains why those patterns were observed or are expected.

Using a series of past findings to justify hypotheses without causal reasoning is "brute empiricism." This applies to both quantitative and qualitative work. Quotes from informants or detailed observations may get closer to underlying causal forces than mean scores, but qualitative evidence by itself cannot convey causal arguments abstract and simple enough to be applied to other settings.

As Mintzberg (1979: 584) summarized: **"The data do not generate theory; only researchers do that."**

### 2.3 Lists of Variables or Constructs Are Not Theory

A catalog of variables, even well-defined ones, does not constitute theory. Theory must explain *why* variables are connected. As Homans noted (cited in Weick, 1989: 517): *"much official sociological theory consists in fact of concepts and their definitions; it provides a dictionary of a language that possesses no sentences."*

Listing demographic characteristics associated with a behavior, dividing the world into personality versus situational determinants, or ordering variables in a LISREL model is not the same as providing a causal explanation. The key issue is always **why** a particular set of variables are expected to be strong predictors.

### 2.4 Diagrams Are Not Theory

Boxes-and-arrows figures can aid in structuring arguments and showing causal ordering, but they are **"stage props rather than the performance itself"** (Whetten, 1989). Figures that simply list categories of variables ("personality," "environmental determinants," "demographics") are the least theoretical. More helpful are figures showing causal chains or temporal processes.

However, the logical reasoning behind the portrayed relationships must always be spelled out in text. One indication of strong theory is that it is possible to discern conditions under which the major proposition is most and least likely to hold, and such reasoning is not apparent from a causal diagram alone.

### 2.5 Hypotheses (or Predictions) Are Not Theory

Hypotheses state *what* is expected to occur, not *why* it is expected to occur. As Kaplan (1964: 350) put it: *"An explanation rests on a nomological or theoretical generalization, or an intelligible pattern, but a prediction need not have such a basis."*

Two telltale signs that a paper has substituted hypotheses for theory:
1. There are so many hypotheses that none can be adequately explained or motivated.
2. The introduction ends with a long list of hypotheses, a table of predictions, or a summarizing figure only tenuously linked to causal explanations.

Strong papers typically start from **one or two core conceptual statements** and build a logically detailed case. They have both **simplicity and interconnectedness** (e.g., Festinger's cognitive dissonance, Campbell's evolutionary forces in social systems, Perrow's normal accidents).

## 3. Attributes of a Strong Theory

### From Sutton & Staw

| Attribute | Description |
|---|---|
| **Answers "why"** | Explains the causal mechanisms behind phenomena, not just what happens |
| **Probes underlying processes** | Burrows into microprocesses, extends laterally into neighboring concepts, or ties upward to broader social phenomena |
| **Logically interconnected arguments** | A convincing chain of reasoning, not a scattered set of claims |
| **Reveals the non-obvious** | Surfaces implications invisible to the "theoretically unassisted" eye, or even counter to common sense |
| **Simplicity and interconnectedness** | Starts from a small set of core ideas and builds outward |
| **Explains, predicts, and delights** | Weick's (1995) succinct criterion for good theory |

### From DiMaggio: Tensions That Strong Theory Navigates

Strong theory requires finding balance across several competing values:

- **Clarity vs. defamiliarization**: Enough novelty (new terms, paradox) to make readers see differently, but not so much jargon or paradox that the theory becomes incomprehensible. All good theory has a germ of paradox; achieving the right balance is the central challenge.

- **Focus vs. multidimensionality**: Strategic reduction that abstracts away enough of the world's confusion to develop pointed explanations, without crude reductionism. One person's multidimensionality is another's goulash; one author's focus is another's crude reductionism.

- **Comprehensiveness vs. memorability**: The most important causal factors are not always the most interesting. Our collective preoccupation with theoretical novelty can lead researchers to overlook important if banal patterns in their data. Strong theory finds the balance between explaining variance and offering surprising insight.

### DiMaggio's Additional Insight: Theory as Social Construction

Theory construction is partly **social construction**. A theory's fate depends on:

- **Cultural resonance**: whether the theory fits the presuppositions and intellectual climate of its time and audience.
- **Reduction to slogans**: widely read theories are often simplified by non-specialist readers until complex arguments fit preexisting schemas.
- **Post hoc reconstruction**: the quality of a theory is partly a function of the quality of the scholars who take it up and develop it further (e.g., Barnard's work was transformed by Simon and March into something far more crystalline than the original).

## 4. Structural Challenges in Theory Building

Sutton & Staw identify several structural reasons why strong theory is rare in organizational research:

- **Training gaps**: Doctoral programs emphasize data collection techniques and analytical tools (LISREL, event-history analysis) over theory building, which is not a step-by-step process that can be easily taught.
- **Double bind from journals**: Editors plead for creative ideas but reviewers punish mismatches between theory and data. Authors learn to craft theory around what they measured, omitting interesting variables or processes that would expose gaps in empirical design.
- **Compensatory education through review**: The review process tries to extract theory from authors who could not initially provide it, but this produces compliance rather than genuine learning.
- **Imbalance of skills**: Most researchers are adept at either theory building or empirical work, rarely both (Northcraft & Neale, 1993).

### Sutton & Staw's Recommendations

- **Rebalance selection between theory and method.** If a theory is particularly interesting, the standards for how well it is tested should be relaxed, not strengthened.
- **Decouple theory from data requirements.** Not everything discussed in the introduction needs to be operationalized. Authors should be rewarded for developing strong conceptual arguments that extend more broadly than the data will justify.
- **Apply qualitative standards more symmetrically.** Qualitative research is held to high theoretical standards but sometimes at the cost of losing rich description; quantitative research is held to high empirical standards but often at the cost of weak theory.

---

## 5. Central Implication

Theory is not a checklist of components (references, data, variables, diagrams, hypotheses) but rather the **causal reasoning that binds them together**. The five "not theory" elements are all necessary ingredients of a good paper, but none substitutes for the intellectual work of explaining *why* things happen the way they do.

DiMaggio usefully complicates this by showing that "good theory" is not monolithic. It can take different forms (covering laws, enlightenment, narrative), and the best theories sit at the intersection of competing values, requiring judgment rather than formula.

**The standard of evaluation:** Does the theoretical section answer "why" with a logically connected causal story, rather than relying on any of the five substitutes Sutton & Staw identify?
