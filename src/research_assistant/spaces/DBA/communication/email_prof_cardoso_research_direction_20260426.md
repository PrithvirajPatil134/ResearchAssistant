**Research direction**

When we last spoke, the thesis was framed around Digital Resource Orchestration and SaaS license rationalization, examining how internal IT governance mechanisms influence capital efficiency and firm performance. Your feedback on terminology (correlation vs. influence vs. impact vs. moderation) was very helpful and shaped how I think about the claims I can defensibly make.

Since then, my coursework in Quantitative Methods (QNTR) and Contemporary Research in Organisations (CRO) at IIM Sambalpur has pushed me toward a related but distinct problem: the governance of agentic AI systems and its relationship to enterprise risk. Agentic AI refers to autonomous, goal-directed programs that plan, call tools, and take actions that commit the firm to financial and operational consequences. The governance challenge is structurally different from governing traditional IT or even predictive AI models, because the agent acts on behalf of the firm with a degree of autonomy that creates a principal-agent problem.

The research I am developing examines whether agentic AI governance maturity predicts firm-level risk outcomes (incident frequency, financial loss, compliance failures). The theoretical anchors are principal-agent theory applied to AI agents (Kolt, 2025, in the Notre Dame Law Review), the IT governance-to-business-value lineage (Weill and Ross, 2004), and the responsible AI governance framework (Mikalef et al., 2025, in Information and Management). The closest empirical predecessor is Vu et al. (2025), who interviewed 22 BPM practitioners about agent governance but stopped at qualitative perceptions. My study proposes an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design: Phase 1 builds governance constructs from public discourse and practitioner data, Phase 2 tests them through binary logistic regression on a firm-level survey.

I see this as an evolution of the original DRO thesis rather than a departure from it. The core question remains the same: how does internal governance of technology resources affect firm outcomes? The object of governance has shifted from SaaS licenses to autonomous AI agents, but the theoretical mechanism (governance reduces agency loss and converts technology investment into performance) is consistent. Your earlier advice about using "moderation" rather than "impact" applies directly here as well: the study includes a moderation proposition (shadow AI prevalence weakening the governance-risk link) and avoids causal claims that the cross-sectional design cannot support.

I would value your perspective on whether this direction is viable for the DBA thesis, and whether the connection to the original DRO framing is strong enough to carry forward or whether it would be better positioned as a fresh start.
