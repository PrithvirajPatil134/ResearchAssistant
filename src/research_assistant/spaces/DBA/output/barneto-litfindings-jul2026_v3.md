# Literature Findings: Punctuated Equilibrium and Organizational Restructuring in the Generative AI Transition

## Abstract

Large technology firms reorganized at notable scale during the generative AI (GenAI) transition, with more than 260,000 tech-sector layoffs recorded in 2023 alone. Whether that restructuring follows the sequence punctuated equilibrium predicts, a stable period, an environmental jolt, revolutionary transformation, then a new equilibrium, remains untested for the GenAI window: the archival coding rules exist but have not yet been applied to it. The three measurement foundations for such a test are in place. AI adoption intensity is measurable and non-circular through Alekseeva et al.'s (2026) continuous AI Share; revolutionary versus nonrevolutionary transformation is separable through Romanelli and Tushman's (1994) coding and Girod and Whittington's (2015) 0.30 threshold; and organizational structure is recoverable from 10-K and DEF 14A filings (Albert et al., 2026).

## 1. Research question and sub-questions

Large technology firms reorganized between late 2022 and mid-2026 at a pace the sector had rarely recorded. The scale is visible in the counts: Layoffs.fyi recorded more than 260,000 tech-sector layoffs in 2023 and 108,724 across 137 companies in 2026 to date, cuts that firms routinely tied to generative AI (GenAI) strategy shifts. Headcount is only the visible edge. Roles, management layers, divisions, and reporting lines all moved. What the record does not yet hold is a systematic account of the organizational form these firms arrived at, read across companies over time rather than inferred from one-time surveys of adoption rates.

The outcome of interest is the shape of the organization, not the rate of adoption. The primary research question:

**How do organizational restructuring patterns differ between technology firms pursuing aggressive generative-AI integration and those adopting an incremental approach, and to what extent do these patterns align with the predictions of punctuated equilibrium theory** (Romanelli and Tushman, 1994)?

Three sub-questions sit under it:

- Which structural elements (management layers, role categories, divisional boundaries, reporting relationships) changed between 2022 and 2027 in the sampled firms?
- Do those firms converge toward common organizational forms, or diverge into distinct structural archetypes?
- Do the observed changes follow the sequence the theory predicts: a stable period, an environmental jolt, transformation, then a new equilibrium?

The question is answerable from public evidence. US-listed firms above $50 billion in market capitalization disclose headcount, segments, named leadership, and restructuring charges in filings and earnings calls, so organizational form can be read from the record.

## 2. Theoretical framework

Punctuated equilibrium (PE) anchors the study because it makes a specific, testable prediction about the shape of organizational change. Romanelli and Tushman (1994) argue that organizations move through long convergent periods of incremental adjustment interrupted by short bursts of revolutionary transformation. In their test of 25 US minicomputer producers, revolutionary transformations outnumbered gradual ones by at least six to one across every measurement criterion (Romanelli and Tushman, 1994, p.1156). The pattern is not a metaphor. It is a coded, replicable result.

The vocabulary needs care. The convergence-and-reorientation framework belongs to Tushman and Romanelli (1985), the earlier metamorphosis model. The 1994 empirical paper coded its dependent variable as "revolutionary transformation" versus "nonrevolutionary transformation," not "reorientation" and "convergence": a revolutionary transformation is recorded when substantial change occurs across all three domains of strategy, structure, and power distribution within a rolling two-year window (Romanelli and Tushman, 1994, p.1154). The 1985 paper supplies the lineage; the 1994 paper supplies the coding rule.

The trigger matters most for this study. Romanelli and Tushman (1994) tested three candidate triggers and found that major environmental change raised the likelihood of revolutionary transformation (b = 1.513, p < .01), as did CEO succession, while a market-share performance crisis did not (p.1159). Major environmental change was coded as a dummy for three industry shifts severe enough to threaten firm survival (pp.1155-1156). That environmental jolt is what the whole sequence turns on, and generative AI is the candidate jolt for large technology firms across 2022 to 2027.

Disruption theory is not operationalized here. It motivates why generative AI qualifies as a jolt forceful enough to compel a reorientation, while punctuated equilibrium describes the shape the response takes and carries every testable prediction. The coding method for revolutionary and nonrevolutionary transformation is established and transferable to public disclosure. Whether the punctuated pattern holds under a generative-AI jolt is the open empirical question, and testing it is what the study contributes. That test rests on three measurable foundations.

## 3. Literature survey across the three foundations

Three things must be measurable before the sequence can be tested: which firms are adopting generative AI and how intensively (the independent variable), whether a firm's transformation over a given window is revolutionary or gradual (the dependent variable), and the organizational structure whose change the coding turns on. The literature supplies a usable method for each, and none has yet been applied to the generative-AI window.

### Foundation 1: classifying firms by AI adoption intensity

Firm-level AI adoption intensity is measurable from archival data as a continuous quantity rather than a yes/no flag. Alekseeva et al. (2026, p.10) construct AI Share, the proportion of a firm's annual job postings that are AI-related, across 823 firms observed from 2010 to 2022; the measure is continuous, with a sample mean of 0.5% and a rise from below 0.1% to roughly 1% across the window (p.12). McElheran et al. (2024) confirm that intensity is gradable rather than binary: their 2018 Annual Business Survey, a US Census instrument sent to roughly 850,000 firms, records use of five AI technologies at less than 5%, 5 to 25%, and more than 25% of production (p.42, p.48). For a spending-based numerator, Mithas and Rust (2016, p.230) supply a ratio template, IT spending over sales revenue, averaging 4.09% in their sample, which a study can adapt to an AI-spending measure; the authors flag that their strategic-emphasis classification rests on a single self-report item (p.237), a limitation any adaptation inherits.

The intensity of a firm's technology pursuit can be captured with a validated instrument. Zahra and Covin's (1993) aggressive technological posture measure, a continuous three-item scale anchored against competitors, reaches a Cronbach's alpha of 0.75 in a sample of 103 mature-manufacturing firms (p.461, p.464). It is a continuous scale, not a leader-versus-follower typology, and it does not supply an AI-specific measure. It serves here as construct-validity evidence for the archival path, and at most as a fallback survey instrument, not as a co-equal measurement route.

What the literature does not yet provide is a validated, disclosure-based intensity measure for the generative-AI period. AI Share ends in 2022 and captures the predictive-AI wave; the authors note that generative and agentic systems redistribute cognitive complexity across organizational roles in qualitatively different ways (Alekseeva et al., 2026, p.4). The study's most exposed proxy addresses this directly: a disclosed AI-intensity measure for 2022 to 2027, built from filings and earnings-call text, then validated by benchmarking each firm's classification against McElheran's production-intensity thresholds (2024, p.48). Because AI Share predates the generative wave, McElheran's graded scale, not AI Share itself, is the anchor a new disclosed measure must reproduce before it can carry the independent variable.

### Foundation 2: operationalizing punctuated equilibrium in archival data

The dependent variable has an established coding rule, and it is fully archival. Romanelli and Tushman (1994, p.1154) code a revolutionary transformation whenever substantial change occurs in all three domains of strategy, structure, and power distribution within a two-year window, restarting the count after each transformation; two independent coding teams reached 87% agreement across 25 firms (p.1149). Lant, Milliken and Batra (1992, p.593) set a lower bar, making a strategy change necessary but not sufficient and requiring it to couple with change in at least two of three supporting domains, with 88% intercoder agreement. Girod and Whittington (2015) convert the idea into a continuous measure: a structural change ratio summing absolute year-over-year shifts in title proportions, with a 0.30 cut-off for discontinuous restructuring validated through executive interviews (pp.1525-1526). The continuous form sits most comfortably with an archival design.

**Exhibit 1. Punctuated-equilibrium coding approaches compared**

| Approach | Rule | Threshold | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multi-domain simultaneity | Substantial change in all three domains (strategy, structure, power distribution) | Rolling two-year window; count restarts after each transformation | Romanelli & Tushman (1994, p.1154) |
| Strategy-plus coupling | Strategy change necessary but not sufficient, plus change in at least two of three supporting domains | 25% power-distribution cut-off, which the authors call "somewhat arbitrary" | Lant et al. (1992, pp.594-595) |
| Proportion-based | Sum of absolute year-over-year proportional title shifts | 0.30 restructuring dummy, interview-validated | Girod & Whittington (2015, p.1526) |

The canonical rule carries a data-driven compromise worth flagging. The 1985 framework behind it proposed five deep-structure domains: culture, strategy, structure, power distribution, and control systems. Romanelli and Tushman collected all five but dropped culture and control systems because firms "reported information about them infrequently and inconsistently," coding only three (1994, p.1147). Richer 2026 disclosure, in particular earnings-call transcripts amenable to natural-language processing, may make the two dropped domains recoverable. Whether it does is untested, and recovering them is a candidate methodological contribution rather than a promised deliverable.

Every one of these operationalizations was built for a pre-generative setting: minicomputers, furniture, software, and large industrials. Each is a method for how to code transformation, not evidence about how AI-era firms behave. Applying the all-three-domains-in-two-years rule, or the 0.30 ratio, to the sample firms across 2022 to 2027 is what turns the open question into a test: each firm-period is classified as revolutionary or nonrevolutionary, and the distribution of those classifications is the finding.

### Foundation 3: measuring organizational structure from disclosure

Structure, the object whose change the transformation coding turns on, is measurable from corporate disclosure. Albert, Eklund and Tang (2026) built a top management team (TMT) structure database covering 161,028 executive-firm-years from 1993 to 2020, drawn from 10-K, 20-F, and DEF 14A filings and labeled into six role groups and twelve hierarchical levels by a fine-tuned GPT-4o model that reached 98.1% accuracy against a hold-out sample (p.14, p.16, p.17). Their recommended change measure is proportional rather than absolute: year-over-year shifts in the share of business-unit versus functional roles, which they use to detect reorganizations at Hewlett-Packard and General Electric (p.21). Structure can also be coded more simply. Lee et al. (2015, p.259) classify a firm as customer-centric or internally aligned from its reportable-segment disclosures under SFAS No. 131, using two expert coders with under 7% disagreement across 137 firms and 1,241 firm-year observations; the classification is a binary customer-centric dummy, not a multi-category scheme. Larcker, Richardson and Tuna (2007) sit at the elaborate end, compressing 39 proxy-statement variables by principal component analysis into 14 components across 2,106 firms (p.966, p.974).

None of these instruments has yet been applied to the generative-AI window. Albert's database ends in 2020, Lee's panel closes in 2010, and Larcker's is a single 2002-to-2003 cross-section. The machinery for reading structure off filings is in good order; evidence on how that structure moves in 2022 to 2027, when the jolt lands, is what remains thin, and earnings-call text as a structure signal is still a frontier method. For each sample firm across 2022 to 2027, the test would code TMT composition into the six role groups and twelve levels, then read structural change from proportional shifts in that composition (Albert et al., 2026, p.3). That structure series feeds the transformation coding of Foundation 2, with Lee's binary form or Larcker's index available as sensitivity alternatives.

Three gaps run through the foundations, each one of insufficiency rather than absence. The literature remains limited on a validated, disclosure-based intensity measure for the generative-AI era; existing PE operationalizations were built for pre-generative settings, so systematic evidence on whether the pattern holds under a generative-AI jolt is scarce; and whether richer 2026 disclosure can recover the culture and control-systems domains has not yet been examined.

The first five are load-bearing: Alekseeva supplies the independent variable, McElheran the scale that validates it, Romanelli and Tushman the transformation coding rule, Girod and Whittington its continuous form, and Albert the structure data source. The remaining five do bounded jobs. All are cited for how to code archival change, not as evidence about how generative-AI-era firms behave; whether the pattern holds is the open question.

## 5. How the theory will be tested

The design never leaves public disclosure and requires no primary data collection. The study would take six to eight US-listed technology firms above $50 billion in market capitalization, observe them across fiscal years 2022 to 2027, and split them into aggressive versus incremental generative-AI adopters. That split is the independent variable, built from inputs each firm files itself: an AI Share ratio of AI-related to total job postings, following Alekseeva et al. (2026, p.10), read alongside the AI language in 10-K filings. Because AI Share predates the generative wave (Alekseeva et al., 2026, p.11), the disclosed 2022-to-2027 measure is benchmarked against McElheran's production-intensity bands (2024, p.48) to sort firms into light, moderate, and heavy adoption.

The dependent variable is structural form, coded two ways so the finding rests on no single rule. First, revolutionary versus nonrevolutionary transformation as Romanelli and Tushman (1994, p.1154) code it: substantial change in strategy, structure, and power distribution inside a two-year window. Second, Girod and Whittington's (2015, p.1526) structural change ratio, with its 0.30 cutoff for discontinuous restructuring. Both read off TMT composition drawn from 10-K and DEF 14A filings using Albert et al.'s (2026, p.14) role-group and hierarchical-level taxonomy, scored as proportional change rather than raw headcount. The two variables do not embed each other: the independent variable is a labor and spending input, the dependent variable is organizational form, so a firm can score high on adoption and low on restructuring, or the reverse.

This is a deductive test of an existing theory against public archival data, the same shape as Romanelli and Tushman's (1994) own empirical test, which coded transformation from objective measures and reported results across four sensitivity thresholds rather than a single cutoff (pp.1151-1153). It is not an interview-based case study, and it does not set out to build new theory. The six to eight firms are chosen to contrast aggressive and incremental adopters so the test is sharp, not to sample a population, so the finding confirms or disconfirms whether the predicted pattern appears in these firms rather than estimating how common it is across the sector. Questions of prevalence, how many firms and how often, fall outside the scope of this archival study.

The expectation follows. Aggressive adopters should cross into revolutionary transformation, multi-domain change within the window or a ratio above 0.30, while incremental adopters stay nonrevolutionary, with disruption theory accounting for why the generative-AI jolt is the trigger. The coding works on public filings; what the study adds is a first archival reading of whether large technology firms punctuate under generative AI.
