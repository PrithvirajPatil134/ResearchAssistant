# SAI TEX LTD.: A RED QUEEN RACE IN ICHHALKARANJI

## Teaching Note

### Synopsis

Avinash Kisanrao Patil, founder and sole proprietor of Sai Tex Ltd., a job-work powerloom unit in Ichhalkaranji, Maharashtra, faces an existential decision in April 2026. His ten Rifa Electronic Rapier looms, purchased in 2010 for US$130,400 (₹1.2 crore), are now 15 years old, operating at 300 RPM against the 1,200 RPM of modern air-jet looms that competitors have adopted. Three converging forces have pushed Sai Tex from a monthly profit of US$5,087 in 2023 to a monthly loss of US$261 in 2025: a 40 per cent collapse in fabric exports following US tariffs, a doubling of electricity costs, and accelerating migrant labour attrition to upgraded mills. Patil must choose among three options: (A) a US$472,826 "Green Technical Pivot" involving twelve new air-jet looms, a rooftop solar plant, and a shift to the sale-purchase business model, requiring his family bungalow as collateral; (B) a US$43,478 to US$54,348 retrofit of existing machines to buy two to three years of survival; or (C) liquidation and exit. The case provides detailed financial, production, and technology data across seven exhibits, enabling quantitative analysis of each option. It is designed for courses in entrepreneurship, operations strategy, MSME management, and family business at the MBA, executive education, and doctoral level.

Note: US$1 ≈ ₹92 (March 2026). All dollar figures in this teaching note use this rate.


### Learning Objectives

After reading and analysing this case, students will be able to:

1. Evaluate strategic options under resource constraints using the financial and operational data provided in the case exhibits.
2. Analyse how the Red Queen Effect operates in a traditional manufacturing cluster where firms must continuously invest in technology merely to maintain their competitive position.
3. Compare the job-work model with the sale-purchase model and evaluate when a business model pivot is warranted.
4. Apply the Theory of Constraints to identify whether Sai Tex's binding constraint is technology, labour, market access, or capital.
5. Examine how socio-emotional wealth (SEW) shapes strategic choices in ways that diverge from purely financial optimisation.

### Assignment Questions

1. Using the data in Exhibits 1, 2, and 7, construct a break-even analysis for Sai Tex under the current job-work model. At what monthly production volume (in metres) does Sai Tex break even at the 2025 cost structure? What job-work rate (paisa per pick) would Sai Tex need to break even at its current average production volume?

2. Evaluate the three strategic options (Exhibit 3) using a decision matrix that weighs financial viability, operational risk, stakeholder impact, and strategic positioning. Which option would you recommend, and under what assumptions does your recommendation hold?

3. How does the Red Queen Effect explain Sai Tex's competitive decline? Identify the specific mechanisms through which technological stagnation has created a self-reinforcing cycle of decline for the firm.

4. Apply the Theory of Constraints to Sai Tex's operations. What is the binding constraint: technology, labour, market access, or capital? How does identifying the correct constraint change the recommended course of action?

5. Patil's father instilled in him the belief that "if you have the means to give someone a livelihood, you have the duty to do it." How does this socio-emotional wealth orientation affect Patil's strategic calculus? Should non-financial considerations change the recommended option? Why or why not?

### Analysis

**Question 1: Break-Even Analysis**

The first step is to separate Sai Tex's fixed and variable costs using Exhibit 7 data.

Fixed costs per month add up as follows: bank interest at US$1,304, base labour payroll at US$2,184, spare parts and mill store at US$130, and other expenses at US$54. That gives a total of roughly US$3,673 per month (₹337,900). These costs do not change with production volume in the short run. Patil pays them whether the looms run or not.

Variable costs are primarily power and transport. Scaled to per-metre terms, these come to about US$0.017 per metre (₹1.56).

At the 2025 average realised rate of US$0.088 per metre, or ₹8.07 (from Exhibit 2, full-year average), the contribution margin per metre is US$0.088 minus US$0.017, which gives US$0.071 (₹6.51).

The break-even volume is then US$3,673 divided by US$0.071, which works out to approximately 51,900 metres per month.

Here is the problem. Sai Tex's peak single-shift production in 2025 was only 45,000 metres (January and August, per Exhibit 2). The break-even volume exceeds the unit's maximum observed output by nearly 7,000 metres. At the current cost structure and rate, Sai Tex cannot break even under any achievable production scenario. The gap is structural, not marginal.

The break-even rate calculation tells the same story from the other direction. At the 2025 average production of about 37,800 metres per month (454,000 total divided by 12), total cost works out to US$3,673 plus (US$0.017 × 37,800) = US$4,316 (₹396,868). Dividing by 37,800 metres gives a required rate of US$0.114 per metre (₹10.50), or roughly 22 paisa per pick at the average 47 picks/cm density.

The 2023 rate was 25 paisa per pick. By 2025, it averaged 17.5 paisa. Sai Tex would need a 26 per cent rate recovery from 2025 levels to break even, and the case offers no evidence that such a recovery is coming. The domestic market remains flooded with surplus fabric from export-oriented units that lost their US market.

The takeaway: Sai Tex's problem is not cyclical. It is not a temporary rate dip that will correct itself. The cost base has permanently outgrown the revenue capacity of the current technology. This is the quantitative foundation for the Red Queen argument in Question 3.

**Question 2: Strategic Option Evaluation**

A useful way to structure this is a decision matrix with four criteria: financial viability, operational risk, stakeholder impact, and strategic positioning. The weights below are suggestive. Part of the exercise is having students argue for their own weighting.

| Criterion (suggested weight) | Option A: Green Pivot | Option B: Hybrid Lean | Option C: Exit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Financial viability (30%) | High upside (US$10,870–16,304/month net profit) but extreme downside risk. Total debt reaches US$603,261 at 14%. | Does not reach break-even. Slows the bleed but does not stop it. | Stops losses immediately. US$108,696 residual debt remains. |
| Operational risk (25%) | High. Assumes full capacity from day one, labour returns, market holds, subsidy arrives on time. | Low. Proven technology, incremental change, no production shutdown. | None. Operations cease. |
| Stakeholder impact (25%) | Positive if successful: retains and attracts workers, transforms community standing. | Neutral to negative: workers continue to leave, slow decline. | Highly negative: 12+ permanent workers displaced, community loss. |
| Strategic positioning (20%) | Strong. Matches competitors, enables sale-purchase pivot, energy independence. | Weak. Remains in the commodity trap with obsolete technology. | None. Exits the industry. |

The class will likely split into three camps.

Those favouring Option A will point to the break-even analysis from Q1 as proof that incremental measures cannot save Sai Tex. The only path to viability is a step-change in capacity and business model. The MAHA-TUFS subsidy (up to 25%) and solar energy independence reduce the effective risk. Two competitors in the same Park have already succeeded with this path.

Those favouring Option B will counter that Option A's assumptions are heroic. Full capacity from day one? Stable domestic market? Timely subsidy disbursement? Labour returning to a unit still under construction? Option B preserves optionality. It buys 2–3 years during which market conditions may improve, subsidy access may become easier, or Patil may accumulate savings toward a future upgrade without risking the family home.

A smaller group will argue for Option C. The US$21,739 resale value against US$130,435 in debt means Patil is already deeply underwater. Adding US$472,826 of new debt at 14 per cent in a declining market is a gamble, not a strategy. Preserving the family's remaining assets is the responsible choice.

The key point to draw out: Option A's attractiveness depends on four assumptions holding simultaneously. The domestic fabric market must hold. The MAHA-TUFS subsidy must be disbursed on time. The air-jet looms must run at capacity from day one. And migrant workers must return. If any one fails, the interest on US$603,261 at 14 per cent consumes the family home. The case is deliberately designed so that no option is clearly dominant. The "right" answer depends on the student's risk tolerance, discount rate, and weighting of non-financial factors.


**Question 3: The Red Queen Effect**

The Red Queen Effect (Barnett and Hansen, 1996) describes a competitive dynamic where firms must continuously adapt merely to maintain their relative position. The case title invokes this metaphor directly, and the mechanism at Sai Tex is textbook.

The cycle works like this. Sai Tex's rapier looms operate at 300 RPM, producing about 100 metres per day per machine. Competitors with air-jet looms run at 1,200 RPM and produce 400 metres per day. That four-fold throughput gap means Sai Tex spreads its fixed costs over far fewer metres, driving up per-unit costs. Higher per-unit costs compress margins. Compressed margins mean Patil cannot match the wages that competitors with newer machines offer, and those competitors pay roughly 10 per cent more. So workers leave. With fewer workers, Sai Tex runs at 50 per cent capacity, which pushes per-unit costs even higher. And with margins this thin, Patil cannot accumulate the capital needed to upgrade. The cycle feeds itself.

At the cluster level, something interesting has happened. Ichhalkaranji's historical strength was flexibility and dense local networks. A weaver could find a new customer within days. But that same network now allows workers to find a better employer within days. The cluster's competitive advantage has shifted from flexibility to technology, and units that have not upgraded are being selected out.

The US tariff (August 2024) did not create this problem. It accelerated a pre-existing decline. The technology gap existed before the tariff. What the tariff did was flood the domestic market with surplus fabric from export-oriented units, compressing job-work rates below break-even. External shocks disproportionately harm firms that are already competitively weakened. Sai Tex was already weakened.

This is why Option B is strategically insufficient even if it is financially safer. Retrofitting 15-year-old machines does not change Sai Tex's position on the technology curve. It merely slows the rate of decline. The competitors who have already upgraded will continue to attract workers, capture clients, and drive down rates. Option B is running faster on the same treadmill. Option A is switching to a faster treadmill.

**Question 4: Theory of Constraints**

Goldratt's Theory of Constraints asks a simple question: what is the one constraint that limits the system's throughput? Four candidates present themselves in this case.

*Technology.* The evidence for: 300 RPM versus 1,200 RPM, a four-fold throughput gap, 15-year-old machines. The evidence against: even the current machines are running at only 50 per cent capacity. The technology is not fully used.

*Labour.* The evidence for: 50 per cent capacity utilisation, workers leaving for upgraded mills, 10 per cent wage premium at competitors. But labour is leaving because competitors with air-jet looms generate higher margins and can afford to pay more. Sai Tex's older machines cannot generate sufficient margin to match those wages. The labour drain is a symptom of the underlying economic gap, not the root cause.

*Market access.* The evidence for: job-work rate below break-even, no pricing power, export market collapsed by 40 per cent. But under the sale-purchase model, market access changes entirely. This constraint is model-dependent.

*Capital.* The evidence for: US$130,435 existing debt, 14 per cent interest rate, bank requires the bungalow as collateral, no formal financial records. But MAHA-TUFS could offset 25 per cent, and capital is available if collateral is pledged. This constraint is binding but relaxable.

The answer I would give: technology is the root constraint. Labour, market, and capital constraints are all downstream effects. Technology limits wage competitiveness, which drives labour attrition, which drives capacity underutilisation, which drives per-unit cost inflation, which drives the inability to accumulate capital for upgrades. The constraints are nested and interactive.

If technology is the root constraint, then all other decisions (business model, energy, workforce) should be subordinated to resolving the technology gap. This points toward Option A.

If capital is the root constraint, the priority shifts to finding the lowest-capital path to viability. That points toward Option B as a bridge, or toward creative financing (phased upgrade, leasing, cooperative investment) that the case does not explicitly offer but that students may propose.

The TOC analysis reinforces the Red Queen argument: Option B addresses symptoms (energy costs, working conditions) but not the root constraint (technology). Breaking the cycle requires addressing the root, not the symptoms.

**Question 5: Socio-Emotional Wealth and Non-Financial Decision Criteria**

The SEW framework (Gómez-Mejía et al., 2007; Berrone et al., 2012) identifies five dimensions of non-financial value that family firm owners seek to preserve. Each one shows up in Sai Tex's story.

*Family control and influence.* Patil is sole proprietor. Every decision is his. Option A requires pledging the family bungalow, effectively transferring control to the bank.

*Identification with the firm.* The case states it directly: "The mill was not merely a business but an extension of Patil's identity and social obligations." This makes Option C psychologically and socially unacceptable, even when the numbers might favour it.

*Binding social ties.* Twelve permanent employees depend on Sai Tex. Shivmani Mishra has been with the firm since founding. Patil supports local schools. These obligations do not appear on a balance sheet, but they weigh on the decision.

*Emotional attachment.* His father's legacy: "If you have the means to give someone a livelihood, you have the duty to do it." That sentence weighs against exit even when exit is financially rational.

*Renewal through dynastic succession.* Not directly addressed, since Patil is first-generation. But the bungalow represents intergenerational family wealth, and Option A puts it at risk.

A purely financial analysis might favour Option C. It stops the bleeding and preserves remaining family assets. But SEW theory predicts that family firm owners will accept greater financial risk to preserve non-financial value. Patil's identification with the firm, his ties to workers, and his father's legacy all create a strong SEW-preservation motive that makes exit psychologically unacceptable.

The tension is real. SEW preservation pushes Patil toward Option A (the only option that preserves the firm and its social role), but Option A also poses the greatest threat to SEW if it fails. Losing the family bungalow would destroy the very family wealth and stability that SEW is meant to protect.

The class debate will likely produce three positions. Some students will argue that SEW considerations are a form of cognitive bias, that Patil is letting emotional attachment cloud his judgment. Others will counter that SEW is not irrational; it reflects a different utility function. Patil's social capital, community standing, and personal identity are real assets with economic value (worker loyalty, supplier trust, cooperative membership). Destroying them through exit has costs that a financial model does not capture. The most thoughtful students will reframe the question: not "should SEW matter?" but "how much risk is SEW worth?", and the answer depends on the probability-weighted outcomes of Option A.

The central pedagogical insight: real-world strategic decisions in MSMEs and family firms are never purely financial. Patil's decision is genuinely difficult not because he lacks information, but because his financial and non-financial objectives pull in opposite directions.

### What Happened

As of April 2026, Patil had not made a final decision. He was in active discussions with his bank regarding the MAHA-TUFS subsidy application for the full modernisation (Option A). The bank had indicated willingness to finance the upgrade but required the family bungalow as additional collateral.

Patil's response to the bank manager's question, "Are we processing the MAHA-TUFS subsidy application for the full modernisation, or are we just renewing the existing cash credit limit?", was: "We are applying for MAHA-TUFS subsidy. Without subsidy there will be huge financial burden."

This suggests Patil was leaning toward Option A but recognised that the subsidy was a precondition for its viability. The case was written during this decision window; the outcome remained uncertain at the time of publication.

Several contextual developments were relevant:

- Two competitors in the Pride Co-operative Textile Park, M.F. Rathi Textiles and P.A. Patil Textiles, had already completed air-jet upgrades and were reportedly capturing Sai Tex's former clients.
- The domestic fabric market remained oversupplied due to diverted exports, with no recovery in job-work rates visible as of early 2026.
- The China+1 sourcing strategy that some industry observers had predicted would benefit Indian textile MSMEs had, as of early 2026, produced no discernible positive impact on units like Sai Tex.
- Patil had not been aware of the MAHA-TUFS subsidy until the case researcher informed him, a telling example of the information asymmetry that small-unit owners face in accessing government support programmes.
