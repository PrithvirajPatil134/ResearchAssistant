**Where the term paper stands**

The paper is titled "Agentic AI Governance and Its Impact on Enterprise Risk and Business Performance." The core argument is that agentic AI systems, autonomous goal-directed programs that plan, call tools, and commit the firm to financial and legal consequences, recreate the classical principal-agent problem inside the firm.

The theoretical anchor is Kolt (2025), who applies principal-agent theory to AI agents and identifies three governance requirements: inclusivity of stakeholders, visibility of agent behaviour, and liability for outcomes. On the management side, I draw on Weill and Ross (2004) for the IT governance-to-business-value link and Mikalef et al. (2021, 2025) for the extension to AI capability and responsible AI governance. The empirical anchor on the BPM side is Vu et al. (2025), who interviewed 22 BPM practitioners and derived six governance recommendations (business context, guardrails, human-agent collaboration, customisation, risk management, and adoption) and four alignment actions for bridging traditional BPM with agentic AI. Their study is the only empirical work on agent governance in organisational settings to date, but it is purely qualitative and expectation-based: none of the 22 participants had deployed agentic AI in production.

The constructs are as follows. The IV is agentic AI governance maturity (breadth and depth of formally adopted governance practices, with the initial item pool drawn from the Vu et al. governance categories and refined through Phase 1 coding). The mediator is workflow control rigidity (deterministic constraints, approval gates, scoped tool use that translate governance policy into operational controls). The DV is enterprise AI risk exposure, operationalised through incident frequency, financial loss, and compliance failures. The moderator is shadow AI prevalence, the share of agentic AI activity happening outside sanctioned governance channels.

The design is exploratory sequential mixed-methods (Creswell and Plano Clark, 2018; Venkatesh, Brown and Bala, 2013). Phase 1 builds governance constructs inductively from public executive discourse, regulatory filings (10-K, 8-K), and practitioner interviews using thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006). Phase 2 tests those constructs on a firm-level survey using binary logistic regression, with the DV being whether the firm experienced a reportable agentic AI incident in the past 12 months.

The paper now carries four propositions:
- P1: Higher governance maturity predicts fewer reportable incidents (direct effect via principal-agent theory).
- P2: Workflow control rigidity mediates the governance-to-risk link (governance policy becomes operational constraint).
- P3: Shadow AI prevalence moderates the governance-risk relationship, weakening governance's protective effect.
- P4 (new): The relationship between workflow control rigidity and risk follows an inverted U-shape, such that moderate control minimises risk while both insufficient and excessive rigidity increase it.

I mention all of this because your feedback yesterday confirmed the direction. You noted that governance is the right focus area since it will remain relevant regardless of how fast the underlying AI technology evolves. The paper is built on exactly that premise: the principal-agent relationship between a firm and its autonomous delegates is a structural problem, not a technology-specific one.

**A question on theory-in-use**

You mentioned exploring theory-in-use as a possible method. I have been thinking about how this could strengthen Phase 1 of the study. Right now, Phase 1 uses thematic analysis on public discourse and filings, with the Vu et al. governance categories as a sensitising framework for deductive coding. I have added a theory-in-use lens (Argyris and Schon, 1974) to structure the axial coding stage: governance practices disclosed in public forums (earnings calls, keynotes, published frameworks) represent espoused theory, while incident disclosures, regulatory filings, and practitioner interview accounts of actual deployment behaviour represent theory-in-use. The gap between what firms say they do and what they actually do becomes a governance variable in its own right, one that may predict risk exposure independently of the governance practices themselves. This also addresses a limitation of Vu et al., whose data capture only espoused governance expectations from practitioners who had not yet deployed agents.

I would appreciate your thoughts on whether this framing is on the right track, or whether theory-in-use would be better suited as a separate follow-up study rather than an embedded analytical lens within Phase 1.

**A question on non-linearity (P4)**

In Q3 of yesterday's exercise, I proposed an inverted U-shaped relationship between the degree of autonomy granted to agentic AI and capital loss prevention. I have now formalised this as P4 in the term paper: the relationship between workflow control rigidity and enterprise AI risk exposure follows an inverted U-shape. At low rigidity, agents operate without adequate oversight and incidents accumulate. At excessively high rigidity, the operational value of the agent is suppressed, practitioners route work around the controls (increasing shadow AI), and the firm loses the capability it deployed the agent to provide. The Vu et al. finding that practitioners recommended a "gradual approach," starting with simple tasks and increasing autonomy as trust grows, implies that practitioners intuitively recognise this non-linearity. I have not seen this tested in the agentic AI literature, but the logic seems defensible. Would you consider this a valid addition to the conceptual framework, or does it overextend the model given the sample size constraints of Phase 2?
