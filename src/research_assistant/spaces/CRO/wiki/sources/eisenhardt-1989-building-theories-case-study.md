## Summary

Eisenhardt sets out a process for inducting theory from case studies, tracing the
work from the first statement of a research question through to closure. The paper's
own abstract describes it this way: some parts of the process, such as problem
definition and construct validation, resemble hypothesis-testing research, while
others, such as within-case analysis and replication logic, are unique to the
inductive, case-oriented process. The approach is "highly iterative and tightly
linked to data" and is "especially appropriate in new topic areas," yielding theory
that is "often novel, testable, and empirically valid"
[spaces/CRO/knowledge/Session 4_002. AMJ_1989_Eisenhardt Kathleen_Theory building from Case Study Research.pdf, p.532].

The paper makes two stated contributions. The first is a roadmap for building theory
from case study research that synthesizes prior work on qualitative methods (Miles &
Huberman, 1984), case study design (Yin, 1981, 1984), and grounded theory building
(Glaser & Strauss, 1967), extending it in areas such as a priori specification of
constructs, triangulation of multiple investigators, within-case and cross-case
analysis, and the role of existing literature. The second is positioning theory
building from cases within the larger context of social science research, including
its strengths, weaknesses, and the situations where it is an attractive approach
[p.532-533]. Eisenhardt frames the gap she addresses as one of insufficiency: prior
authors had "outlined pieces of the process," but confusion remained about how to
combine them, when to use the approach, and how to evaluate it [p.532, p.534].

## Key Arguments

- The tie between theory and actual data has often been thin in organizational
  research; the intimate connection with empirical reality is what permits a testable,
  relevant, and valid theory (citing Glaser & Strauss, 1967) [p.532].
- Theory-building research should begin "as close as possible to the ideal of no
  theory under consideration and no hypotheses to test," because preordained
  perspectives may bias and limit the findings. Eisenhardt concedes the clean slate is
  impossible to achieve but argues that approaching it matters [p.536].
- Both the initial research question and any a priori constructs are tentative. No
  construct is guaranteed a place in the resultant theory no matter how well measured,
  and the research question itself may shift during the study [p.536].
- Case selection rests on theoretical, not statistical, sampling: cases are chosen to
  replicate or extend emergent theory, fill theoretical categories, or provide polar
  types. Random selection is "neither necessary, nor even preferable" [p.537].
- Building theory from cases centers on juxtaposing contradictory or paradoxical
  evidence, which "unfreezes" thinking and can generate theory with less researcher
  bias than incremental studies or armchair deduction [p.546-547].
- The process is "strikingly iterative," moving backward and forward between steps,
  and is held in tension between divergence (multiple investigators, multiple data
  types, diverse cross-case tactics) and convergence (settling on construct
  definitions, measures, and a framework) [p.546].
- The overall message: most empirical work runs from theory to data, but the
  accumulation of knowledge requires cycling between the two; Eisenhardt hopes to
  prompt more research going "from data to theory" [p.548 (top), continued from p.548
  corrupt region].

## Methodology

This is a methodological essay, not an empirical study. Eisenhardt builds the roadmap
by synthesizing prior methods literature and illustrating each step with published
inductive case studies (summarized in the paper's Table 2, "Recent Examples of
Inductive Case Study Research," p.535). The recurring exemplars include Bourgeois &
Eisenhardt / Eisenhardt & Bourgeois (1988) on strategic decision making in high
velocity environments, Harris & Sutton (1986) on parting ceremonies in dying
organizations, Gersick (1988) on group development, Sutton & Callahan (1987) on
bankruptcy stigma, Mintzberg & McHugh (1985) on the National Film Board of Canada,
Burgelman (1983) on internal corporate venturing, Leonard-Barton (1988) on technical
innovation, and the Warwick study (Pettigrew, 1988) on strategic change [p.534-535].

The roadmap is presented as a sequence of steps, each paired with an activity and a
rationale, and is summarized in the paper's Table 1, "Process of Building Theory from
Case Study Research" [p.533]. The step headings that appear in the text are:

1. **Getting Started** — define the research question in broad terms and, optionally,
   specify a priori constructs; both remain tentative [p.536].
2. **Selecting Cases** — specify a population to control extraneous variation, then use
   theoretical (not statistical) sampling [p.536-537].
3. **Crafting Instruments and Protocols** — combine multiple data collection methods;
   combine qualitative and quantitative evidence; use multiple investigators [p.537-538].
4. **Entering the Field** — overlap data collection with analysis; keep field notes;
   practice "controlled opportunism" in adjusting data collection [p.538-539].
5. **Analyzing Within-Case Data** — write detailed within-case write-ups to become
   intimately familiar with each case as a stand-alone entity [p.539-540].
6. **Searching for Cross-Case Patterns** — use structured, divergent tactics (category
   dimensions, pairwise comparison, division by data source) to counter
   information-processing biases [p.540-541].
7. **Shaping Hypotheses** — sharpen constructs and verify emergent relationships case
   by case using replication logic [p.541-543].
8. **Enfolding Literature** — compare emergent theory with both conflicting and similar
   prior literature to build internal validity and generalizability [p.544].

The text also refers to a "Reaching Closure" step and to guidelines for evaluating
this type of research, but the passages covering closure and part of the assessment
guidelines fall in the OCR-lost regions of journal pages 545 and 548 (see Ingest
Notes). Those specific step details are not recoverable from this scan and are not
reconstructed here.

## Findings

Since the paper is methodological, its "findings" are the arguments for each step and
the summary judgments about the approach. The substantive points recovered from the
source:

- **On multiple investigators** [p.538]: teams add complementary insights and creative
  potential; convergent perceptions strengthen empirical grounding while conflicting
  perceptions guard against premature closure. Tactics include team site visits,
  assigning distinct roles (interviewer versus notetaker), separate research teams for
  different sites, and a resident "devil's advocate" kept out of the field (Sutton &
  Callahan, 1987).
- **On within-case analysis** [p.539-540]: the driving reality is a "staggering volume
  of data," and Pettigrew (1988) is cited for the phrase "death by data asphyxiation."
  There is no standard write-up format; the goal is intimate familiarity with each
  case before generalizing across cases.
- **On cross-case search** [p.540-541]: people are "notoriously poor processors of
  information" (citing Kahneman & Tversky; Nisbett & Ross), so the key is to look at
  the data in many divergent ways to avoid premature or false conclusions.
- **On shaping hypotheses** [p.541-543]: constructs are sharpened by constant
  comparison between data and construct across cases; relationships are verified case
  by case using replication logic, treating each case as an experiment (citing Yin,
  1984). The qualitative data explain the "why" behind relationships and help
  establish internal validity.
- **Strengths** [p.546-547]: (1) likelihood of generating novel theory through
  juxtaposition of contradictory evidence; (2) emergent theory tends to be testable
  because constructs and hypotheses have already been measured and verified during the
  process; (3) resultant theory is likely to be empirically valid because it is so
  tightly tied to evidence.
- **Weaknesses** [p.547]: (1) intensive use of evidence can yield overly complex theory
  that loses parsimony ("tries to capture everything"); (2) the bottom-up approach can
  produce narrow, idiosyncratic, "mid-range" theory that lacks the sweep of theories
  like resource dependence, population ecology, or transaction cost. Eisenhardt
  suggests "grand" theory may require an accumulation of both theory-building and
  theory-testing studies.

## Relevance to Research

This is a foundational methodological citation for any qualitative, inductive, or
case-based design. Within CRO work it grounds several recurring decisions: justifying
theoretical (rather than statistical) sampling [p.537], defending the combination of
qualitative and quantitative evidence within a single design [p.537-538], explaining
why data collection and analysis overlap and why instrument adjustment mid-study is
legitimate "controlled opportunism" rather than sloppiness [p.539], and setting
expectations that case-derived theory is often mid-range rather than grand [p.547]. It
also supplies the standard framing for a case study contribution: novel, testable, and
empirically valid theory in a new or under-theorized topic area [p.532].

## Limitations

Eisenhardt is explicit that the approach she codifies has real weaknesses, discussed
above under Findings [p.547]: a tendency toward theory that is either too complex
(sacrificing parsimony) or too narrow (idiosyncratic, mid-range). She also positions
the roadmap itself as adopting "a positivist view of research," directed toward
testable, generalizable hypotheses, and contrasts this explicitly with the more
description-oriented stances of Strauss (1987) and Van Maanen (1988) [p.546]. Readers
drawing on this paper for interpretivist work should note that stated positioning.

## Key Quotes

- On the value of empirical grounding: "it is the intimate connection with empirical
  reality that permits the development of a testable, relevant, and valid theory"
  [p.532].
- On starting with a focus (quoting Mintzberg, 1979): "No matter how small our sample
  or what our interest, we have always tried to go into organizations with a
  well-defined focus — to collect specific kinds of data systematically" [p.536].
  [OCR-uncertain: the dash in the original may be an em dash; transcribed here as a
  spaced dash.]
- On the synergy of systematic and anecdotal data (quoting Mintzberg, 1979): "For
  while systematic data create the foundation for our theories, it is the anecdotal
  data that enable us to do the building. Theory building seems to require rich
  description, the richness that comes from anecdote... it is only through the use of
  this soft data that we are able to explain them" [p.538].
- On the analysis gap (quoting Miles & Huberman, 1984): "One cannot ordinarily follow
  how a researcher got from 3600 pages of field notes to the final conclusions,
  sprinkled wth vivid quotes though they may be" [p.539]. [OCR-uncertain: "wth"
  appears as an OCR artifact for "with"; "3600" reads clearly but the original figure
  should be verified against a clean copy.]
- On flexibility versus rigor: "This flexibility is not a license to be unsystematic.
  Rather, this flexibility is controlled opportunism in which researchers take
  advantage of the uniqueness of a specific case and the emergence of new themes to
  improve resultant theory" [p.539].
- On the closing aim: "Most empirical studies lead from theory to data. Yet, the
  accumulation of knowledge involves a continual cycling between theory and data.
  Perhaps this article will stimulate some researchers to complete the cycle by
  conducting research that goes in the less common direction from data to theory"
  [p.548 top / continued from corrupt region].
