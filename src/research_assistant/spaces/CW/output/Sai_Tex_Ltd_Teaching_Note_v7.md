## SYNOPSIS

Avinash Kisanrao Patil, founder and sole proprietor of Sai Tex Ltd., a job-work powerloom unit in Ichhalkaranji, Maharashtra, faces an existential decision in April 2026. His ten Rifa Electronic Rapier looms — purchased in 2010 for ₹1.2 crore (US$144,600) — are now 15 years old, operating at 300 RPM against the 1,200 RPM of modern air-jet looms that competitors have adopted. Three converging forces — a 40 per cent collapse in fabric exports following US tariffs, a doubling of electricity costs, and accelerating migrant labour attrition to upgraded mills — have pushed Sai Tex from a monthly profit of ₹468,000 in 2023 to a monthly loss of ₹24,000 in 2025. Patil must choose among three options: (A) a ₹4.35 crore "Green Technical Pivot" involving twelve new air-jet looms, a rooftop solar plant, and a shift to the sale-purchase business model — requiring his family bungalow as collateral; (B) a ₹40–50 lakh retrofit of existing machines to buy two to three years of survival; or (C) liquidation and exit. The case provides detailed financial, production, and technology data across seven exhibits, enabling quantitative analysis of each option. It is designed for courses in entrepreneurship, operations strategy, MSME management, and family business, and is suitable for MBA, executive education, and doctoral-level instruction. The case is accompanied by seven exhibits and 64 endnotes.

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After reading and analyzing this case study, students will be able to do the following:

1. Evaluate strategic options under resource constraints — Apply decision-analysis frameworks to compare mutually exclusive investment alternatives (full modernization, incremental retrofit, and exit) using the financial and operational data provided in the case exhibits.

2. Analyze the dynamics of technology-driven competitive decline — Identify how the Red Queen Effect operates in a traditional manufacturing cluster, where firms must continuously invest in technology merely to maintain their competitive position.

3. Assess the interaction between business model choice and competitive positioning — Compare the job-work model (low risk, low margin, no pricing power) with the sale-purchase model (higher margin, inventory risk, market access requirements) and evaluate the conditions under which a business model pivot is warranted.

4. Apply the Theory of Constraints to identify and prioritize operational bottlenecks — Use Goldratt's five focusing steps to determine whether Sai Tex's binding constraint is technology, labour, market access, or capital — and how the answer changes the optimal strategic response.

5. Examine the role of socio-emotional wealth (SEW) in entrepreneurial decision-making — Analyze how non-financial considerations — family legacy, worker welfare, community identity, and the founder's sense of social obligation — shape strategic choices in ways that diverge from purely financial optimization.

## ASSIGNMENT QUESTIONS

1. Using the data in Exhibits 1, 2, and 3, construct a break-even analysis for Sai Tex under the current job-work model. At what monthly production volume (in metres) does Sai Tex break even at the 2025 cost structure? What job-work rate (paisa per pick) would Sai Tex need to break even at its current average production volume?

2. Evaluate the three strategic options (Exhibit 4) using a decision matrix that weighs financial viability, operational risk, stakeholder impact, and strategic positioning. Which option would you recommend, and under what assumptions does your recommendation hold?

3. How does the Red Queen Effect explain Sai Tex's competitive decline? Identify the specific mechanisms through which technological stagnation has created a self-reinforcing cycle of decline for the firm.

4. Apply the Theory of Constraints to Sai Tex's operations. What is the binding constraint — technology, labour, market access, or capital? How does identifying the correct constraint change the recommended course of action?

5. Patil's father instilled in him the belief that "if you have the means to give someone a livelihood, you have the duty to do it." How does this socio-emotional wealth orientation affect Patil's strategic calculus? Should non-financial considerations change the recommended option? Why or why not?

## ANALYSIS

### 1. Break-Even Analysis (Assignment Question 1)

Data extraction from exhibits:

From Exhibit 7, at 45,000 metres production (near-peak single-shift capacity):
- Total production expenses (excluding interest): ₹287,900/month
- Bank interest: ₹120,000/month
- **Total monthly cost: ₹407,900**
- Selling price per metre: ₹7.68
- Cost per metre (production only): ₹6.40
- Gross margin per metre: ₹1.28

From Exhibit 1 (2025 averages):
- Total monthly expenses: ₹510,000
- Average monthly revenue: ₹486,000 (from "Financial Performance Stats")
- Average realized rate: ₹8.07/metre (from Exhibit 3 full-year average)

Break-even volume calculation:

Monthly fixed costs must be estimated by separating fixed and variable components. From the case data:

| Cost Component | Nature | Monthly Amount (₹) |
|:---------------|:-------|-------------------:|
| Bank interest | Fixed | 120,000 |
| Spare parts & mill store | Semi-fixed | 12,000 |
| Transport & hamali | Variable | 5,000 |
| Other expenses | Fixed | 5,000 |
| Power bill | Semi-variable | 65,000 |
| Labour (base payroll) | Fixed (in short run) | 200,900 |

Approximate fixed costs: ₹120,000 + ₹12,000 + ₹5,000 + ₹200,900 = ₹337,900/month

Variable cost per metre (power + transport, scaled): approximately ₹1.56/metre

At the 2025 average rate of ₹8.07/metre:
- Contribution margin per metre = ₹8.07 − ₹1.56 = ₹6.51/metre
- **Break-even volume = ₹337,900 ÷ ₹6.51 ≈ 51,900 metres/month**

This is critical: Sai Tex's peak single-shift production in 2025 was only 45,000 metres (January and August). The break-even volume exceeds the unit's maximum observed output. At the current cost structure and rate, Sai Tex cannot break even under any achievable production scenario.

Break-even rate calculation:

At the 2025 average production of ~37,800 metres/month (454,000 ÷ 12):
- Total cost = ₹337,900 + (₹1.56 × 37,800) = ₹337,900 + ₹58,968 = ₹396,868
- Required rate per metre = ₹396,868 ÷ 37,800 = ₹10.50/metre
- Required pick rate (at average 47 picks/cm) = approximately 22 paisa/pick

The 2023 rate was 25 paisa/pick. The 2025 rate averaged 17.5 paisa. Sai Tex would need a rate recovery of approximately 26 per cent from 2025 levels — a recovery that the case provides no evidence to expect, given the structural oversupply from diverted exports.

Key teaching point: The break-even analysis demonstrates that Sai Tex's problem is not merely cyclical (a temporary rate decline) but structural (the cost base has permanently outgrown the revenue capacity of the current technology). This is the quantitative foundation for the Red Queen argument.

### 2. Strategic Option Evaluation (Assignment Question 2)

Students should construct a decision matrix using the data from Exhibit 4. A sample framework:

| Criterion (Weight) | Option A: Green Pivot | Option B: Hybrid Lean | Option C: Exit |
|:--------------------|:---------------------:|:---------------------:|:--------------:|
| Financial viability (30%) | Medium-High: Transformative upside (₹10–15L/mo net profit) but extreme downside risk (₹5.55 cr debt at 14%) | Low: Does not reach break-even; slows the bleed but does not stop it | Medium: Stops losses immediately; ₹1 cr residual debt remains |
| Operational risk (25%) | High: Assumes full capacity from day one, labour returns, market holds, subsidy disbursed on time | Low: Proven technology; incremental change | None: Operations cease |
| Stakeholder impact (25%) | Positive if successful: retains and attracts workers; transforms community standing | Neutral-Negative: workers continue to leave; slow decline | Highly negative: 12+ permanent workers displaced; community loss |
| Strategic positioning (20%) | Strong: Matches competitors; enables sale-purchase pivot; energy independence | Weak: Remains in commodity trap with obsolete technology | None: Exits the industry |

Expected student debate:

Students will likely split into three camps:

- **Option A advocates** will argue that the break-even analysis (Q1) proves that incremental measures cannot save Sai Tex. The only path to viability is a step-change in capacity and business model. The MAHA-TUFS subsidy (up to 25%) and solar energy independence reduce the effective risk. Two competitors in the same Park have already succeeded with this path.

- **Option B advocates** will argue that Option A's assumptions are heroic: full capacity from day one, stable domestic market, timely subsidy disbursement, and labour returning to a unit that is still under construction. Option B preserves optionality — it buys 2–3 years during which market conditions may improve, subsidy access may become easier, or Patil may accumulate savings toward a future upgrade without risking the family home.

- **Option C advocates** (likely a minority) will argue that the rational economic decision is to cut losses. The ₹20 lakh resale value against ₹1.2 crore debt means Patil is already deeply underwater. Adding ₹4.35 crore of new debt on a 14% interest rate in a declining market is a gamble, not a strategy. Preserving the family's remaining assets is the responsible choice.

Instructor guidance: Push students to articulate the assumptions underlying each option. Option A's attractiveness depends entirely on four assumptions holding simultaneously: (1) domestic fabric market holds, (2) MAHA-TUFS subsidy is disbursed on time, (3) air-jet looms run at capacity from day one, and (4) migrant workers return. If any one fails, the interest on ₹5.55 crore at 14% consumes the family home. The case is deliberately designed so that no option is clearly dominant — the "right" answer depends on the student's risk tolerance, discount rate, and weighting of non-financial factors.

### 3. The Red Queen Effect (Assignment Question 3)

The Red Queen Effect (Barnett and Hansen, 1996) describes a competitive dynamic in which firms must continuously adapt merely to maintain their relative position. The case title directly invokes this metaphor. Students should identify the following mechanisms:

The self-reinforcing cycle of decline:

Technological stagnation (300 RPM vs. 1,200 RPM)
        ↓
Lower throughput per machine (100 m/day vs. 400 m/day)
        ↓
Higher per-unit fixed costs (fixed costs spread over fewer metres)
        ↓
Inability to offer competitive wages
        ↓
Migrant labour attrition to upgraded mills
        ↓
Further capacity reduction (50% utilization)
        ↓
Even higher per-unit costs → even lower margins
        ↓
Inability to invest in upgrades → back to start


Cluster-level dynamics: The case describes how Ichhalkaranji's historical strength — flexibility and dense local networks — has become a liability. The same network that once allowed a weaver to find a new customer within days now allows workers to find a better employer within days. The cluster's competitive advantage has shifted from flexibility to technology, and units that have not upgraded are being selected out.

The tariff shock as an accelerant, not a cause: Students should recognize that the US tariff (August 2024) did not create Sai Tex's problem — it accelerated a pre-existing decline. The technology gap existed before the tariff. The tariff's effect was to flood the domestic market with surplus fabric from export-oriented units, compressing job-work rates below break-even. This is a classic example of how external shocks disproportionately harm firms that are already competitively weakened.

Key teaching point: The Red Queen Effect explains why Option B (retrofit) is strategically insufficient even if it is financially safer. Retrofitting 15-year-old machines does not change Sai Tex's position on the technology curve — it merely slows the rate of decline. The competitors who have already upgraded will continue to attract workers, capture clients, and drive down rates. Option B is running faster on the same treadmill; Option A is switching to a faster treadmill.

### 4. Theory of Constraints Analysis (Assignment Question 4)

Goldratt's Theory of Constraints (TOC) asks: What is the one constraint that limits the system's throughput? Students should apply the five focusing steps:

Step 1: Identify the constraint.

Students will debate which of four candidates is the binding constraint:

| Candidate Constraint | Evidence For | Evidence Against |
|:---------------------|:-------------|:-----------------|
| Technology | 300 RPM vs. 1,200 RPM; 100 m/day vs. 400 m/day; 15-year-old machines | Even current machines are running at only 50% capacity — the technology is not fully utilized |
| Labour | 50% capacity utilization; workers leaving for upgraded mills; 10% wage premium at competitors | Labour is leaving because competitors with air-jet looms can afford to pay approximately 10% higher wages — Sai Tex's margins on older machines cannot match this. The labour drain is a symptom of the underlying economic gap, not the root cause |
| Market access | Job-work rate below break-even; no pricing power; export market collapsed 40% | Under the sale-purchase model, market access changes entirely — this constraint is model-dependent |
| Capital | ₹1.2 cr existing debt; 14% interest rate; bank requires bungalow as collateral; no formal financial records | MAHA-TUFS subsidy could offset 25%; capital is available if collateral is pledged |

Step 2: Decide how to exploit the constraint.

If technology is the binding constraint: maximize throughput on existing machines (run all 10 looms, not 5). But this requires labour — which is leaving because competitors with newer machines generate higher margins and can offer approximately 10% higher wages. Sai Tex's older machines cannot generate sufficient margin to match these wages, creating an interactive constraint where technology limits wage competitiveness, which drives labour attrition.

If capital is the binding constraint: the MAHA-TUFS subsidy, the solar plant (eliminating the electricity bill), and the sale-purchase model (capturing full margin) are all mechanisms to relax the capital constraint.

Step 3: Subordinate everything else to the constraint.

This is where the analysis becomes most productive. If students identify technology as the root constraint (with labour as a derived constraint), then all other decisions — business model, energy, workforce — should be subordinated to resolving the technology gap. This points toward Option A.

If students identify capital as the root constraint, then the priority is to find the lowest-capital path to viability — which points toward Option B as a bridge, or toward creative financing (phased upgrade, leasing, cooperative investment) that the case does not explicitly offer but that students may propose.

Key teaching point: The TOC analysis reveals that Sai Tex's constraints are nested and interactive. Technology drives labour attrition, which drives capacity underutilization, which drives per-unit cost inflation, which drives the inability to accumulate capital for upgrades. Breaking the cycle requires addressing the root constraint (technology), not the symptoms (labour, costs). This is why Option B — which addresses symptoms — is strategically insufficient.

### 5. Socio-Emotional Wealth and Non-Financial Decision Criteria (Assignment Question 5)

The SEW framework (Gómez-Mejía et al., 2007; Berrone et al., 2012) identifies five dimensions of non-financial value that family firm owners seek to preserve:

| SEW Dimension | Manifestation in Sai Tex |
|:--------------|:-------------------------|
| Family control and influence | Patil is sole proprietor; all decisions are his. Option A requires pledging the family bungalow — transferring control to the bank. |
| Family members' identification with the firm | "The mill was not merely a business but an extension of Patil's identity and social obligations." |
| Binding social ties | 12 permanent employees; Shivmani Mishra has been with the firm since founding; Patil supports local schools. |
| Emotional attachment | Father's legacy: "If you have the means to give someone a livelihood, you have the duty to do it." |
| Renewal of family bonds through dynastic succession | Not explicitly addressed — Patil is first-generation. But the bungalow represents intergenerational family wealth. |

How SEW changes the calculus:

A purely financial analysis might favour Option C (exit) — it stops the bleeding and preserves remaining family assets. But SEW theory predicts that family firm owners will accept greater financial risk to preserve non-financial value. Patil's identification with the firm, his binding social ties to workers, and his father's legacy of social obligation all create a strong SEW-preservation motive that makes Option C psychologically and socially unacceptable — even if it is financially rational.

This creates a tension: SEW preservation pushes Patil toward Option A (the only option that preserves the firm and its social role), but Option A also poses the greatest threat to SEW if it fails (loss of the family bungalow, the ultimate symbol of family wealth and stability).

Expected student debate:

- Some students will argue that SEW considerations are a form of cognitive bias — Patil is letting emotional attachment cloud his judgment. The "duty to provide livelihoods" is admirable but not a sound basis for risking the family home.
- Others will argue that SEW is not irrational — it reflects a different utility function. Patil's social capital, community standing, and personal identity are real assets that have economic value (e.g., worker loyalty, supplier trust, cooperative membership). Destroying them through exit has costs that a financial model does not capture.
- The most sophisticated students will argue that the relevant question is not "should SEW matter?" but "how much risk is SEW worth?" — and that the answer depends on the probability-weighted outcomes of Option A.

Key teaching point: The SEW framework explains why Patil's decision is genuinely difficult — not because he lacks information, but because his financial and non-financial objectives are in tension. This is the central pedagogical insight of the case: real-world strategic decisions in MSMEs and family firms are never purely financial.

### PESTEL Analysis (Supplementary Framework)

Instructors may optionally use a PESTEL analysis to structure the industry context discussion:

| Factor | Impact on Sai Tex |
|:-------|:------------------|
| Political | MAHA-TUFS subsidy (up to 25%); export penalty notice (₹6 lakh fine); government modernization incentives |
| Economic | US tariffs → 40% export decline → domestic oversupply → rate compression; 14% interest rates; yarn price inflation (20–47%) |
| Social | Migrant labour dynamics (UP/Bihar workers as rational economic actors); community obligations; first-generation entrepreneur identity |
| Technological | Rapier (300 RPM) vs. air-jet (1,200 RPM); 4× throughput gap; energy efficiency differential; infrastructure requirements (AC plant + compressors) |
| Environmental | Rooftop solar opportunity (₹50 lakh); electricity cost doubling (₹3.50 → ₹6.50/unit); energy independence as competitive advantage |
| Legal | Export obligation notice; cooperative park regulations; labour laws for migrant workers |

## WHAT HAPPENED

As of April 2026, Patil had not yet made a final decision. He was in active discussions with his bank regarding the MAHA-TUFS subsidy application for the full modernization (Option A). The bank had indicated willingness to finance the upgrade but required the family bungalow as additional collateral. Patil's response to the bank manager's question — "Are we processing the MAHA-TUFS subsidy application for the full modernization, or are we just renewing the existing cash credit limit?" — was: "We are applying for MAHA-TUFS subsidy. Without subsidy there will be huge financial burden."

This response suggests that Patil was leaning toward Option A but recognized that the subsidy was a precondition for its viability. The case was written during this decision window — the outcome remained uncertain at the time of publication.

Several contextual developments were relevant:

- Two competitors in the Pride Co-operative Textile Park — M.F. Rathi Textiles and P.A. Patil Textiles — had already completed air-jet upgrades and were reportedly capturing Sai Tex's former clients.
- The domestic fabric market remained oversupplied due to diverted exports, with no recovery in job-work rates visible as of early 2026.
- The China+1 sourcing strategy that some industry observers had predicted would benefit Indian textile MSMEs had, as of early 2026, produced no discernible positive impact on units like Sai Tex.
- Patil had not been aware of the MAHA-TUFS subsidy until the case researcher informed him — highlighting the information asymmetry that small-unit owners face in accessing government support programs.

### EXHIBIT TN-4: THEORY OF CONSTRAINTS — BINDING CONSTRAINT IDENTIFICATION

| Candidate | Evidence For | Evidence Against | Verdict |
|:----------|:-------------|:-----------------|:--------|
| Technology | 300 vs 1,200 RPM; 4× throughput gap | Current machines at only 50% utilization | Root cause — drives all downstream constraints |
| Labour | 50% utilization; workers leaving for ~10% higher wages at upgraded mills | Labour attrition is a consequence of the wage gap created by lower margins on older machines | Derived constraint (symptom of technology gap) |
| Market access | Job-work rate below break-even; no pricing power | Sale-purchase model changes market access entirely | Model-dependent constraint |
| Capital | ₹1.2 cr debt; 14% rate; bungalow as collateral | MAHA-TUFS subsidy could offset 25%; capital available if collateral pledged | Binding but relaxable |

**Instructor's recommended answer**: Technology is the root constraint. Labour, market, and capital constraints are all downstream effects. Breaking the cycle requires addressing the root (technology upgrade), not the symptoms (wage increases, cost-cutting, or market diversification within the current technology base).

*Source: Goldratt's Theory of Constraints (2004) applied to case data.*
