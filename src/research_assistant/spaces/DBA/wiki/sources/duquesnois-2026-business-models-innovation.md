## Key Arguments

### Part A: Business Models, Entrepreneurship, and Innovation (N1)

#### Business Model Definitions

The deck presents multiple BM frameworks:

- **Casadesus-Masanell and Ricart (2010)**: a business model refers to a company's underlying logic, its mode of operation, and the way it creates value for its stakeholders. Strategy refers to the choice of the business model through which the company will compete in the market. [N1, p.8]

- **Teece (2010)**: a business model describes how an organization operates. Strategy describes how to compete and protect that operational logic against competitors. [N1, p.8]

- **GRP Business Model** (GRP Lab): an alternative French framework for business model analysis, applied through case studies in the wine industry (Clos Manou, Etablissements Thunevin). [N1, pp.9-27]

- **Business Model Canvas** (Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2009): introduced as a standard framework. [N1, p.6]

#### Strategic Innovation

- Strategic innovation "creates economic growth, even in slow, flat or shrinking markets" and particularly in mature non-high-tech industries (Moingeon & Lehmann-Ortega, 2006). [N1, p.29]

- "Look for a virgin competitive space… If it has always been people from outside the sector that reinvent it, it must have a reason" (Hamel, 2000). [N1, p.29]

- "Concentrate yourself on alternatives, not being interested by your clients anymore but only by your non clients" (Kim and Mauborgne, 2005). [N1, p.29]

- Strategic innovation involves a radical modification of both the value proposition and the architecture of value — a "breakthrough strategy" (Lehmann-Ortega and Moingeon, 2004). [N1, p.35]

#### Blue Ocean Strategy

Presented as an example of strategic innovation. Key principles: reconstruct market boundaries, focus on the big picture, reach beyond existing demand. Assumptions: market boundaries are not given and can be changed; designed to create uncontested market space; competition is made irrelevant. [N1, pp.31-32]

The value curve tool (Kim and Mauborgne, 2005) is presented for creativity and competitive positioning analysis. [N1, pp.39-41]

#### Value Architecture

The deck discusses rethinking the value chain: for each step, asking how the firm proceeds vs. competitors, what advantages exist, and how to do differently. IKEA's value architecture is used as an example of radical architecture innovation (kit packaging, common components, lower-cost production). [N1, pp.43-46]

#### Academic Literature on Business Models

The deck surveys Long Range Planning Vol. 43, Issues 2-3 (2010), identified as a special issue on Business Models (Rank 2 FNEGE 2022). Key papers listed include Baden-Fuller & Morgan, Teece, Casadesus-Masanell & Ricart, Zott & Amit, Demil & Lecocq, McGrath, Chesbrough, Doz & Kosonen, Yunus/Moingeon/Lehmann-Ortega, and others. [N1, pp.49-54]

### Part B: Introduction to Innovation (N2)

#### Franck Aggeri's Critical Perspective on Innovation

The deck presents Aggeri's 2023 book "Innovation, but what for? An essay on an economic, social and managerial myth" (Editions du Seuil, 256 pages). Aggeri is Professor of Management at Mines Paris-PSL. [N2, p.6]

Key arguments from Aggeri as presented in the deck:

1. **Three phases of innovation culture** (post-WWII, Western-centric):
   - Phase 1: linear model of technological innovation driven by R&D departments and public authorities. [N2, p.7]
   - Phase 2 (1980s): open innovation through clusters, and disruptive innovation through start-ups. [N2, p.7]
   - Phase 3: metamorphosis with new players (associations, NGOs, social economy) and new contexts — green innovations, frugal innovations, 'lowtech'. [N2, p.7]

2. **Critique of pro-innovation bias**: innovation culture imposes a catch-all, consensual notion compatible with liberal conceptions of permanent transformation. Negative impacts are deferred in time. Managerial innovations can undermine autonomy of work collectives. [N2, pp.8-9]

3. **Proposed exits**: change criteria for assessing innovation; agree on new legal definitions of liability to commit innovators to long-term consequences. Innovators taking responsibility is inseparable from democratic exercise of innovation governance. [N2, p.10]

4. **Path of sobriety**: fight against obsolescence, social and collaborative innovations, development of second-hand, repair, circularity, frugal use of resources. Not degrowth, but post-growth models requiring new conceptions of innovation. [N2, p.12]

Reference to Jacques Ellul (1912-1994), professor of law at University of Bordeaux, theologian: produced work on the relationship between humans and technology, advocated for technology that does not enslave humans. [N2, p.9]

#### Comments from Prof. Gilles Garel (CNAM)

The deck includes a reading note by Gilles Garel (Revue Française de Gestion, Vol. 49, No. 311, July-August 2023, pp.95-101). Garel notes that Aggeri did not draw on historian David Edgerton (2006) and his iconoclastic approach to innovation, Robert Gordon (2017), or Edmund Phelps (2013). The Easterlin paradox (1974) is cited: wealth of a society does not correlate with well-being of individuals. In France, GDP per capita doubled since 1970s, but well-being indicators stagnated. [N2, pp.13-14]

#### Schumpeterian Innovation

- Joseph Alois Schumpeter recognized as founder (1928) of work on innovation. [N2, p.17]
- Term "innovation" first used by Walker (1888) and Andrews (1890) to designate "something unusual." [N2, p.17]
- Schumpeter (1934) defined innovation as "the commercialisation of any new combination of new materials and components, the introduction of new processes, the opening of new markets or the introduction of a new organisational form." [N2, p.17]
- Five types of innovation (Schumpeter): (a) new products/services; (b) new production method/process; (c) new market; (d) new source of raw materials; (e) new organization of industry leading to monopolistic position. [N2, p.18]
- Tidd et al. (2008) proposed incremental vs. radical innovation typology based on Schumpeter. [N2, p.18]
- Schumpeter's definition only accounts for radical innovation (non-existence in the past). This leads to "creative destruction" — "a permanent process of creation, destruction and restructuring of economic activities." [N2, p.19]

#### Neo-Schumpeterian Vision

- Nelson and Winter (1982): "An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change" — importance of organisational learning in maintaining competitiveness. [N2, p.22]
- Giovanni Dosi (1988): processes of innovation, diffusion of technologies, learning mechanisms. [N2, p.22]
- Paul Romer (1990): role of knowledge and innovation in sustainability of economic growth. [N2, p.23]
- Aghion and Howitt (1992): stimulating economic growth through innovation and entrepreneurship. Aghion received "Nobel Prize" 2025 in Economics. [N2, p.23]
- Lucas Bretschger (1999): endogenous growth incorporating environmental constraints. [N2, p.23]
- Five pillars of neo-Schumpeterian vision: (i) innovation/entrepreneurship as drivers; (ii) creative destruction; (iii) technology and endogenous growth; (iv) lifecycle of products/industries; (v) positive impacts beyond the innovating company. [N2, p.25]
- Schumpeterian paradox (Munier, 2013): Schumpeter Mark I (1934) says small firms play major role; Schumpeter Mark II (1942) says innovation initiated by large firms. [N2, p.26]

#### Oslo Manual (OECD, 2005)

Innovation defined as "either the bringing to market of a new or significantly improved product (good or service), or a process, or a new marketing, organisational or business strategy." Four types: (a) product, (b) process, (c) organisational, (d) marketing. [N2, p.21]

#### Other Innovation Theories

- Christensen, Anthony and Roth (2007): three theories — disruptive innovation, resources/processes/values, evolution of the value chain. [N2, p.27]
- Barney's RBV (1991, 2001): complements innovation for sustainable competitive advantage. [N2, p.28]
- Freeman's stakeholder theory (1984, 2021). [N2, p.28]
- Environmental innovation: Schot and Kanger (2016) — urgent need to review innovation process for environmental transition. [N2, p.30]

#### Managerial and Organizational Innovation

- Damanpour and Evans (1984): Organizational Innovation classified as part of Managerial Innovations (Kimberly, 1981). [N2, p.31]
- Birkinshaw et al. (2008): managerial innovation believed to be main factor explaining organizational performance. [N2, p.31]
- Coopetition as organizational innovation (Granata and Duquesnois, 2020). [N2, p.31]

### Part C: Introduction to Bibliometric Analysis (N2)

The deck introduces two software tools for bibliometric analysis:
- **Bibliometrix** (R language, RStudio): for studying the performance of academic literature linked to a research object — quantitative approach. [N2, p.36]
- **VOSviewer** (Java): for identifying collaboration networks between researchers and major trends in keywords — qualitative approach. [N2, p.36]

Workflow presented: access Scopus (or Web of Science) via university ENT → build keyword queries using Boolean operators (AND, OR, inverted commas for compound words) → refine scope (year range, field: Business Management and Accounting) → export CSV → import into Bibliometrix or VOSviewer. [N2, pp.37-38]

Bibliometrix capabilities shown: main information overview, annual scientific production, relevant sources (journals), most frequent keywords, word clouds, country collaboration world map. [N2, pp.39-41]

VOSviewer capabilities shown: bibliographic coupling (analysis of Present/main articles), co-citation (analysis of Past/theoretical foundations), co-occurrence of author keywords. Node size indicates exploration intensity; distance between keywords indicates co-occurrence frequency. [N2, p.42]

## Methodology

Lecture slides with case studies (Clos Manou wine case, Fat Bastard wine case, IKEA). No primary research methodology — this is pedagogical material.

## Relevance to Research

The lecture content covers business models, entrepreneurship, and innovation theory — topics that are tangential to the user's thesis on organizational restructuring and GenAI (punctuated equilibrium). The Schumpeterian creative destruction concept has potential links to technology-driven organizational change, but the connection is not direct.

The most directly useful content is **Part C: bibliometric analysis using Bibliometrix and VOSviewer** [N2, pp.35-42], which provides a concrete tool-based approach for the literature review methodology. The deck teaches how to use these tools to identify key papers, map collaboration networks, and visualize keyword co-occurrence — all applicable to building the thesis literature review.
