# Critical Review of Article

## Title of Article

**Why Are Companies Offshoring Innovation? The Emerging Global Race for Talent**

**Authors:** Arie Y. Lewin, Silvia Massini, and Carine Peeters

**Journal:** *Journal of International Business Studies*, Vol. 40, No. 6, pp. 901–925, 2009.

---

## 1. Phenomena Being Studied

The paper investigates the phenomenon of multinational enterprises (MNEs) offshoring core innovation activities (research and development, product development, and engineering services) to emerging markets, principally India and China. This contradicts the prevailing theoretical assumption that innovation must remain geographically centralised at headquarters to preserve tacit knowledge transfer and intellectual property protection. The phenomenon under scrutiny is not the well-documented offshoring of manufacturing or routine back-office processes, but rather the relocation of high-value, knowledge-intensive innovation work that constitutes the core of firm competitive advantage. The authors frame this as a qualitatively distinct strategic behaviour: a shift from Home-Base-Exploiting (HBE) and Home-Base-Augmenting (HBA) R&D internationalisation towards what they term Home-Base-Replacing (HBR) innovation, wherein firms relocate core innovation capabilities offshore not merely to supplement domestic capacity but to replace it. The central contention is that this phenomenon is driven primarily by a global race for qualified scientific and engineering talent rather than by conventional labour cost arbitrage.

---

## 2. Level(s) of Analysis

The paper adopts a multi-level analytical framework grounded in co-evolutionary theory.

At the **macro level (Environmental)**, it examines how environmental constraints, specifically the declining domestic supply of science and engineering (S&E) talent in the United States and the 2003 H1B visa quota reduction from 195,000 to 65,000, create exogenous pressures that incentivise firms to seek qualified personnel offshore.

At the **meso level (Organisational/Firm)**, it investigates organisational path dependence and learning, wherein prior offshoring implementations reduce uncertainty and transaction costs, creating cumulative experience that enables firms to pursue progressively more complex offshoring initiatives.

At the **micro level (Project)**, it analyses individual offshoring project decisions as reflections of managerial intentionality (MI), capturing the strategic objectives (talent access, cost savings, speed to market, competitive pressure) that managers articulate for specific implementation decisions.

This multi-level design is a notable strength, as it avoids both macro determinism (assuming environmental conditions fully determine behaviour) and micro contingency (assuming decisions reflect pure managerial preference), recognising that outcomes emerge from the interaction among environmental pressures, managerial strategy, and organisational learning.

---

## 3. Research Questions

The paper addresses four core research questions, though they are partially implicit rather than formally stated:

(1) Is access to qualified technical talent, rather than labour cost savings, the primary driver of innovation offshoring decisions? (2) Do environmental factors (domestic talent supply constraints, visa policy shocks) independently influence the probability of offshoring product development? (3) Does organisational learning through prior offshoring experience play a causal role in future innovation offshoring decisions (path dependence)? (4) Did the 2003 H1B visa quota reduction structurally alter firm strategies regarding innovation offshoring?


### Quality of Research Questions (6Cs Analysis)

**Curiosity:** The questions demonstrate exceptional intellectual curiosity by challenging the fundamental assumption in international business theory that innovation must remain geographically centralised for competitive advantage. The counterintuitive proposition that talent access, not cost, drives innovation offshoring represents a genuinely novel line of inquiry.

**Confirmability:** The questions are highly confirmable. Each is operationalised with testable variables. Binary logit regression with clearly defined dependent (PD offshoring = 1/0) and independent variables allows effect sizes to directly compare competing drivers, enabling falsification.

**Cost:** The paper engages economic cost factors substantively. The counterintuitive finding that labour cost importance *reduces* PD offshoring probability reveals that cost-driven and knowledge-seeking offshoring represent distinct strategies with fundamentally different drivers and outcomes.

**Cupidity:** This dimension is very high. The entire analytical framework is grounded in competitive self-interest and strategic organisational motivations, treating talent as a strategic asset rather than a commodity input. The research questions are firmly rooted in understanding how firms pursue competitive advantage.

**Conformability:** Strong. The variables are reliably measurable through established survey research methods, and binary logit regression is methodologically appropriate for the analytical structure involving a dichotomous dependent variable.

**Contribution:** The questions promise substantial theoretical contribution by redefining location advantages in international business theory from cost-based to talent-based advantages, which amounts to a fundamental update to Dunning's OLI framework.

### Formulation in Terms of Variables

The dependent variable is binary: whether a given offshoring project involves product development (PD = 1) or other functions (PD = 0). Key independent variables include: (a) an environmental measure of U.S. talent supply (logarithm of S&E degree production multiplied by H1B visa quota availability); (b) seven managerial intentionality measures on 1–5 Likert scales (qualified personnel access, labour cost savings, other cost savings, speed to market, growth acceleration, global strategy, competitive pressure); (c) a path dependence measure (count of prior PD implementations); and (d) control variables (firm size, industry and country dummies, delivery model type, post-2003 period indicator).

### Critical Gap

A notable gap in the research questions is their insufficient attention to interaction effects. The questions do not explore whether environmental pressure (talent shortage) drives offshoring regardless of managerial strategy, or whether environmental pressures only trigger offshoring when managers actively pursue talent access. Testing such interactions would sharpen causal understanding and clarify whether environmental determinism or managerial agency drives outcomes.

## 4. Critical Influences

Three foundational works shaped the paper's analytical framework:

### 4.1 Dunning's Eclectic (OLI) Paradigm

Dunning (1993) argued that successful foreign direct investment requires the simultaneous presence of Ownership advantages (firm-specific strengths), Location advantages (country-specific attractiveness), and Internalisation advantages (benefits from internal versus contractual governance). The paper makes a key contribution by redefining location advantages for innovation: rather than traditional cost-based advantages (cheap labour, natural resources), the relevant location advantage for innovation offshoring is access to qualified technical talent. This fundamentally updates international business theory by suggesting that 21st-century competitive advantage depends on attracting geographically dispersed talent rather than protecting centralised innovation.

### 4.2 Maskell, Pedersen, and Petersen (2007): Learning Paths to Offshore Outsourcing

Maskell and colleagues documented an evolutionary progression in offshore R&D activity. Firms begin at Stage 1 with cost-driven offshoring of routine tasks, progress to Stage 2 through capability-building as they develop processes and managerial confidence for coordinating offshore work, and advance to Stage 3 where they pursue knowledge-seeking offshoring of innovation work motivated by access to specialised talent. The paper validates this progression empirically through path dependence: each prior PD implementation increases future PD offshoring probability by 21%, demonstrating that experience reduces uncertainty and enables firms to migrate from cost-driven to capability-driven strategies. The counterintuitive finding that labour cost importance reduces PD offshoring probability aligns with Maskell's framework. Firms pursuing cost arbitrage remain at Stages 1–2, while firms at Stage 3 pursuing innovation are motivated primarily by talent access.

### 4.3 Freeman (2005): The Great Doubling

Freeman documented the crisis in U.S. science and engineering workforce production, showing that despite 43% GDP growth between 1995 and 2006, the number of U.S. nationals earning S&E doctorates declined by 7%. Simultaneously, reliance on H1B visas for accessing global talent intensified. The 2003 policy shock, when the H1B cap dropped from 195,000 to 65,000, created an exogenous environmental constraint on domestic talent access. The paper operationalises Freeman's findings as a quantitative environmental variable and tests whether the visa policy shock had structural impact on firm strategies. The finding that post-2003 patterns differ markedly (talent access becomes a strong predictor while cost remains statistically weak) demonstrates that policy shocks have real causal consequences for organisational strategy.

These three influences integrate powerfully: Freeman provides the *when* (environmental pressures create urgency), Maskell provides the *how* (learning pathways enable strategic progression), and Dunning provides the *why* (economic logic makes talent-based location strategy rational for competitive advantage).

## 5. Literature Review

The paper integrates several streams of literature.

The **R&D Internationalisation Literature** historically assumed innovation should remain geographically concentrated at headquarters for tacit knowledge transfer and IP protection (Patel & Pavitt, 1991). By the 1990s–2000s, scholars documented MNEs establishing R&D centres abroad, distinguishing Home-Base-Exploiting (HBE) R&D that adapts products for local markets (Kuemmerle, 1999) from Home-Base-Augmenting (HBA) R&D that accesses new knowledge (Cantwell, 1991; Florida, 1997). The paper introduces Home-Base-Replacing (HBR) innovation, defined as moving core innovation offshore to replace, not supplement, domestic capacity. This represents a strategic transformation in how firms organise competitive advantage globally.

**Transaction Cost Economics**, drawing on Williamson (1975, 1985), recognises that complex, knowledge-intensive work should be kept in-house because transaction costs are high. Such activities are difficult to specify contractually, monitor effectively, or enforce legally. The empirical finding that captive centres (retaining ownership and control) have 153% higher probability for PD offshoring compared with outsourcing arrangements validates this logic.

**Co-evolutionary Theory** (Lewin & Volberda, 1999) argues that systems evolve through the interaction of external environment, organisational strategy and capabilities, and organisational experience and path dependence. The multi-level framework embodies this co-evolutionary logic.

**Knowledge Spillover and Location Theory**, building on Feinberg and Gupta (2004) and Dunning (2000), acknowledges that knowledge spillover opportunities and the availability of supporting industries influence location decisions for R&D FDI.

The literature review is thorough in scope but could have engaged more deeply with the knowledge management literature on how tacit innovation knowledge transfers across geographic distances. This gap limits the paper's practical implications for managers implementing offshore innovation.

## 6. Theoretical Foundation

The paper is grounded in three complementary theoretical traditions.

**Dunning's Eclectic (OLI) Paradigm** provides the overarching framework for understanding why firms invest abroad, with the paper's key theoretical move being the redefinition of location advantages from cost-based to talent-based for innovation activities.

**Transaction Cost Economics (Williamson, 1975, 1985)** explains governance mode choices. It accounts for why firms prefer captive (in-house) arrangements over outsourcing for innovation offshoring, given the high asset specificity, uncertainty, and coordination complexity inherent in knowledge-intensive work.

**Co-evolutionary Theory (Lewin & Volberda, 1999)** provides the dynamic, multi-level analytical lens, arguing that offshoring decisions emerge from the joint interaction of environmental pressures (macro), managerial intentionality (micro), and organisational path dependence (meso), rather than from any single level of analysis in isolation.

These theoretical foundations are well-integrated and mutually reinforcing. The OLI framework explains the economic rationale, transaction cost economics explains the governance structure, and co-evolutionary theory explains the dynamic process through which firms arrive at innovation offshoring decisions.

## 7. Conceptual/Research Model

The research model specifies:

**Probability(PD Offshoring) = f(Talent Supply, Strategic Drivers, Path Dependence, Controls)**

The **dependent variable** is binary, capturing whether a project involves product development offshoring (1) or other functions (0).

The **environmental variable** is U.S. talent supply, operationalised as the logarithm of S&E degree production multiplied by H1B visa quota availability. A decrease in this composite measure is hypothesised to increase PD offshoring probability.

The **managerial intentionality variables** (1–5 Likert scales) include access to qualified personnel (hypothesised positive effect on PD offshoring), labour cost savings (hypothesised weaker or negative effect for PD vs. other functions), other cost savings, speed to market, growth acceleration, global strategy alignment, and competitive pressure.

The **path dependence variable** is the count of prior PD implementations by the firm, hypothesised to increase future PD offshoring probability through organisational learning.

**Control variables** include firm size (log employees), industry dummies, country dummies (India, China), delivery model (captive vs. outsourced), and a post-2003 period indicator.

The **estimation method** is binary logit regression, with odds ratios (e^β) indicating how each unit change in an independent variable affects the odds of PD offshoring. For example, if qualified personnel importance yields OR = 1.67, each 1-point Likert increase raises PD offshoring odds by 67%.

The model is parsimonious and well-specified for the research questions, though the absence of interaction terms between environmental and managerial variables represents a missed opportunity for deeper causal understanding.

## 8. Research Strategy

**Epistemology:** The paper adopts a positivist epistemological stance, seeking to identify generalisable causal relationships through hypothesis testing with quantitative data. The authors formulate testable propositions derived from theory and subject them to empirical verification using statistical methods.

**Research Approach:** The approach is deductive, moving from theoretical frameworks (OLI framework, transaction cost economics, co-evolutionary theory) to specific hypotheses about the determinants of innovation offshoring, which are then tested against empirical data.

**Analytical Techniques:** Binary logit regression is employed as the primary analytical technique, appropriate for the dichotomous dependent variable (PD offshoring = 1/0). The paper reports both coefficient estimates and odds ratios, and tests models across the full sample as well as pre-2003 and post-2003 subsamples to assess the structural impact of the H1B visa policy shock. McKelvey and Zavoina R², McFadden's R², and likelihood ratio chi-square tests are reported as model fit statistics.

---

## 9. Research Method

**Sample Assessment:** The study uses original survey data from the Offshoring Research Network (ORN) project, comprising 880 offshoring implementations from 253 U.S. companies spanning 1990–2006. The use of individual projects rather than firms as the unit of analysis is a notable methodological strength. It enables detection that the same companies offshore different functions for different reasons, revealing strategic heterogeneity that firm-level aggregation would obscure. The 16-year timespan captures innovation offshoring's emergence and acceleration. However, survivorship bias is present: the sample includes only firms that actually offshored, excluding firms that never offshored, which limits the ability to assess why some companies avoid offshoring entirely.

**Measures Assessment:** The environmental variable (S&E talent supply × H1B quota) is a creative operationalisation, though it conflates two distinct policy mechanisms. The managerial intentionality variables use self-reported Likert scales, which are subject to post-hoc rationalisation. Managers may retrospectively reframe decisions to align with subsequent outcomes. Stronger measurement would employ revealed preference data (actual budget allocations, wage premiums paid, time-to-hire metrics) rather than stated preference.

**Analytical Techniques Assessment:** Binary logit regression is methodologically appropriate for the binary dependent variable. Several limitations constrain causal inference, however. On reverse causality in path dependence: does prior experience cause future offshoring by reducing costs and risks, or do naturally offshoring-capable firms simply accumulate more experience? On endogeneity: unobserved factors (CEO personality, organisational culture, industry-specific shocks) may drive both offshoring decisions and correlate with measured variables, inflating effect sizes. On weak causal identification: causality is inferred from correlation without strong identification strategies. Instrumental variables, natural experiments (comparing firms just above and below H1B visa quota thresholds), or difference-in-differences estimation would strengthen causal claims.

## 10. Contributions

**Stated/Implied Contributions**

First, the paper redefines location advantages. It establishes empirically that access to qualified technical talent is a primary location advantage for 21st-century innovation, fundamentally different from traditional resource-based or cost-based advantages. This reframes competitive advantage theory: if innovation depends on geographically dispersed talent, sustained advantage comes from building organisational capabilities to attract, retain, and coordinate global expertise.

Second, it distinguishes knowledge-seeking from cost-driven offshoring. The negative coefficient on labour cost importance demonstrates that these are distinct strategies with different drivers, evolutionary paths, and presumably different outcomes. This challenges dominant narratives that assume cost arbitrage drives globalisation.

Third, it provides empirical validation of path dependence. The paper offers among the first large-scale empirical tests of path dependence in offshoring contexts, demonstrating that organisational learning through experience reduces transaction costs and uncertainty, enabling firms to pursue progressively more complex offshoring initiatives (21% increase per prior PD implementation).

Fourth, it introduces Home-Base-Replacing (HBR) innovation. The paper extends R&D internationalisation literature by conceptualising a new category of offshore innovation that goes beyond adaptation (HBE) or augmentation (HBA) to actual replacement of domestic innovation capacity.

Fifth, it demonstrates policy impact. The finding that the 2003 H1B visa cut structurally altered firm strategies provides empirical evidence that immigration policy has real causal consequences for corporate innovation strategy.

**Assessment of Whether Contributions Are Reflected in Modelling/Data**

Contributions 1–3 and 5 are well-supported by the empirical modelling and data analyses. The talent access variable is statistically strong and substantively large (OR ≈ 1.67), the labour cost variable shows the predicted negative effect, path dependence is validated with a 21% increase per prior implementation, and the pre/post-2003 subsample analysis demonstrates structural change. Contribution 4 (HBR innovation) is more conceptual and is not directly tested in the empirical model. It is inferred from the pattern of results rather than operationalised as a distinct variable.

---

## 11. Learnings

**Phenomenon-Related Learnings**

Innovation offshoring represents a qualitatively different phenomenon from manufacturing or service offshoring. The finding that talent access, not labour cost, drives innovation offshoring reveals that the globalisation of innovation operates under a distinct logic from the globalisation of production. The 2003 H1B visa policy shock serves as a powerful illustration of how government policy can have unintended consequences for corporate strategy. Restricting talent immigration did not retain innovation domestically but rather accelerated its relocation offshore. The concept of a "global race for talent" reframes competitive advantage in knowledge-intensive industries: firms compete not merely on products, markets, or costs, but on their ability to attract and coordinate geographically dispersed human capital.

**Methodological Learnings**

Project-level analysis reveals strategic heterogeneity invisible at firm-level aggregation. The same firm may offshore different functions for entirely different reasons, and aggregating to the firm level would mask this variation. This is a valuable lesson for research design in international business. The use of a co-evolutionary multi-level framework (macro, meso, micro) provides a richer understanding of complex organisational phenomena than single-level analyses, though it introduces challenges in testing interaction effects across levels. The limitations of self-reported Likert scales for measuring strategic intent highlight the importance of triangulating stated preferences with revealed preference data in future research on managerial decision-making. The paper demonstrates both the power and the limits of cross-sectional survey data for causal inference. While the large sample and multi-level design are strengths, the absence of instrumental variables or natural experiment designs constrains the ability to make strong causal claims.
