## 1. SYNOPSIS

In April 2026, Avinash Kisanrao Patil, founder and sole proprietor of Sai Tex Ltd., a small powerloom unit in Ichhalkaranji, Maharashtra — India's "Manchester of Maharashtra" — faced an existential decision. His ten Rifa Electronic Rapier looms, purchased in 2010 for ₹1.2 crore (US$144,600),¹ were running at half capacity. A United States tariff shock had collapsed job-work rates from 25 to 17–18 paisa per pick, migrant workers were leaving for competitors with newer air-jet looms, and energy costs had doubled. The unit was losing ₹24,000 every month (case Exhibit 1). Patil had three options: a ₹4.35 crore green technical pivot to air-jet looms with solar power and a sale-purchase business model, a ₹40–50 lakh hybrid retrofit of existing machines, or asset liquidation and exit. The bank would finance the full modernization — but only if Patil pledged his family's bungalow as additional collateral. Eighteen families depended on Sai Tex for their livelihoods. The case presents a classic MSME survival dilemma in which financial analysis alone is insufficient — students must weigh competitive dynamics, family stakes, and socio-emotional wealth to arrive at a recommendation.

---

## 2. LEARNING OBJECTIVES

This case is set in the Indian textile MSME sector, a market undergoing rapid technological disruption while thousands of family-owned firms struggle to adapt. It presents students with the task of evaluating strategic alternatives for a resource-constrained firm facing simultaneous competitive, financial, and social pressures.

After reading and analysing this case, students will be able to do the following:

1. **Analyse** how macro-level disruptions (trade policy, energy inflation, labour mobility) cascade into a micro-level MSME crisis, using PESTEL and Porter's Five Forces frameworks.
2. **Evaluate** three strategic alternatives (full modernization, hybrid retrofit, and exit) by conducting break-even analysis, debt-service calculations, and scenario modelling under resource constraints.
3. **Assess** the role of non-financial factors — specifically socio-emotional wealth (SEW), founder identity, and worker welfare — in shaping strategic decisions that deviate from purely financial optimization.
4. **Design** a phased implementation plan that sequences investments to manage risk, rather than treating modernization as a single binary decision.

These objectives guide students through a learning path progressing from environmental analysis (LO1) to financial evaluation (LO2), then to stakeholder integration (LO3), and finally to actionable strategic design (LO4).

## 3. POSITION IN COURSE

The case is designed for graduate and executive management programmes, particularly for courses in strategic management, operations management, entrepreneurship, and family business. It is also suitable for undergraduate capstone courses in business strategy.

| Course | Module | Positioning |
|:-------|:-------|:------------|
| Strategic Management (MBA/PGDM) | Competitive dynamics, technology disruption, MSME strategy | Second half of course, after students have been introduced to PESTEL, Porter's Five Forces, and basic financial analysis |
| Operations Management | Capacity planning, throughput analysis, Theory of Constraints | Mid-course, when students can apply quantitative tools to real-world operational data |
| Entrepreneurship / Family Business | Socio-emotional wealth, founder identity, growth vs. survival | Late in course, as an integrative case requiring synthesis across financial and non-financial dimensions |
| Executive Education | MSME competitiveness, cluster development, Make in India policy | Standalone session or paired with a policy case |

**Prerequisites**: Before beginning the case, students should have a theoretical understanding of industry analysis frameworks (PESTEL, Five Forces), basic financial analysis (break-even, debt service), and capacity utilization concepts. Familiarity with the socio-emotional wealth construct is helpful but not required — the case can serve as the introduction to SEW.

**Expected Outcomes**: The case works well as a capstone integrative exercise that requires students to synthesize across strategy, finance, operations, and organizational behaviour. Students should leave the session understanding that MSME strategy cannot be reduced to financial optimization alone, and that implementation sequencing is itself a strategic variable.

**Session Duration**: A 75-minute session is recommended. A 90-minute session allows for the optional role-play extension described in the teaching plan.

## 6. ASSIGNMENT QUESTIONS

The following questions should be distributed to students before class. They are designed to guide pre-class preparation and structure the in-class discussion. Each question maps to one or more learning objectives.

1. **Break-Even Analysis (LO2)**: Using the data in Exhibits 1, 2, and 7 of the case, calculate Sai Tex's monthly break-even volume under the current job-work model. Is break-even achievable given the constraints described in the case? What does this tell you about the viability of the status quo?

2. **Environmental Analysis (LO1)**: Conduct a PESTEL analysis of the Ichhalkaranji textile cluster as of April 2026. Which factor represents the most significant threat to Sai Tex, and which represents the most significant opportunity?

3. **Option Evaluation (LO2, LO3)**: Evaluate the three strategic options available to Patil (case Exhibit 4). For each option, assess the financial viability, the risk to the family, and the impact on the eighteen families who depend on Sai Tex. Which option would you recommend, and why?

4. **Implementation Design (LO4)**: Patil's family wants him to "turn this into a profitable business without adding more stress and debt." Is this possible? Design a phased implementation plan that addresses the family's concerns while moving toward long-term viability.

```

        ↓
Export orders collapse (−40%)
        ↓
Large export-oriented units divert surplus → domestic market
        ↓
Domestic oversupply → job-work rates fall (25 → 17–18 paisa/pick)
        ↓
Revenue per metre collapses (₹15 → ₹6.40–₹9.00)
        ↓
Revenue below break-even (₹486,000 vs. ₹510,000 expenses)
        ↓
Cannot match competitor wages → workers leave for air-jet units
        ↓
Capacity drops to 50% → fixed costs spread over fewer metres
        ↓
Per-unit cost rises → losses deepen → THE RED QUEEN TRAP
```

*Instructor note: Ask students to identify where Patil could have broken the chain. Common responses: "He should have upgraded earlier" (hindsight bias — useful teaching moment); "He should have diversified customers" (limited agency under job-work model); "He should have built financial reserves" (valid, but MSME cash constraints are real). The most insightful response is that the chain has no single breakable link for a firm of Sai Tex's size — the vulnerability is structural, not managerial.*

The analysis below presents planned discussion questions and expectations of how students may respond. The case has not yet been classroom-tested; instructors should treat the anticipated student responses as indicative rather than definitive.²

### Question 1: Break-Even and the Viability of the Status Quo

**Assignment Question**: Using the data in Exhibits 1, 2, and 7 of the case, calculate Sai Tex's monthly break-even volume under the current job-work model. Is break-even achievable given the constraints described in the case? What does this tell you about the viability of the status quo?

**Theoretical Framework**: Theory of Constraints (Goldratt & Cox, 1984). The break-even calculation reveals that the binding constraint is not production capacity but the structural economics of the job-work model itself. When the contribution margin is negative or negligible, no volume of production can cover fixed costs — the system is fundamentally broken, not merely underperforming.

**Analysis**:

The break-even calculation requires students to separate fixed and variable costs from the case exhibits.

From case Exhibit 7, at a production volume of 45,000 metres (near-peak single-shift capacity), total production expenses excluding interest are ₹287,900, the cost per metre is ₹6.40, and the selling price per metre is ₹7.68. The monthly interest burden is ₹120,000 (case Exhibit 1). Students should recognize that interest is a fixed cost that must be covered regardless of production volume.

**Simplified Break-Even Model** (see Exhibit TN-3 for full scenario analysis):

| Component | Value | Source |
|:----------|:------|:-------|
| Revenue per metre (range) | ₹6.40–₹9.00 | Case Exhibit 3 (varies by pick quality and month) |
| Revenue per metre (annual average) | ₹8.04 | Case Exhibit 3: ₹3,650,860 ÷ 454,000 metres |
| Variable cost per metre (labour + energy) | ₹8.66 | Case Exhibit 2: labour ₹6.66/m + energy ₹2.00/m |
| Contribution margin per metre (at average) | **(₹0.62)** | Revenue ₹8.04 minus variable cost ₹8.66 |
| Contribution margin per metre (at best rate) | ₹0.34 | Revenue ₹9.00 minus variable cost ₹8.66 |
| Fixed costs per month | ~₹155,000 | Interest ₹120,000 + manager ₹20,000 + spare parts ₹12,000 + other ₹3,000 |
| Break-even volume (at ₹0.34 margin) | ~455,900 metres/month | Fixed costs ÷ contribution margin |
| Break-even volume (at negative margin) | **Not achievable** | Negative contribution margin |

The maximum single-shift capacity of the unit is approximately 45,000 metres per month (case Exhibit 3, January and August peaks). Even at the most favourable contribution margin (₹0.34, corresponding to the highest-quality picks at ₹9.00/metre), break-even requires approximately 455,900 metres — more than ten times the unit's maximum observed output. At the average selling price of ₹8.04 per metre, the contribution margin turns negative: every additional metre produced increases the loss. The status quo is structurally unviable.

**Expected Student Responses**:

- *Quantitatively oriented students* will build the break-even model and arrive at the conclusion that the current model cannot reach break-even under any realistic scenario. They may debate whether certain costs (e.g., the manager's salary of ₹20,000) are truly fixed or could be reduced. This is a productive discussion — it highlights the difference between accounting flexibility and operational reality in a small firm where the manager is also the coordinator, purchaser, and delivery supervisor (case Exhibit 6).

- *Qualitatively oriented students* may skip the calculation and argue directly that "the numbers don't work." The instructor should push these students to show their work: "How do you know the numbers don't work? Show me the break-even point." This reinforces the discipline of grounding strategic arguments in data.

- *A common error* is to use the 2023 figures (when the unit was profitable at ₹15/metre and ₹603,000 monthly profit) as the baseline. The instructor should redirect: "The question asks about the current model. What has changed between 2023 and 2025 that makes the old math irrelevant?" This leads naturally into the PESTEL discussion.

**Key Teaching Takeaway**: The status quo is not an option. This is not a case about *whether* to change, but about *which* change to pursue. Establishing this early prevents students from spending class time defending inaction.

### Question 2: PESTEL Analysis of the Ichhalkaranji Cluster

**Assignment Question**: Conduct a PESTEL analysis of the Ichhalkaranji textile cluster as of April 2026. Which factor represents the most significant threat to Sai Tex, and which represents the most significant opportunity?

**Theoretical Framework**: PESTEL analysis (macro-environmental scanning) combined with Porter's Five Forces (industry-level competitive dynamics). The value of this question lies not in the checklist exercise but in identifying interactions between factors and distinguishing between structural vulnerabilities and triggering events.

**Analysis**:

| Factor | Key Forces | Impact on Sai Tex |
|:-------|:-----------|:------------------|
| **Political** | US tariff on Indian fabric exports (August 2024); Maharashtra Textile Policy 2023–28; MAHA-TUFS subsidy (up to 25% capital subsidy); export compliance notice (₹10 lakh export obligation or ₹6 lakh penalty) | The tariff is the proximate cause of the crisis — it triggered the domestic oversupply that collapsed job-work rates. The MAHA-TUFS subsidy is a potential lifeline, but Patil lacks the formal financial records to apply easily. The export penalty adds a further compliance burden. |
| **Economic** | 14% interest rates for MSMEs; energy cost inflation (₹3.50 to ₹6.50 per unit, +86%); yarn price inflation (20–47% across grades); domestic oversupply from diverted exports | Every economic factor is moving against Sai Tex. The interest rate makes large capital investments extremely risky for a firm with no formal balance sheet. |
| **Social** | Migrant labour dependency (workforce entirely from UP and Bihar); worker mobility toward modern mills offering 10% higher wages; founder's social obligations to eighteen families | Labour is the binding constraint. Workers are rational economic actors who compare machinery across units. The social dimension — socio-emotional wealth — prevents Patil from making a purely financial exit decision. |
| **Technological** | Air-jet looms (1,200 RPM) replacing rapier looms (300 RPM); 4× production speed differential (case Exhibit 5); solar energy viability (₹50 lakh investment to eliminate ₹65,000–₹90,000/month energy bill) | Technology is both the threat (competitors upgrading) and the opportunity (Option A). The 4× speed differential is the core of the Red Queen dynamic. |
| **Environmental** | Rising energy costs (₹45,000 to ₹90,000/month, +100%); solar energy as a hedge; no direct pollution compliance burden (Sai Tex does not process or dye fabric) | Solar investment has the clearest and most immediate return of any single investment — it eliminates the energy cost that case Exhibit 7 shows consuming ₹65,000/month even at reduced production. |
| **Legal** | MAHA-TUFS eligibility requirements; cooperative park regulations; lack of formal financial documentation; export compliance notice | Patil's informal record-keeping is a barrier to accessing the very subsidies designed to help MSMEs like Sai Tex. He was unaware of MAHA-TUFS until the case researcher informed him. |

**Porter's Five Forces Overlay** (see Exhibit TN-2):

Students should be encouraged to layer the Five Forces analysis onto the PESTEL to show how macro factors translate into industry-level competitive pressure. The key insight is that the tariff shock (Political) did not create the competitive vulnerability — it activated a latent technology gap (Technological) that had been building for years. Before August 2024, Patil was surviving with the same old machines. The tariff made the technology gap lethal.

**Expected Student Responses**:

- Students will likely identify the *technological factor* as the most significant threat, citing the 4× speed differential between Rifa and air-jet looms (case Exhibit 5). This is correct but incomplete. The instructor should probe: "Is technology the threat, or is it the tariff that made the technology gap lethal? Patil survived for fifteen years with the same machines."

- On the opportunity side, students will split between the *MAHA-TUFS subsidy* (political) and *solar energy* (environmental). Both are valid. The instructor can use this to illustrate that PESTEL factors interact: the subsidy reduces the financial barrier to the technological upgrade, while solar reduces the economic burden of energy costs.

- Some students may argue that the *social factor* (labour mobility) is the most significant threat because it is the binding constraint on current operations — production swung from 45,000 metres in August to 25,000 metres in October 2025 (case Exhibit 3), driven by worker departures. This is a sophisticated response that connects PESTEL to the Theory of Constraints.

- A common oversight is the *legal factor* — students often miss the export compliance notice (₹10 lakh export obligation or ₹6 lakh penalty) described in the case. The instructor can use this to illustrate how regulatory burdens compound financial distress for MSMEs.

**Key Teaching Takeaway**: PESTEL is not a checklist exercise. The value lies in identifying interactions between factors and distinguishing between structural vulnerabilities (technology gap, building since 2010) and triggering events (tariff shock, August 2024).

### Question 3: Evaluating the Three Strategic Options

**Assignment Question**: Evaluate the three strategic options available to Patil (case Exhibit 4). For each option, assess the financial viability, the risk to the family, and the impact on the eighteen families who depend on Sai Tex. Which option would you recommend, and why?

**Theoretical Frameworks**: Red Queen Effect (Barnett & Hansen, 1996); Socio-Emotional Wealth (Gómez-Mejía et al., 2007; Berrone, Cruz, & Gómez-Mejía, 2012); Theory of Constraints (Goldratt & Cox, 1984).

This is the central question of the case and should consume the largest portion of class time. The analysis integrates financial modelling, competitive positioning, and socio-emotional wealth considerations.

**Option A: The Green Technical Pivot (₹4.35 Crore New Investment)**

*Financial Analysis*:

| Metric | Current State (Case Data) | Option A (Projected) |
|:-------|:--------------------------|:--------------------|
| Machines | 10 Rifa rapier looms, 300 RPM | 12 air-jet looms, 1,200 RPM (case Exhibit 5) |
| Daily capacity | 1,000 m (10 × 100 m/day) | 4,800 m (12 × 400 m/day) (case Exhibit 5) |
| Monthly capacity (single shift) | ~45,000 m (peak observed, case Exhibit 3) | 144,000 m |
| Revenue model | Job-work at 17–18 paisa/pick | Sale-purchase (full product margin) |
| Revenue per metre | ₹6.40–₹9.00 (case Exhibit 3) | ₹15–₹35 (depending on fabric grade) |
| Monthly energy cost | ₹65,000–₹90,000 (case Exhibits 2, 7) | Near zero (with ₹50 lakh solar plant) |
| Total debt | ₹1.2 Cr (existing) | ₹5.55 Cr (₹4.35 Cr new + ₹1.2 Cr existing) (case Exhibit 4) |
| Monthly interest (at 14%) | ₹120,000 (case Exhibit 1) | ~₹647,500 |
| MAHA-TUFS subsidy potential | N/A | Up to ₹1.09 Cr (25% of ₹4.35 Cr) |

The financial case for Option A is strong on paper. At even moderate utilization rates (31%), the unit would cover its debt service (see Exhibit TN-3). At 80% utilization, monthly revenue could exceed ₹20 lakh. However, students should note three critical assumptions: (a) the sale-purchase model generates the projected margins — and Patil has never purchased yarn directly in fifteen years of weaving; (b) Patil can develop yarn procurement and sales capabilities he has never possessed; and (c) the MAHA-TUFS subsidy is approved and disbursed on time — and Patil lacks the formal financial records that the application requires.

*Risk to Family*: The bank requires the family bungalow as additional collateral. If the modernization fails, the family loses both the business and their home. The wife's statement — "We want this to work, Avinash. But not at the cost of everything else" — signals that the family's risk tolerance has a hard limit.

*Worker Impact*: Positive. Air-jet looms require fewer workers per machine (3 weavers + 1 technician per shift for 12 machines, per case Exhibit 5) but the four-fold increase in throughput means the unit would likely need to hire additional staff. Modern machines also attract workers — the case notes that workers leave Sai Tex specifically because competitors have newer equipment offering 10% higher wages.

**Option B: The Hybrid Lean Model (₹40–50 Lakh)**

*Financial Analysis*: Retrofitting with inverter motors reduces the energy bill by an estimated 20–30% (saving ₹13,000–₹27,000 per month) but does not increase throughput. The unit would still produce 100 metres per machine per day on 300 RPM rapier looms (case Exhibit 5). Total monthly savings: approximately ₹13,000–₹27,000. This does not close the ₹24,000/month loss gap (case Exhibit 1), let alone generate profit. Total debt would rise to ₹1.6–1.7 crore, with monthly interest of ₹187,000–₹198,000 (case Exhibit 4).

*Risk to Family*: Moderate. The capital requirement is one-tenth of Option A. No bungalow collateral required. But the option does not solve the fundamental problem — it buys two to three years of runway at best.

*Worker Impact*: Neutral. Jobs are preserved in the short term but remain precarious. The labour attraction problem persists — workers will continue to leave for units with newer technology.

**Option C: Asset Liquidation and Exit**

*Financial Analysis*: Resale of ten Rifa looms at ₹2 lakh each yields ₹20 lakh against ₹1.2 crore in outstanding debt — a shortfall of ₹1 crore that must be covered from other assets or negotiated with the bank (case Exhibit 4). Monthly losses stop immediately.

*Risk to Family*: Low in financial terms (bleeding stops), but high in identity terms. The family wants the business to succeed. Exit means acknowledging failure after sixteen years.

*Worker Impact*: Severe. Eighteen families lose their primary income source in a labour market with limited alternatives. For workers like Shivmani Mishra, who has been with the unit since its founding, the loss is both economic and relational.

**Socio-Emotional Wealth (SEW) Overlay**:

The SEW framework (Gómez-Mejía et al., 2007; Berrone, Cruz, & Gómez-Mejía, 2012) identifies five dimensions of non-financial value that founder-led firms seek to preserve (see Exhibit TN-1 for the full mapping):

- **Family control and influence**: Patil is sole proprietor; pledging the bungalow transfers risk to the entire family and introduces the bank as a de facto stakeholder with veto power over the family's housing.
- **Identification with the firm**: Patil continues operating despite monthly losses; the mill is his identity. Exit is experienced as a loss of self.
- **Binding social ties**: Shivmani Mishra's tenure since founding; community school support when profitable. Workers are named individuals with histories (case Exhibit 6), not interchangeable inputs.
- **Emotional attachment**: Father's voice: "A weaver doesn't just make cloth; he weaves the lives of his workers together." Emotional attachment biases toward preservation even when financial logic favours exit.
- **Renewal through succession**: Family wants the business to succeed — "without adding more stress and debt." No clear succession plan; the next generation's willingness to continue is uncertain.

**Expected Student Responses**:

In the opening poll, students typically split roughly evenly across Options A, B, and C, with a small minority proposing a hybrid or fourth option. This even split is pedagogically valuable — it demonstrates that reasonable people disagree, which is the hallmark of a well-constructed case dilemma.

- *Students advocating for Option A* will emphasize the 4× production advantage and the break-even achievability at 31% utilization (moderate scenario). The instructor should challenge: "The math works on a spreadsheet. But Patil has never purchased yarn, never sold fabric directly, and has no formal financial records. How does he execute a ₹4.35 crore transformation when he cannot produce a balance sheet for the bank?"

- *Students advocating for Option B* will argue for risk management and incremental learning. The instructor should challenge: "You are recommending that Patil spend ₹40–50 lakh to save ₹13,000–₹27,000 per month. That is a 15–38 month payback on an investment that does not solve the fundamental problem. Is this prudent risk management or expensive procrastination?"

- *Students advocating for Option C* will cite the family's risk tolerance and the ₹1 crore debt shortfall. The instructor should challenge: "You are recommending that eighteen families lose their income so that Patil can preserve his bungalow. Is that a defensible trade-off? And what does Patil do next — he is a first-generation weaver in a textile town with no other skills."

- The most productive classroom moments typically emerge when students realize that their recommendation depends on *which stakeholder they prioritize*. This is the SEW insight: financial rationality and socio-emotional preservation pull in different directions, and there is no formula for resolving the tension.

**Key Teaching Takeaway**: The "right" answer depends on the decision-maker's values, risk tolerance, and time horizon. Strategy is not optimization — it is choice under uncertainty with competing objectives.

### Question 4: Designing a Phased Implementation Plan

**Assignment Question**: Patil's family wants him to "turn this into a profitable business without adding more stress and debt." Is this possible? Design a phased implementation plan that addresses the family's concerns while moving toward long-term viability.

**Theoretical Framework**: Real options reasoning applied to MSME strategy. Each phase is an investment that purchases information and preserves optionality for the next decision. The phased approach converts a single high-stakes bet into a sequence of smaller, reversible commitments.

**Analysis**:

This question pushes students beyond option selection toward implementation design. The key insight is that the three options presented in the case are not necessarily mutually exclusive or all-at-once propositions. A phased approach can manage risk while preserving optionality (see Exhibit TN-4 for the visual framework).

**A Possible Phased Plan**:

| Phase | Timeline | Action | Investment | Risk Mitigation |
|:------|:---------|:-------|:-----------|:----------------|
| **Phase 1: Stop the Bleeding** | Months 1–6 | Install rooftop solar to eliminate ₹65,000–₹90,000 monthly energy bill (case Exhibits 2, 7). Apply for MAHA-TUFS subsidy. Begin formalizing financial records (prerequisite for subsidy and future bank financing). | ₹50 lakh (solar) | Immediate ROI; no bungalow collateral needed; payback in ~6–8 months from energy savings alone |
| **Phase 2: Test the Model** | Months 4–12 | Pilot the sale-purchase model on 2–3 existing Rifa looms. Use ₹25 lakh working capital to purchase yarn and sell fabric directly. Learn procurement, inventory management, and direct sales. | ₹25 lakh (working capital) | Small-scale test; if it fails, losses are contained to ₹25 lakh; if it succeeds, provides proof of concept for bank |
| **Phase 3: Selective Upgrade** | Months 12–24 | If Phase 2 succeeds and MAHA-TUFS subsidy is approved, replace 4–6 Rifa looms with air-jet looms. Sell remaining Rifas (₹2 lakh each, per case Exhibit 4) to partially offset cost. Retain some Rifa capacity as fallback. | ₹1.2–₹1.8 Cr (partial upgrade) | Smaller debt than full Option A; proven business model from Phase 2; subsidy reduces net cost by up to 25% |
| **Phase 4: Full Transformation** | Months 24–36 | If Phase 3 succeeds, complete the upgrade to 12 air-jet looms. Full transition to sale-purchase model. | Remaining ₹1.8–₹2.4 Cr | By this point, the unit has a track record, formal financials, and demonstrated capability in the sale-purchase model |

**Decision Gates**: Each phase includes a decision gate. At the end of Phase 2, Patil has information he does not have today: Can he source yarn competitively? Can he find direct buyers? What margins does the sale-purchase model actually generate? This information fundamentally changes the Phase 3 decision. If Phase 2 fails, Patil can exit having invested ₹75 lakh (solar + working capital) rather than ₹4.35 crore — a dramatically different risk profile.

**Expected Student Responses**:

- Students often propose phased plans that are either *too cautious* (essentially Option B with extra steps) or *too aggressive* (Option A relabelled as "phases" but with the same total risk). The instructor should push for genuine optionality: "At the end of Phase 2, what information will Patil have that he does not have today? How does that information change the Phase 3 decision?"

- Strong students will recognize that the phased approach has a *cost: delay*. While Patil is piloting the sale-purchase model on old Rifa looms, competitors M.F. Rathi Textiles and P.A. Patil Textiles are capturing market share with their air-jet machines. The Red Queen does not pause while you experiment. This tension between "move fast" and "manage risk" is the strategic crux of the case.

- Some students may propose a *fourth option* not in the case: forming a cooperative with other small units in the Pride Park to jointly invest in shared air-jet capacity. This is a creative response that connects to Schmitz's (1995) work on collective efficiency in industrial clusters. The instructor should acknowledge it and ask: "What coordination challenges would this create? Why hasn't it happened already in a Park that already has a cooperative structure?"

- Executive education participants often focus on the *formalization of financial records* as the critical first step — recognizing that Patil's inability to produce a balance sheet is a barrier to every option, including subsidies, bank financing, and even informed decision-making. This is a practical insight that MBA students sometimes overlook.

**Key Teaching Takeaway**: Implementation sequencing is itself a strategic choice. The order in which investments are made determines what information is available for subsequent decisions. Strategy is not just "what to do" but "in what order."

## 10. WHAT HAPPENED

When presented with the bank manager's question — "Are we processing the MAHA-TUFS subsidy application for the full modernization, or are we just renewing the existing cash credit limit?" — Patil chose to apply for the MAHA-TUFS subsidy and pursue the full modernization, effectively Option A. His reasoning: "Without subsidy there will be huge financial burden" — but without modernization, there was no future at all.³

As of April 2026, the subsidy application was in process. The outcome remained uncertain. Patil had not been aware of the MAHA-TUFS scheme until it was brought to his attention during the preparation of this case — a detail that underscores the information asymmetry facing MSMEs in India's industrial clusters.

**Discussion Prompt for Instructors**: After revealing the epilogue, ask: "Was Patil's decision rational? Under what conditions would it succeed? Under what conditions would it fail? If you were advising Patil, what milestones would you set for the first twelve months to determine whether to continue or reverse course?"

This prompt is particularly effective after the phased-plan discussion, as students can compare Patil's actual all-or-nothing decision with the phased approach they designed. The contrast often generates the richest discussion of the session.

## EXHIBIT TN-2: PORTER'S FIVE FORCES — ICHHALKARANJI TEXTILE CLUSTER (APRIL 2026)

| Force | Intensity | Key Drivers (from Case) | Impact on Sai Tex |
|:------|:----------|:------------------------|:------------------|
| **Industry Rivalry** | Very High | Export diversion has flooded the domestic market; competitors in the same Park (M.F. Rathi, P.A. Patil Textiles) have upgraded to air-jet looms; Ichhalkaranji houses 65,000 machines across thousands of units | Sai Tex is losing clients to faster, lower-cost competitors operating within walking distance |
| **Buyer Power** | High | Under job-work, merchants and exporters set the per-pick rate; weavers have no pricing power; buyers can switch between weavers with zero switching cost; rates fell from 25 to 17–18 paisa/pick (case Exhibit 2) | Job-work rates have fallen below break-even; Patil is a price-taker in a buyer's market |
| **Supplier Power** | Low (job-work) / Moderate (sale-purchase) | Under job-work, yarn is supplied by the customer — no supplier risk. | **Threat of Substitutes** | High | The sale-purchase model is itself a "substitute" business model for job-work, offering higher margins but requiring capital and market knowledge; several other units are contemplating the same pivot | If Patil does not pivot, competitors who do will capture the higher-margin segments |
| **Barriers to Entry** | Low (job-work) / High (air-jet) | Anyone can enter job-work with modest capital; air-jet looms require ₹30 lakh per machine plus AC plant and compressors (case Exhibit 5) | Low barriers in job-work mean unlimited competition; high barriers in air-jet mean the upgrade is a one-way door |
