# Integrated Community-Based Internal Branding — A Holistic Approach to Internal Branding for B2B Organizations

**Atul Prashar** (Head of Marketing & Communications, Hub India Design, Ericsson India Global Services Pvt. Ltd., Gurgaon) and **Moutusy Maity** (Department of Digital and Data-Driven Marketing, Southampton Business School, University of Southampton). [p.1]

*Industrial Marketing Management*, 120 (2024), 62–77. DOI: 10.1016/j.indmarman.2024.05.006. Received 18 September 2023; revised 31 March 2024; accepted 14 May 2024. [p.1]

> **Wiki relevance**: This is QNTR course mentor Prof. Atul Prashar's own published research. It is retained as a methodological exemplar of the design he advises students to follow (narrative review to build a conceptual model, then a quantitative SEM test with mediation and moderation). ## Abstract (as published)

Purpose: the study proposes and tests the effectiveness of an integrated approach to community-based internal branding (CBIB) in a business-to-business (B2B) setting, integrating classical and contemporary views on internal branding and building community activities into the framework for co-creation of a corporate brand identity. It also examines the moderating effect of employees' personal, job, and community-related characteristics. [p.1]

Design/methodology/approach: a detailed narrative review proposes a conceptual model; a quantitative design tests the hypotheses using structural equation modeling on 400 survey responses. [p.1]

Findings: integrated CBIB is a viable approach for implementing internal branding; the model demonstrates its effectiveness in building employee brand commitment. Employee age, gender, organizational tenure, brand-community membership duration, education level, customer interaction, leadership status, and participation in brand fests moderate the proposed relationships. [p.1]

## Research Objectives

Three objectives: (1) extend and test a community-based approach to internal branding ("CBIB") that integrates organization-driven operationalizations with socialization-driven ones in a community of employee brand ambassadors in the B2B context; (2) measure the effectiveness of CBIB in building brand knowledge and commitment among brand ambassadors; (3) identify key moderating factors in the relationship between employee-driven CBIB and brand knowledge/commitment. [p.2]

## Core Construct: CBIB

CBIB is defined as "a cross-functional, organization-wide, sociological effort to co-create and maintain the brand identity by engaging employees in internal brand communities, which are not bounded by cultures and geographies." [p.5] It reconciles the classical, organization-driven, top-down view of internal branding (Fig. 1a) with the contemporary, socialization-driven, bottom-up view (Fig. 1b) into an integrated dyadic model (Fig. 2). [p.2, p.5]

CBIB is modeled as a **second-order construct** built from six first-order constructs: internal brand communication, brand-centered HR, brand-centered leadership, shared consciousness of the internal brand community, rituals and traditions of the community, and community norms. Social identity is a second-order construct built from three first-order constructs: cognitive, evaluative, and affective social identity. Brand knowledge and brand commitment are the outcome constructs. [p.7]

## Method

- Narrative (multidisciplinary) literature review on internal branding, brand communities, social identity theory, and theories of intentional action, used to derive the conceptual model and hypotheses. [p.2, p.5–7]
- Quantitative test via structural equation modeling in **IBM SPSS 18 and SPSS AMOS 26**. [p.7]
- All items measured on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). Firm size is a control variable; employees' personal, job, and community-participation characteristics are moderators. [p.7]
- Content validity assessed by one academic and one practitioner expert; initial 42-item instrument refined; inter-rater agreement Cohen's Kappa = 0.901. [p.7]
- Sample: snowball sampling of employee brand ambassadors in multinational B2B firms in the information-communication-technology and telecommunication sectors. 585 respondents approached over nine months; 400 valid responses (response rate 68.3%). 32% female, 67.75% male, 1.25% undisclosed; mean respondent age 31.11 years. Data collected August 2021 to April 2022. [p.7–8]
- Endogeneity/robustness: non-response bias tested (Armstrong & Overton extrapolation + subjective methods); common method variance tested three ways (Harman's single factor = 18.2% variance; second-order vs single-factor comparison; Cote & Buckley three-competing-models), demonstrating negligible CMV. [p.8]
- Measurement model fit: χ²/df = 2.160, CFI = 0.950, TLI = 0.942, RMSEA = 0.054; construct reliability (Cronbach α > 0.70), composite reliability > 0.70, AVE > 0.50; discriminant validity established via Fornell–Larcker. [p.8]

## Findings (Hypothesis Tests)

From the structural model (Table 5, standardized β): [p.9–10]
- **H1** (CBIB → brand knowledge): **not supported** (β = 0.011, ns).
- **H2** (CBIB → brand commitment): **not supported** (β = −0.159, ns).
- **H3** (CBIB → social identity): **supported** (β = 0.944, p < 0.001).
- **H4** (social identity → brand commitment): **supported** (β = 0.646, p < 0.05).
- **H4a**: social identity **fully mediates** CBIB → brand commitment (indirect 0.555**, direct −0.158 ns). **Supported.**
- **H5** (social identity → brand knowledge): **supported** (β = 0.764, p < 0.05).
- **H5a**: social identity **fully mediates** CBIB → brand knowledge. **Supported.**
- **H6** (brand knowledge → brand commitment): **supported** (β = 0.431, p < 0.001).
- **H6b**: social identity and brand knowledge **serially mediate** CBIB → brand commitment (indirect 0.284**). **Supported.**

Headline result: CBIB does not directly build brand knowledge or commitment; it works by **activating employees' social identity**, which in turn drives brand knowledge and commitment (full/serial mediation). [p.9–10] Mediation tested via product-of-coefficients with bias-corrected bootstrapping (2000 resamples, MacKinnon et al. 2007). [p.9]

## Moderation

Multi-group moderation (measurement invariance per Putnick & Bornstein 2016; Chen 2007), grouping by age (career-stage model, Cohen 1993: Explorers <30, Advancers 30–39, Maintainers ≥40), gender, education, tenure, leadership status, customer interaction, membership duration, and brand-fest participation. [p.10–11] Selected path-level effects: younger/lower-education employees show stronger socialization-driven paths, while higher-education/veteran/manager employees show stronger brand-knowledge-to-commitment paths; membership duration and brand-fest participation differ at group level but not at path level. [p.11, p.14]

## Alternate Models and Contribution

The paper compares five co-creation CBIB configurations — employee-driven, communications-driven, leadership-driven, HR-driven, and the integrated model — against the classical organization-driven model, using nested Δχ² tests (Tables 7a, 7b). All models are tenable and significantly different; the all-integrated model gives the strongest explanation of brand commitment. [p.7, p.11–13] A "strategy square" (Fig. 4) maps internal-branding success against the level of organizational vs employee-ambassador involvement. [p.14]

Theoretical contribution: extends Muniz & O'Guinn's (2001) brand-community concept to a community of employee brand ambassadors and empirically tests internal brand communities as a mechanism for internal branding. [p.13]

## Limitations and Future Research

- Single industry cluster (software, IT services, telecom); authors argue the large multinational firms studied are representative but recommend extending to healthcare, hospitality, and financial services. [p.15]
- 86% of the sample is from Asian/Oceanic countries (Europe 10%, USA 3%, Arab countries 1%); a more multi-cultural test is recommended. [p.15]
- Future directions: other internal-branding outcomes (organizational commitment, brand citizenship behavior, job satisfaction, turnover intention, brand performance, employee-based brand equity); antecedents of CBIB; virtual-only vs offline community comparison. [p.15]
