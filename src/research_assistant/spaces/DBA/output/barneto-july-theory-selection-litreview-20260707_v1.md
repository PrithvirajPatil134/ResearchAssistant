# Theory Selection for GenAI-Driven Organizational Restructuring: A Comparative Literature Review

## Abstract

Major technology firms restructured simultaneously between 2022 and 2026, yet the theoretical lens for interpreting these changes remains unsettled. A comparative assessment of six candidate theoretical families was conducted against three criteria: fit to the structural change dependent variable, archival firm-level operationalization precedent, and complementarity. Punctuated equilibrium emerges as the strongest primary spine because it directly models multi-domain structural change and carries established archival coding methods (Romanelli & Tushman, 1994; Girod & Whittington, 2015). Disruption theory supplies the trigger mechanism punctuated equilibrium leaves thin; institutional theory explains cross-firm convergence through mimetic pressure. Adoption models are eliminated because they address the decision to use a technology, not its structural consequences. The review recommends a multi-theory architecture for the December 2026 thesis proposal: punctuated equilibrium as spine, with disruption and institutional theory as supporting lenses.

## 1. Framing

Between 2022 and 2026, major technology firms restructured at a pace without recent precedent: headcount reductions in the hundreds of thousands, new leadership roles (Chief AI Officer, Head of AI Platform), management layers consolidated, divisions merged, and reporting structures redesigned [.kiro/.long-doc/barneto-2page-proposal-v2-20260610/assembled.md, §Background and Gap]. The phenomenon is visible and documented. Its theoretical interpretation is not settled.

A critical framing fork sits at the centre of any attempt to theorise this activity. The phenomenon can be read as an adoption question (why and how firms take up generative AI) or as a structural change question (what happened to organizational form once they did). These framings imply different literatures and different research designs. Adoption models (TAM, UTAUT, TOE) explain the decision to use a technology; they do not explain the consequences of that decision for organizational structure [data/summaries/technology_adoption_models_review.md, §6]. Structural change theories (punctuated equilibrium, institutional theory, disruption theory) address the dependent variable directly: changes in form, hierarchy, and control.

This review surveys candidate theoretical lenses and lets the literature's fit to the phenomenon indicate which best anchors the research. The lens is argued from evidence, not assumed. Disruption theory and decision theory are treated as potential supporting mechanisms, not as competitors in a winner-take-all contest [data/drafts/ide_session_2026-06-17.md, Correction 2]. The output is a recommendation for Professor Barneto's reaction, framed as earned by the comparative assessment rather than given in advance.

## 2. Candidate Lenses Surveyed

Six theoretical families offer mechanisms relevant to the phenomenon. Four receive primary assessment because they address organizational structural change directly or supply an established trigger mechanism with archival precedent. Two receive briefer treatment as candidate supporting lenses whose strength lies in explaining heterogeneity or micro-level decision processes rather than the aggregate pattern itself.

### 2.1 Punctuated Equilibrium

Punctuated equilibrium describes organizations as alternating between long convergent periods, during which incremental changes preserve deep structure, and brief reorientation episodes in which strategy, structure, power distributions, and control systems shift simultaneously (Tushman & Romanelli, 1985). Gersick (1991) formalized the mechanism: revolution occurs when deep structure itself is dismantled, not when surface routines change.

The empirical record is strong. Romanelli and Tushman (1994) coded 2+ domain shifts within compressed timeframes as reorientation across a multi-industry sample, establishing multi-domain simultaneity as the canonical operationalization method. Their coding scheme distinguishes first-order change (within existing structure) from second-order change (restructuring the structure). Meyer (1982) introduced the environmental jolt concept: an external disruption destabilizes equilibrium and may trigger reorientation. Greenwood and Hinings (1996) extended the framework to organizational archetypes, classifying shifts between archetypes as punctuation. Girod and Whittington (2015) moved beyond binary coding to proportion-based structural measurement, computing firm-level proportions of structural title change per period to distinguish incremental reconfigurations from discontinuous restructurings.
[Source: data/summaries/lit-search-thesis-foundations-v2.md, §2]

The fit to the phenomenon is direct. The pattern of stability (2010-2022 growth model in technology firms) followed by rapid concurrent restructuring (2023-2026) maps onto the convergence-reorientation alternation. Multi-domain simultaneity captures the observable reality: headcount, divisions, leadership roles, reporting lines all changing within compressed timeframes. Archival operationalization is well-established for large-N designs.

What punctuated equilibrium leaves thin is threefold. First, the trigger mechanism: the theory identifies that environmental jolts trigger reorientation but does not explain why this particular jolt (GenAI) produces structural change while earlier technology transitions (cloud computing, mobile platforms) did not produce equivalent restructuring episodes. Second, the decision micro-level: who decides to restructure, on what basis? The theory treats reorientation as an organizational-level event, not as a set of executive decisions. Third, variation across firms: the framework predicts convergence versus reorientation but struggles to explain why some firms reorient aggressively while others in the same industry do not.
[Source: data/summaries/lit-search-thesis-foundations-v2.md, §2; brain/literature-map.md, Stream 1]

**Archival precedent**: Romanelli and Tushman (1994, Academy of Management Journal, A*, 2,815 citations); Gersick (1991, Academy of Management Review, A*, 4,057 citations); Meyer (1982, Administrative Science Quarterly, A*, 3,518 citations); Girod and Whittington (2015, Organization Science, A*, 90 citations).
[Source: data/summaries/lit-search-thesis-foundations-v2.md, §2]

### 2.2 Disruption Theory

Christensen's (1997) theory of disruptive innovation argues that incumbent firms fail to respond to inferior-but-improving technologies entering from below because their existing customers and resource allocation processes screen out the new entrant until it is too late. The mechanism operates at the market/industry level: disruption is about competitive displacement, not internal structural rearrangement.
[UNSOURCED: Christensen 1997 foundational text; not in wiki]

The fit to the phenomenon is partial but instructive. Disruption theory supplies a candidate trigger mechanism: GenAI enters as a tool for automating tasks cheaper than human labor, forcing incumbents to respond or face displacement by AI-native competitors. It explains the urgency. Firms that do not restructure risk the classic incumbent trap.

The gaps are substantial. Christensen's theory addresses market dynamics and business model change, not internal organizational form. Its level of analysis is industry/firm-competitive, not intra-firm structural. Archival operationalization is limited: the theory was developed from case narratives, and coding "disruption" from SEC filings lacks methodological precedent at the standard required for a large-N archival design.
[Source: brain/literature-map.md, Stream 2]

**Archival precedent**: Limited. Primarily case-based operationalization; no established protocol for coding disruption from corporate disclosure data.

### 2.3 Institutional Theory

DiMaggio and Powell (1983) identified three mechanisms through which organizations become structurally similar: mimetic isomorphism (copying under uncertainty), coercive isomorphism (regulatory and stakeholder pressure), and normative isomorphism (professionalization). Scott (2001) consolidated these into three institutional pillars: regulative, normative, and cognitive.
[UNSOURCED: DiMaggio & Powell 1983, Scott 2001; foundational works not yet in wiki]

The fit is revealing. The observable similarity of restructuring announcements across technology firms (creation of AI-specific C-suite roles, layoffs framed as "AI efficiency," divisional merges under AI banners) is consistent with mimetic isomorphism: firms copy early movers under conditions of uncertainty about the correct structural response. Board-level and investor pressure to demonstrate "AI readiness" is consistent with coercive isomorphism.

Institutional theory explains convergence and divergence in structural responses. If mimetic pressures dominate, firms should converge toward similar structures. If different firms face different institutional environments (regulatory, normative, competitive), divergence is predicted. This is testable from archival data.

Three limitations matter. First, timing: institutional theory explains equilibrium forms and isomorphic outcomes but is weak on the timing of transitions. It predicts what forms organizations will take, not when they will take them. Second, heterogeneity: if mimetic pressures dominate, the theory struggles to explain why firms vary in restructuring depth within the same institutional field. Third, archival operationalization has limited precedent; most institutional studies use survey or qualitative methods, not corporate disclosure coding.
[Source: brain/literature-map.md, Stream 3]

**Archival precedent**: Limited for SEC-filing-based designs. Stronger in survey-based and qualitative designs.

### 2.4 Adoption and Diffusion Models

The technology adoption literature spans four generations of models (Rogers, 1962; Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990). The critical distinction for this review is between individual-level models (TAM, UTAUT) and organizational-level frameworks (TOE, DOI).
[Source: data/summaries/technology_adoption_models_review.md, §1]

TAM and UTAUT operate at the individual level: perceived usefulness and ease of use predict whether a person accepts a technology. Venkatesh et al. (2003) unified eight models into UTAUT, explaining 70% of variance in behavioral intention. These are irrelevant to firm-level structural change. They answer "will people use this?" not "what happens to organizational form after they do?"
[Source: data/summaries/technology_adoption_models_review.md, §§1-2]

TOE is organizational-level, capturing technological, organizational, and environmental contexts that influence the adoption decision. It could frame adoption intensity as the independent variable that triggers structural change. The AI-specific literature largely adapts TOE and DOI with tailored constructs: AI readiness, explainability requirements, data infrastructure as enablers.
[Source: data/summaries/technology_adoption_models_review.md, §§3,5]

The limitation is fundamental. Adoption models explain the decision to adopt, not the structural consequences of adoption. Brown and Grant (2005, 808 citations) explicitly separate governance research from adoption research. Weill and Ross (2004, 4,177 citations) define IT governance through decision rights allocation and accountability structures, positioning it as an organizational design challenge distinct from adoption. The thesis studies what happens to organizational form after firms adopt GenAI. Adoption explains the precondition; it does not explain the dependent variable.
[Source: data/summaries/technology_adoption_models_review.md, §6; data/wiki/shared/concepts/principal-agent-theory-for-ai.md, "Application to Research"]

This is the framing fork resolved. When the dependent variable is structural change (headcount, divisions, leadership roles, reporting lines), the relevant theories are those that address organizational form, not those that address the decision to use. TAM/UTAUT are eliminated as primary candidates. TOE survives as a potential framing for the independent variable (adoption intensity) but not for the dependent variable (structural change).
[Source: data/summaries/technology_adoption_models_review.md, §6]

**Archival precedent for TOE**: Moderate at organizational level but addresses adoption decisions, not structural outcomes.

### 2.5 Dynamic Capabilities

Teece, Pisano, and Shuen (1997) define dynamic capabilities as the firm-level capacity to sense, seize, and reconfigure resources in response to environmental change. The framework explains why some firms restructure effectively while others do not: firms with stronger dynamic capabilities sense the GenAI opportunity earlier, seize it through resource reallocation, and reconfigure their structures accordingly.
[UNSOURCED: Teece et al. 1997 foundational; not in wiki]

The limitation for this research is operationalization. Dynamic capabilities predict performance variance, not structural change patterns. Most studies use survey-based measures; limited archival precedent exists for coding capabilities from disclosure data. Dynamic capabilities is better positioned as a moderating explanation (why some firms reorient faster or more effectively) than as the primary lens for describing the structural change itself.
[Source: brain/literature-map.md, Stream 5]

### 2.6 Decision Theory

Simon (1947), March and Simon (1958), and Cyert and March (1963) established the behavioral theory of the firm: decision-makers operate under bounded rationality, satisfice rather than optimize, and respond to problems through local search. The attention-based view (which follows from this lineage) explains why restructuring decisions cluster in time: GenAI captures executive attention, triggering decision cascades across firms.
[UNSOURCED: Simon 1947, March & Simon 1958, Cyert & March 1963; foundational works not in wiki]

Decision theory explains the micro-level: who decides to restructure, on what information, under what cognitive constraints. It fills the "who decides" gap that punctuated equilibrium leaves open. Its limitation is operational. Coding decision processes from disclosure data is difficult; the theory is more suited to interview and case methods than to archival coding at scale.
[Source: brain/literature-map.md, Stream 6]

## 3. Comparative Assessment

Three criteria distinguish a primary theoretical spine from a supporting lens: fit to the dependent variable, archival operationalization precedent at the firm level, and complementarity with what the chosen lens leaves unexplained.

Before applying these criteria, a prior question must be resolved. The phenomenon can be framed as an adoption question (why and how firms take up GenAI) or as a structural change question (what happened to organizational form after they did). These are different dependent variables requiring different theories. Adoption frameworks (TAM, UTAUT, TOE) answer the first question. Pattern theories of organizational change (punctuated equilibrium, institutional theory) answer the second. The Barneto two-page proposal frames the research around structural outcomes: headcount shifts, divisional mergers, new governance roles, reporting-line changes [data/summaries/technology_adoption_models_review.md, §6]. The dependent variable is organizational form, not adoption behavior. This resolves the fork. TAM and UTAUT are eliminated as primary candidates because they predict individual acceptance, not organizational restructuring (Brown & Grant, 2005; Weill & Ross, 2004) [data/summaries/technology_adoption_models_review.md, §6]. TOE operates at the organizational level but still explains the decision to adopt rather than its structural consequences [data/summaries/technology_adoption_models_review.md, §6].

Table 1 summarizes the comparative assessment across all six lens families.

[Source for precedent assessments: data/summaries/lit-search-thesis-foundations-v2.md, §§2,4]

The table reveals a clear differentiation. Punctuated equilibrium is the only lens that directly models multi-domain structural change and carries established archival coding methods at the firm level. Romanelli and Tushman (1994) coded reorientation from strategy, structure, and power shifts in SEC filings. Girod and Whittington (2015) extended this with proportion-based continuous measurement [data/summaries/lit-search-thesis-foundations-v2.md, §2]. No other lens matches this combination.

The complementarity argument follows from what PE leaves unexplained. PE identifies the pattern (convergence punctuated by reorientation) but is silent on why GenAI specifically triggers reorientation while earlier technology shifts did not produce equivalent disruption. Disruption theory fills this gap: it supplies the trigger mechanism. Institutional theory fills a second gap: it explains why restructuring patterns converge across firms through mimetic pressure [data/summaries/lit-search-thesis-foundations-v2.md, §2]. Decision theory operates at a level of analysis (individual executive cognition) better suited to qualitative interview data than to the archival design this thesis proposes [data/summaries/lit-search-thesis-foundations-v2.md, §4].

The assessment points toward PE as the primary spine, with disruption theory and institutional theory as supporting lenses that address PE's identified weaknesses.

## 4. Proposed Spine and Supporting Theories

The comparative assessment points toward punctuated equilibrium as the primary theoretical spine. I recommend this lens not because the earlier search assumed it, but because the evidence in Sections 2 and 3 consistently favours it on all three criteria. PE fits the dependent variable directly: it theorizes organizational structural change as its core phenomenon, not adoption behaviour or competitive dynamics (Romanelli & Tushman, 1994). Its archival operationalization is the most mature of any candidate, with six validated coding approaches published across AMJ, Organization Science, and SMJ (Girod & Whittington, 2015; Gordon et al., 2000). And its gaps are precisely the kind that supporting theories can fill without requiring a different primary frame.

Two supporting lenses complete the architecture. Disruption theory supplies the trigger mechanism that PE leaves thin. PE identifies that environmental jolts produce reorientation (Meyer, 1982) but does not explain why GenAI, rather than earlier technology transitions, produces this particular structural response. Christensen's (1997) framework answers this: GenAI enters from below as a cheap substitute for expensive labour, forcing incumbents to restructure or face displacement by AI-native competitors. Decision theory supplies the micro-level choice basis. PE treats reorientation as an organizational-level event; it does not address who decides to restructure, on what information, under what cognitive constraints. The Simon-March-Cyert lineage positions restructuring as a bounded-rationality problem shaped by executive attention and information availability.

This recommendation is offered for Professor Barneto's reaction, not as a settled conclusion. Institutional theory remains a plausible alternative primary spine if the data ultimately show convergent restructuring patterns driven by mimetic pressure rather than firm-specific jolts. The December proposal should commit to a primary lens only after the spine papers have been read in full text.

The spine papers that follow from this recommendation are: Romanelli and Tushman (1994, AMJ, A*) for the canonical PE empirical test and coding protocol; Gersick (1991, AMR, A*) for the deep structure construct; Girod and Whittington (2015, Organization Science, A*) for proportion-based continuous measurement; Albert, Eklund and Tang (2026, SMJ, A*) for the archival structure database that operationalizes the DV; and Alekseeva et al. (2026, SMJ, A*) for the AI adoption intensity measure that operationalizes the IV. The first three anchor the theoretical frame. The last two anchor the method, which is theory-neutral and stands regardless of final lens choice.

## 5. Gap and Next Steps

The literature surveyed above establishes that punctuated equilibrium has strong archival operationalization precedent for coding structural change in organizations (Romanelli & Tushman, 1994; Girod & Whittington, 2015). Yet existing PE research has not sufficiently applied the theory to AI-driven technology transitions, and few studies have examined how AI adoption intensity relates to organizational reorientation patterns in archival firm-level settings [data/summaries/lit-search-thesis-foundations-v2.md, §6]. The measurement methods exist. The theoretical mechanism exists. The application to the GenAI transition does not.

Three immediate full-text reads follow from this recommendation. Romanelli and Tushman (1994) contains the exact PE coding scheme, domain definitions, and inter-rater reliability protocol required to operationalize the dependent variable. Albert, Eklund, and Tang (2026) provides the database construction protocol for measuring organizational structure from SEC filings across 11,658 firm-years. Girod and Whittington (2015) supplies the continuous proportion-based thresholds that distinguish incremental reconfigurations from discontinuous restructurings [data/summaries/lit-search-thesis-foundations-v2.md, §5].

Additional searches are needed in two areas: disruption theory's empirical application to technology-driven structural change (to ground the supporting lens), and institutional isomorphism studies examining why multiple firms restructure concurrently under the same technology shock.

This review provides the theoretical foundation for the December 2026 defense document (25-30 pages), which will formalize the research question, specify the archival methodology, and present preliminary coding protocols [data/drafts/ide_session_2026-06-17.md, Barneto direction #8]. The spine is proposed, not closed; Barneto's reaction will determine whether PE holds or an alternative lens better serves the phenomenon.
