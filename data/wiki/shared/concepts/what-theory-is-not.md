## Definition

Sutton and Staw (1995) define theory as "the connections among phenomena, a story about why acts, events, structure, and thoughts occur" (p.378). Their contribution is primarily negative: specifying five elements commonly mistaken for theory in organizational research. Strong theory "delves into underlying processes so as to understand the systematic reasons for a particular occurrence or nonoccurrence" and "burrows deeply into microprocesses, laterally into neighboring concepts, or in an upward direction, tying itself to broader social phenomena" (p.378).

## Key Dimensions

### Five Criteria (What Theory Is Not)

1. **References are not theory.** Listing citations does not constitute theory. Authors must explicate which concepts and causal arguments are adopted from cited sources and how they connect to the theory under development. References can serve as "a smoke screen to hide the absence of theory" (p.372).

2. **Data are not theory.** Empirical patterns describe *which* patterns were observed; theory explains *why*. "The data do not generate theory; only researchers do that" (p.374, citing Mintzberg 1979). This applies equally to qualitative findings: rich descriptions of informant statements are not theory unless accompanied by causal reasoning.

3. **Lists of variables or constructs are not theory.** A theory must explain why variables come about or why they are connected. Listing demographic characteristics, categorizing determinants, or ordering variables in a model does not make a theoretical argument.

4. **Diagrams are not theory.** Figures are "stage props rather than the performance itself" (p.376, citing Whetten 1989). Most diagrams show expected associations but fail to explain why those connections exist. A strong theory specifies conditions under which the proposition is most and least likely to hold.

5. **Hypotheses are not theory.** "Hypotheses are concise statements about what is expected to occur, not why it is expected to occur" (p.377). When papers present many hypotheses only tenuously linked to causal explanations, hypotheses are substituting for theory.

[Source: wiki/sources/sutton-staw-1995-what-theory-is-not.md, §Key Arguments]

### What Strong Theory Requires

Theory requires explaining the causal mechanism: the *why* behind the *what*. One indication of strong theory: "it is possible to discern conditions in which the major proposition or hypothesis is most and least likely to hold" (p.377). Strong theory usually stems from "a single or small set of research ideas" rather than a proliferation of hypotheses (p.377).

## How Sources Relate

This is a single-source concept page promoted to shared because the Sutton-Staw criteria apply across all research spaces. Every research paper, thesis chapter, literature review, and term paper produced in this workspace must satisfy these criteria. The paper's arguments have been cited over 9,000 times and remain the standard editorial touchstone for evaluating theoretical contributions in management and IS research.

The paper complements the research paradigms concept (Rehman and Alharthi, 2016): paradigms tell you *how* to produce knowledge; Sutton-Staw tells you *what constitutes* a theoretical contribution regardless of paradigm. Both interpretivist and positivist research must explain why, not merely describe what.

## Application to Research

For the agentic AI governance paper, the five criteria function as a self-diagnostic checklist:

1. **References risk**: The literature review cites Kolt (2025), Jarrahi and Ritala (2025), Berente et al. (2021), and others. Citing them does not constitute theory. The paper must explicate *which* causal mechanisms from these sources apply to the governance-risk relationship and *how* they connect.

2. **Data risk**: If Phase 1 qualitative findings (executive interviews about governance practices) are presented without explaining why those practices reduce or increase risk, the paper substitutes data for theory.

3. **Variables risk**: Listing "governance maturity," "workflow control rigidity," "shadow AI prevalence," and "enterprise risk" as constructs does not constitute theory. The paper must explain the causal logic: *why* does governance maturity reduce incidents? Through what mechanism does control rigidity mediate?

4. **Diagram risk**: The conceptual framework diagram (showing governance maturity -> enterprise risk, mediated by control rigidity, moderated by shadow AI) is not theory. It is a stage prop. The surrounding text must articulate the causal process.

5. **Hypotheses risk**: Propositions P1-P4 state what is expected. Theory must explain why these propositions follow from principal-agent dynamics.

The test: can a reader who covers the conceptual framework section explain not just *what* relationships the paper proposes but *why* governance maturity reduces risk at the micro-process level?

## Open Questions

1. Sutton and Staw do not address what makes theory *strong* beyond identifying what theory is *not*. How should quality of causal reasoning be evaluated in nascent fields like agentic AI governance where empirical evidence is thin?

2. The paper acknowledges a double bind: journals demand both strong theory and precise measurement, but theoretical variables often resist full operationalization. For formative constructs like governance maturity (where measures may not yet exist, per Prof. Prashar's feedback), how should the theory-measurement tradeoff be navigated?

3. Sutton and Staw recommend journals accept "illustrative" rather than "definitive" data when theory is strong (p.382). Does this support the exploratory sequential design where Phase 1 qualitative data illustrates theorized mechanisms before Phase 2 tests them quantitatively?
