## Summary

Zeithaml and colleagues formalise the Theories-in-Use (TIU) approach as a route to building "organic" or "home-grown" marketing theory: theory whose central constructs are grounded in the marketing context itself rather than borrowed from other disciplines (p. 32). The method elicits practitioners' own if-then beliefs about actions and outcomes, then abstracts across many such beliefs into constructs, propositions, and arguments the discipline can use. It is a method for theory construction, not theory testing (p. 45).

## Key Arguments

### Origin and definition

The term comes from Argyris and Schon (1974), cited on p. 34, who used "theories-in-use" for the mental models that actually govern behaviour, as distinct from the "espoused theories" people claim to follow. The two often differ, and part of a theory-in-use is tacit (p. 34). A practitioner's theory-in-use can be represented as a set of if-then links between actions and outcomes (p. 33). The researcher elicits these from knowledgeable people, evaluates them, and abstracts commonalities into a broader emergent theory, acting as an active partner rather than a passive recorder (p. 34).

### The process (Figure 1, p. 37)

Figure 1, labelled "an approximation," shows an iterative loop, not a pipeline:

1. Start with a tentative construct definition; if it is not well defined, return to participant conversations.
2. Once the construct holds, develop tentative propositions and arguments.
3. Two gates: a **foundational test** (are the propositions plausible and aligned with the definitions and arguments?) and an **advanced test** (are they interesting, substantively informative, and held together by one or a few common themes?). Failing either sends the researcher back to the data or to new conversations (p. 37).

Three kinds of work run through the loop: construct development, proposition development, and argument development (p. 38). Argument development is the search for the "why": pressing participants on the reasons they hold their if-then beliefs (p. 39).

### Participants and data collection

A typical project uses 15-25 participants selected in successive phases for their knowledgeability, not for representativeness (pp. 34, 36). Data come from one-on-one in-depth conversations (and focus groups), each recorded, noted, and followed by a memo (p. 38). The authors recommend a brief conversation guide be published with the article (p. 38, n. 4).

### Coding and analysis

The authors adopt grounded-theory coding vocabulary from Corbin and Strauss (2008), cited on pp. 39-41: open coding (identifying and categorising elements), axial coding (linking concepts into antecedents, consequences, moderators), and selective coding (unifying around a core category). Abstraction pools granular data into higher-order constructs (p. 39). Theoretical saturation and theoretical sampling are named and defined (p. 42, Table 7 p. 46).

### Five rigor criteria (Table 6, p. 44)

Four are from the interpretive tradition of Lincoln and Guba (1985), cited on p. 42; the fifth is the authors' addition.

1. **Credibility** (≈ internal validity): the if-then propositions are plausible; demonstrated by supporting arguments, range-spanning questions, and data from multiple participants (p. 43).
2. **Transferability** (≈ external validity): the theory holds in contexts not sampled; demonstrated by similar theory emerging across varied contexts (p. 43).
3. **Dependability** (≈ reliability): multiple researchers reach the same constructs and propositions from the same data (p. 44).
4. **Confirmability** (≈ objectivity): the theory is certified as coming from the data, not the researcher's predispositions; demonstrated via participant checks (like member checks) and interjudge reliability (p. 44).
5. **Distinctiveness** (≈ discriminant validity): the constructs and propositions are genuinely new relative to the literature (pp. 44-45).

The authors weight the set: emphasis on credibility, transferability, and distinctiveness, with dependability and confirmability "not as pertinent" (p. 45).

### When TIU is a good fit, and when not

Three stated motivations (pp. 34-36): to construct organic theory about new or emerging phenomena; to extend existing perspectives and resolve ambiguities; and to guide future empirical (testing) efforts. The authors note TIU "has fewer advantages when working with well-defined constructs where the nomological net has been mapped out comprehensively" (p. 35).

### Comparison to related approaches (Table 2, pp. 34-35)

TIU is set beside case-based grounded theory, ethnography, and deductive theoretical modeling across purpose, mindset, process, data collection, sampling, and sample size. TIU and case study both move "from data to theory" via theoretical sampling; TIU is distinguished by its one-on-one elicitation of if-then beliefs and by inviting participants to critique the researcher's tentative theory, which the authors argue is less common in other grounded-theory approaches (p. 34).

## Methodology

Conceptual/methodological article in the Journal of Marketing. No primary data collection; the authors codify practice from their own body of TIU-based studies and illustrate with exemplars (Table 3, pp. 35-36), including service quality (Parasuraman, Zeithaml, and Berry 1985, cited in the paper) and market orientation (Kohli and Jaworski 1990).

## Findings

Not applicable (methodological paper). The contribution is prescriptive guidance for building organic marketing theory.

### Why this matters for the DBA thesis / a research paper

- TIU is a candidate method for the qualitative, theory-building phase of a study on a new or emerging phenomenon (for example, organisational restructuring during the GenAI transition), where validated constructs and a mapped nomological net do not yet exist (p. 35). This is the same justification pattern used for theory-building from cases.
- The five rigor criteria (Table 6, p. 44) give a ready-made standard for defending a qualitative phase in a proposal or methods chapter, and the fifth criterion (distinctiveness) is a useful prompt for the contribution statement.
- The if-then elicitation and the construct/proposition/argument split map cleanly onto a sequential mixed-methods design: TIU builds the constructs and propositions in Phase 1, which a Phase 2 study then tests. See `data/wiki/shared/methods/mixed-methods-sequential-design.md` and `construct-operationalization.md`.
- The construct-development guidance (abstraction, proximal-construct comparison) is directly reusable when moving from interview data to an operational definition.

## Limitations

The authors' own acknowledged limits (p. 45): TIU is not for theory testing; it requires interviewing skill researchers often have to acquire; and it works only when informants have developed theories, so on genuinely new phenomena participants may offer a "guess" rather than an "aha." The sample-size rule of thumb (15-25) is loose, and the authors say "more systematic clarity is needed" (p. 48). The advanced test for retaining a proposition leans on the words "interesting" and "substantively informative" (p. 37), which are judgment calls.

## Key Quotes

- "is not suited for theory testing" (p. 45).
- Advanced test: propositions should be "interesting, substantially informative, and hang together" (p. 37).
