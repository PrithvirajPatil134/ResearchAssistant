# Human-Authored Writing Standard

All research and academic content generated — case studies, teaching notes, research papers, literature reviews, working papers, conference submissions, dissertation chapters, abstracts, research proposals, annotated bibliographies — must read as if written by a human researcher, not generated by AI. This rule applies to every piece of scholarly writing produced in this workspace.

## Banned Vocabulary

Never use these words or phrases. They appear disproportionately in AI-generated text and are the fastest way a reviewer spots machine writing:

**Words**: delve, tapestry, nuanced, landscape, multifaceted, leverage, utilize, streamline, foster, facilitate, harness, robust, seamless, scalable, comprehensive, pivotal, groundbreaking, transformative, revolutionary, game-changing, cutting-edge, remarkable, crucial, significant, aforementioned, underscores, underpin, elucidate, myriad, plethora, paradigm

**Phrases**: "at its core", "this underscores", "it is worth noting", "it is important to note", "in today's [anything]", "in an era of", "in the realm of", "it can be argued that", "one could argue", "this highlights the fact that", "plays a crucial role", "a testament to", "serves as a reminder", "sheds light on", "paves the way for", "the ever-evolving", "navigating the complexities"

**Openers to never start a sentence with**: Furthermore, Moreover, Additionally, Consequently, Subsequently, Nevertheless, "In conclusion", "To sum up", "This essay/paper/section will discuss"

## Structural Rules

1. **Vary sentence rhythm**: Never write 5+ consecutive sentences within 5 words of each other in length. Follow a long sentence with a short one. Use a two-word sentence after a complex explanation. Start a paragraph with a question occasionally.

2. **No em dashes**: Never use em dashes (—) in any academic writing. Replace with periods and new sentences, commas, colons, semicolons, or parentheses. This is a zero-tolerance rule.

3. **Break parallel symmetry**: If writing a list, do not start every item with the same verb form or make every item the same length. Let one item run long because it needs context. Keep another short because the point is obvious.

4. **No formulaic headings**: Use normal-sized headings. Do not make every heading the same font size or follow the same noun-phrase pattern. Vary heading styles naturally.

5. **No announcing structure**: Do not write "This section will discuss..." or "The following analysis presents..." — just present the content directly.

## Voice and Tone

1. **Own your claims**: State judgments and interpretations directly. Do not hedge every sentence with "it can be argued", "some might say", "to some extent", "potentially", "it seems that". If you cannot fully back a claim, one qualifier is enough ("often", "in most cases"). But when giving direct analysis, commit to it.

2. **Use concrete verbs**: "Cut" not "optimize". "Flag" not "identify areas for improvement". "Dropped" not "experienced a decline". "Rose" not "witnessed an increase". Every verb should show what actually happened.

3. **Be specific, not generic**: Replace "studies show" with the actual citation. Replace "significant impact" with the actual number. Replace "various factors" with the actual factors. Specificity is the strongest signal of human authorship.

4. **Write as the author, not a narrator**: In teaching notes, write "The key insight here is..." not "Students should identify...". In analysis sections, write "The data shows..." not "The instructor should guide students to see...". First-person academic voice ("I argue", "this paper contends") is acceptable and preferred over detached prescriptive framing.

5. **Vary tone across sections**: Show more confidence on strong findings. Show appropriate caution on weaker evidence. Let your emphasis reflect what actually matters most, not a uniform level of importance for everything.

## Academic Genre-Specific Rules

### Case Studies
- Present the dilemma. Do not explain it or solve it in the case itself. The case is the problem; the teaching note is the analysis.
- Keep the narrative grounded in the protagonist's perspective and real events.
- Do not over-detail every aspect. Focus on the tension and the decision.

### Teaching Notes
- Answer each discussion question directly, as you would teach it in class.
- Do not use prescriptive instructor-manual language ("The instructor should...", "Push students to...").
- Present analysis in your own analytical voice, not as a structured rubric.

### Research Papers and Literature Reviews
- Synthesize, do not merely summarize. Show how papers relate, conflict, or build on each other.
- Take a position. A literature review without a point of view is a bibliography.
- Use discipline-appropriate formality but avoid the sterile, hedged-on-every-claim tone that marks AI output.

### Quantitative Analysis Sections
- Walk through calculations naturally, as you would explain them to a colleague.
- Do not use the format "Data extraction from exhibits: [bullet list]" — that is an AI pattern.
- Integrate numbers into flowing prose. "At 45,000 metres per month and a rate of ₹8.07, the contribution margin works out to ₹6.51 per metre" reads human. A formatted extraction table does not.

## Formatting

- Use normal-sized, naturally varied headings.
- Do not highlight figures or data without explaining why they are highlighted.
- Tables are fine, but do not make every cell the same word count or every row follow identical structure.
- Avoid perfectly uniform formatting across all sections — some natural variation is expected in human-written documents.

## Abbreviations

- When a term is used for the first time, spell it out in full followed by the abbreviation in parentheses: e.g., "Business Process Management (BPM)". Use only the abbreviation for all subsequent occurrences.
- Do not introduce an abbreviation if the term appears only once in the document. Spell it out and move on.
- In reference lists and paper titles within references, use the full term as published; do not substitute abbreviations.
- Common discipline abbreviations that need no introduction on first use: AI, IT, IS, CEO, CFO. All others must be introduced.

## The Acid Test

Before finalizing any content, ask: "If a reviewer read this, would they think a person with domain knowledge wrote it, or would they suspect a machine?" If the answer is uncertain, rewrite the weakest three sentences in each section — that is usually enough to shift the entire piece.
