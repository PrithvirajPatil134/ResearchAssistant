## Summary

Allan Dafoe's report (first draft July 2017; v1.0 dated 27 August 2018, Centre for the
Governance of AI, Future of Humanity Institute, University of Oxford) sets out a research
agenda that defines and organizes the field of AI governance. Dafoe frames "the AI
governance problem" as "the problem of devising global norms, policies, and institutions
to best ensure the beneficial development and use of advanced AI" (abstract, p.i). The
field itself "studies how humanity can best navigate the transition to advanced AI systems,
focusing on the political, economic, military, governance, and ethical dimensions" (p.5).
At the time of writing Dafoe judged that "scholarly attention to the AI revolution remains
negligible" (abstract, p.i).

The report divides the field into three complementary research clusters: the technical
landscape (technical inputs, possibilities, and constraints for AI), AI politics (political
dynamics among firms, governments, publics, researchers, and other actors), and AI ideal
governance (what global arrangements humanity would build if it could sufficiently cooperate)
(p.i, p.11). Dafoe uses the analogy of building a new city: the technical landscape is the
price and strength of steel, politics is the contending motivations of developers and
residents, and ideal governance is the master plan of infrastructure, laws, and norms (p.11).

This is a scoping and agenda-setting document, not an empirical study or a governance
framework for organizations. Dafoe is explicit about its limits: it focuses on extreme
risks by design, it is "an introduction, not the introduction," it is "neither parsimonious
nor comprehensive," and it is "not authoritative" on topics outside his expertise (pp.2-3).

## Key Arguments

- AI governance and AI safety are distinct but paired pursuits with a shared goal of
  beneficial AI. "AI safety focuses on the technical questions of how AI is built; AI
  governance focuses on the institutions and contexts in which AI is built and used" (p.6).
  The two can be treated as exclusive and exhaustive categories of the work needed to build
  beneficial AI (footnote 56, p.25).
- The field is best organized as three research clusters (technical landscape, AI politics,
  AI ideal governance), and most work must engage across all three (p.11).
- Four sources of catastrophic risk from advanced AI: (1) robust totalitarianism enabled by
  lie detection, social manipulation, autonomous weapons, and ubiquitous sensors; (2)
  preventive, inadvertent, or unmanageable great-power (nuclear) war; (3) broadly superhuman
  AI not fully aligned with human values; (4) systematic value erosion from competition
  (pp.7-8).
- These risks are negative externalities: "harms from socio-technical developments that
  impact individuals other than those responsible for the developments." They are hard to
  manage because they may be extreme, complex, and spill across borders and generations (p.8).
- Building the right institutions (norms and political arrangements) is "plausibly close to a
  necessary and sufficient condition" for adequately addressing these risks (p.8).
- AI accident risks and AI systemic risks are different framings. Accident risks arise from a
  particular, intentionally designed AI system. Systemic risks are "undesired outcomes...
  that can emerge from a system of competing and cooperating agents and can be amplified by
  novel forms of AI." Dafoe calls the systemic framing "relatively neglected" (p.28).
- The AI safety problem is analogous to the alignment problem in capitalism: "how to build a
  legal and regulatory environment so that the profit motive leads firms to produce social
  value." Dafoe cites Enron (creative accounting), Deepwater Horizon (disaster/pollution),
  Theranos (deceiving investors), and Volkswagen (deceiving regulators) as cases of firms
  "perversely optimizing for profit" despite informed humans and governance institutions
  being present. Powerful AI systems may lack even the common sense those humans had, and
  could be more intelligent than their governing institutions (p.27).
- Researchers should prioritize questions that are important, tractable, and neglected, and
  for which they have comparative advantage and interest. Questions are more likely to be
  neglected if they "do not directly and exclusively contribute to an actor's profit or
  power" (p.13).
- Race dynamics are a central governance danger: when large gains flow from relative
  advantage, actors have strong incentives to trade off against safety, transparency,
  accountability, and democracy to raise their odds of gaining advantage (pp.43-44). "A
  plausible and important proposition is that races are more dangerous the smaller the margin
  of the leader" (p.44).
- Routes for escaping or avoiding a race run, in increasing institutionalization, from norms
  to agreements ("soft law") to treaties to institutions (pp.45-46). Third-party standards,
  verification, enforcement, and control offer four escalating institutional models, from an
  international safety-standards agency up to an "Atomic Development Authority" that itself
  controls the dangerous capability (pp.46-47).

## Methodology

Conceptual scoping and agenda-setting report. Dafoe surveys the problem space, poses research
questions, and points to published work relevant to each question. There is no primary data
collection, no survey, and no empirical test. The document is explicitly a "firehose" of
questions rather than a parsimonious model or a validated framework (p.2). Contributions and
comments from a large set of collaborators affiliated with the Governance of AI Program are
acknowledged, with primary authorship attributed to Dafoe (footnote 1, p.1).

## Findings

As an agenda rather than a study, the document reports positions and framings rather than
empirical findings. The load-bearing claims are:

- AI safety and AI governance partition the work of building beneficial AI, and the character
  of the safety problem (its "safety production function," how steep the safety-performance
  tradeoff is) shapes how hard the governance problem will be (pp.31-32).
- Complex, fast-moving, tightly coupled systems can produce emergent behavior and "normal
  accidents." Dafoe grounds this in the 2010 "flash crash," in which automated trading
  algorithms produced 20,000 "erroneous trades" and a sudden trillion-dollar decline in US
  financial market value, halted not by real-time human intervention but by automated safety
  mechanisms (p.26).
- Simple AI fails by being "too dumb" (fragile classifiers, adversarial attacks, Microsoft's
  "Tay"); more capable AI gains a new failure mode of being "too clever," where a powerful
  optimizer finds unintended and harmful solutions (the boat-racing reward hack, the evolved
  radio circuit, the tic-tac-toe memory bomb) (pp.26-27).
- Everitt et al.'s (2018) framework lists desirable safety properties for AI systems:
  reliability and security, corrigibility, intelligibility/interpretability/transparency,
  value specification, limiting capabilities, and performance and safety guarantees (pp.29-30).
- International cooperation on transformative AI is more likely when parties perceive strong
  mutual interest, have a trusting relationship, agree on what compliance means, can verify
  compliance easily and rapidly, face low risk from being defected on, and have low incentives
  to defect (p.46).

## Relevance to Research

This is a foundational framing reference for the agentic AI governance thread, not a
theoretical anchor for the enterprise-level model. Its value is upstream of the constructs.

- **Field definition for the literature review.** Dafoe supplies the canonical definition of
  AI governance as a field and the safety-vs-governance distinction (pp.5-6). The Prashar
  paper's introduction can situate enterprise-level agentic governance as one slice of the
  larger field Dafoe defines, rather than asserting the field's boundaries independently.
- **Neglectedness framing for the gap.** Dafoe's important/tractable/neglected heuristic
  (p.13) offers a sourced, non-absolute way to argue the gap: the enterprise-outcome question
  is neglected precisely because, in Dafoe's terms, long-term and cross-cutting governance
  questions "do not directly and exclusively contribute to an actor's profit or power." This
  aligns with Prof. Prashar's instruction to position via what past studies could improve
  rather than via absence claims.
- **Governance-to-enterprise-risk bridge (conceptual).** The alignment-in-capitalism analogy
  (p.27) is the closest Dafoe comes to the paper's core pathway. It frames misaligned AI as a
  corporate-governance failure of the Enron/Volkswagen type, which supports P1 (governance
  maturity reduces incidents) at the level of mechanism, not measurement.
- **Emergent behavior and control risk.** The accident-vs-systemic-risk distinction (p.28)
  and the flash-crash case (p.26) give a concrete, cited anchor for the emergent-behavior and
  control-rigidity concerns that Kolt (subagent delegation) and Taeihagh (multi-agent emergent
  behavior) raise. It supports P2 (workflow control rigidity) by showing that automated safety
  mechanisms, not human oversight, stopped a fast-moving multi-agent failure.
- **Positioning against existing pages.** Where Kolt supplies the micro-mechanism (agency
  costs inside a single principal-agent relationship) and Papagiannidis supplies the
  org-level practice taxonomy, Dafoe supplies the macro-frame (the field, its clusters, and
  its risk structure). The three levels are complementary, not redundant.

## Limitations

- The document operates at the civilizational and geopolitical level (existential risk, great-
  power war, international AI races, superintelligence). It does not address enterprise-level
  or organizational AI governance, does not define governance-maturity constructs, and does
  not measure firm-level risk or performance outcomes. Its direct fit to the Prashar paper's
  IV-to-DV design is therefore limited to framing and mechanism, not to measurement.
- It is a 2018 agenda that predates the current agentic-AI wave. It does not discuss "AI
  agents," "agentic AI," LLM-based autonomous agents, or subagent delegation in the terms the
  later sources (Kolt 2025, Taeihagh 2025, Vu 2025) use. The relevant bridge is conceptual
  (emergent/systemic risk, alignment) rather than terminological.
- Dafoe states the document is "neither parsimonious nor comprehensive," "not authoritative,"
  and deliberately focused on extreme rather than moderate risks (pp.2-3). Claims outside his
  expertise "may be discussed in less detail than their importance and tractability warrants."
- It offers no operational definitions, no measures, and no empirical evidence. Every proposal
  is a research question or a framing, by design.
