## 1. Introduction

Enterprises are deploying agentic artificial intelligence at a pace that outstrips the governance mechanisms built to supervise it. An agentic system is not a predictive model that outputs a score. It is a goal-directed program that plans, calls tools, writes to systems of record, and takes actions that commit the firm to financial, operational, and legal consequences. The shift from model-as-prediction to model-as-actor changes the risk surface of the firm. It also changes what governance must do. Governance of a classifier is about bias, accuracy, and drift. Governance of an agent is about authority, delegation, revocation, visibility, and liability.

The literature on either side of this shift is well developed, but in isolation. On the technical side, Kolt (2025) applies principal-agent theory to AI agents and identifies three governance requirements: inclusivity, visibility, and liability. Lu et al. (2024) catalogue 35 responsible AI design patterns. Cohen et al. (2024) argue that advanced artificial agents cannot be reliably safety-tested. Chan et al. (2023) document that harms scale with the level of agency. Chan, Kolt et al. (2024) argue that visibility is a precondition for governance. On the management side, Weill and Ross (2004) show that firms with mature information technology governance earn returns on IT investment up to 40 percent higher than peers. Friede, Busch and Bassen (2015) aggregate 2,200 studies and find a positive governance-to-financial-performance link in roughly 90 percent of cases. Mikalef et al. (2021, 2025) extend the pattern to general AI: AI capability and responsible AI governance translate into competitive performance through organisational creativity and knowledge management.

Each literature is rigorous, but neither answers the question the firm actually faces. The closest attempt is Vu et al. (2025), who interview 22 Business Process Management (BPM) practitioners about agent governance and derive six governance recommendations and four alignment actions. Their study captures practitioner expectations, but none of the 22 participants had deployed agentic AI in production. The study stops at perceptions. No empirical work yet connects agentic AI governance maturity to measurable firm-level risk outcomes. The technical side offers mechanisms without validation; the management side offers validation for the wrong object of study; the BPM side offers practitioner expectations without outcome measurement.

The objective of this research is to examine the relationship between agentic AI governance maturity and enterprise risk exposure, and to identify the governance practices that predict measurable firm-level outcomes. Two research questions follow.

- **RQ1 (qualitative).** What governance practices are organisations adopting for agentic AI deployments, and what risk categories emerge from those deployments?
- **RQ2 (quantitative).** Do those governance practices predict firm-level risk outcomes, specifically incident frequency, financial loss, and compliance failure?

The study uses an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design. Phase 1 discovers governance constructs inductively from public discourse and practitioner data. Phase 2 tests those constructs in a firm-level survey analysed by binary logistic regression. The design responds to the state of the field: validated instruments for agentic AI governance do not yet exist, and a pure hypothesis-testing study would assume constructs that the literature has not produced. Vu et al. (2025) themselves call for quantitative studies across industries and for governance structures that ensure transparent and accountable decision-making (p. 42) as the next step. This paper answers that call.

## 2. Theoretical Background and Literature Review

Sutton and Staw (1995) are unambiguous. References are not theory, data are not theory, lists of variables are not theory, diagrams are not theory, hypotheses are not theory. Theory is the causal reasoning that answers why. The sections below synthesise three streams of literature around that standard, rather than summarising them paper by paper.

### Stream 1: Agentic AI creates an agency problem that existing AI governance does not solve

Kolt (2025) makes the central move in the technical literature: once an AI system plans and acts on behalf of a principal, the governance question is not accuracy but delegation. Principal-agent theory supplies the causal mechanism. Agents have private information the principal does not observe (information asymmetry), discretion the principal has granted but cannot fully monitor (authority), and incentive structures that may diverge from the principal's interests (loyalty). Kolt's three requirements, inclusivity of stakeholders, visibility of agent behaviour, and liability for outcomes, are not design aesthetics. They are the minimum conditions under which a principal can rationally delegate at all.

Cohen et al. (2024) deepen the problem. Advanced agents cannot be reliably safety-tested because their capability surface grows with context, tools, and composition; tests that hold in one configuration do not transfer. Lu et al. (2024) respond with 35 responsible AI design patterns but explicitly mark empirical validation in organisations as missing. Kolt, Shur-Ofry and Cohen (2025) reframe the target: agentic systems are complex systems with emergent, path-dependent behaviour, and single-point controls predictably fail. Chan et al. (2023) document that harms grow with the agency level across finance, healthcare, and criminal justice, and Chan, Kolt et al. (2024) argue that without agent identifiers, activity logs, and real-time monitoring, governance is an aspiration rather than a mechanism. Bengio, Hinton et al. (2024) connect the individual-agent case to systemic risk.

The stream converges on one causal claim: without governance mechanisms that address authority, visibility, and liability simultaneously, agentic deployments produce outcomes the principal did not authorise and cannot reverse.

### Stream 2: IT and AI governance maturity translate into measurable business outcomes

The management literature has established, repeatedly and across settings, that governance maturity is not a compliance cost but a performance variable. Weill and Ross (2004) study more than 250 firms and show that firms in the top governance quartile earn returns on IT investment on the order of 40 percent higher than firms in the bottom quartile, and that the mechanism is the quality of decision rights over IT assets, not the size of IT spend. Friede, Busch and Bassen (2015) aggregate 2,200 studies of environmental, social and governance factors and corporate financial performance; roughly 90 percent of studies show a non-negative link, and the governance component is consistently the strongest driver. Mikalef and Gupta (2021), writing in *Information and Management*, operationalise AI capability and show, via partial least squares structural equation modelling, that the effect on firm performance is mediated by organisational creativity. Mikalef et al. (2025) extend the argument to responsible AI governance and show that knowledge management capabilities carry the effect to competitive performance. Enholm et al. (2022) survey the field and observe that the dark side of AI, the pathway through which AI can damage firm value, remains understudied.

The causal story across these works is consistent. Governance works not by preventing technology adoption but by shaping how the organisation absorbs, monitors, and reconfigures technology in use. Decision rights, oversight routines, and knowledge capabilities convert technology investment into performance.

### Stream 3: The bridge, and the gap

Vu et al. (2025) provide an empirical study on agent governance in organisational settings to date. Their qualitative study, conducted through semi-structured interviews with 22 BPM practitioners across diverse industries, surfaces both the promise and the risk of agentic AI. Practitioners anticipate gains in efficiency, data quality, compliance, and scalability. They also flag bias, over-reliance on automation, cybersecurity threats, job displacement, and unauthorised decision-making as risks. From these findings, Vu et al. derive six governance recommendations: define clear business goals, set legal and ethical guardrails, establish human-agent collaboration, customise agent behaviour, manage risks, and ensure safe integration with fallback options. They also propose four alignment actions for bridging traditional BPM with agentic AI: balance human and agent roles, redefine human involvement, adapt process structures, and introduce performance metrics.

Two features of the Vu et al. study are directly relevant to the present research. First, none of the 22 participants had actual production experience with agentic AI. Their assessments are expectation-based, grounded in prior experience with robotic process automation (RPA) and conventional automation rather than observed agent behaviour. This means the governance recommendations are derived from anticipated, not experienced, risk. Second, the study is purely qualitative. It identifies what practitioners believe governance should look like, but it does not test whether those governance practices reduce measurable harm. The authors acknowledge both limitations and call explicitly for quantitative validation across industries.

The gap is therefore specific. The CS literature has the mechanisms but not the firm-level evidence. The management literature has the firm-level evidence but not for agentic AI. The BPM literature has practitioner expectations but not outcome data. No study combines qualitative discovery of agentic AI governance constructs with quantitative testing of their business impact. The theoretical lens for closing that gap is the intersection of principal-agent theory (Kolt, 2025) and the IT governance-to-business-value lineage (Weill and Ross, 2004; Mikalef and Gupta, 2021). Agents are a new class of principal-agent relationship inside the firm; the existing lineage predicts that governance of that relationship should translate into measurable risk and performance outcomes, in the same way IT governance did two decades earlier. This research is one of the few to test that prediction.

## 3. Research Methodology

The design is an exploratory sequential mixed-methods study (Creswell and Plano Clark, 2018). Phase 1 is qualitative and theory-building; Phase 2 is quantitative and theory-testing. Venkatesh, Brown and Bala (2013) in *MIS Quarterly* serve as the information systems (IS) methodological anchor. Their central argument is that mixed methods are defensible in IS when neither purely interpretive nor purely positivist approaches can answer the research question alone. That condition holds here: validated instruments for agentic AI governance do not exist, so quantitative testing without prior qualitative discovery would operationalise the wrong constructs.

The philosophical stance is pragmatist. Pragmatism does not collapse the two paradigms into one; it accepts that each phase makes a different philosophical commitment. Phase 1 is interpretive and inductive: meaning is constructed through engagement with practitioner discourse and documents. Phase 2 is post-positivist and deductive: propositions generated in Phase 1 are subjected to statistical test on a firm-level sample. The paper is explicit about this dual commitment, as Creswell and Plano Clark require.

### Phase 1: Qualitative, theory-building

The objective is to identify governance practices, risk categories, and contextual factors relevant to agentic AI in enterprise settings, and to produce propositions that are testable in Phase 2.

Data sources are triangulated across source types. Publicly available executive discourse includes earnings calls, keynote talks, podcast transcripts, and published interviews with leaders at firms deploying agentic AI at scale. Regulatory disclosures include 10-K risk factors and 8-K incident disclosures filed with the United States Securities and Exchange Commission. Industry reports include Gartner, Forrester, and McKinsey State of AI surveys, included where widely cited in the peer-reviewed literature. Five to ten targeted practitioner interviews with chief information security officers, AI governance leads, or risk officers will supplement these sources where access is available. The triangulation across source types addresses the credibility concern that any one source carries its own selection bias.

Analysis follows thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006) with open coding to surface in vivo concepts and axial coding to link those concepts into higher-order categories. Document analysis draws on Bowen (2009), who provides the protocol for treating filings and reports as primary qualitative data rather than background context. A theory-in-use lens (Argyris and Schon, 1974) will structure the axial coding stage: governance practices disclosed in public forums (earnings calls, keynotes, published frameworks) represent espoused theory, while incident disclosures, regulatory filings, and practitioner interview accounts of actual deployment behaviour represent theory-in-use. The gap between what firms say they do and what they actually do is, in this framing, a governance variable in its own right, one that may predict risk exposure independently of the governance practices themselves. This analytical distinction addresses a limitation of Vu et al. (2025), whose data capture only espoused governance expectations from practitioners who had not yet deployed agents in production.

Trustworthiness is addressed through Lincoln and Guba's four criteria: credibility through triangulation, transferability through thick description, dependability through an audit trail maintained in a research journal, and confirmability through reflexivity notes recorded throughout coding. Inter-rater reliability checks with a second coder will be pursued where feasible, but the single-coder design with a transparent audit trail is the baseline commitment.

The output is three to five propositions linking governance practices to risk outcomes. The six governance recommendations from Vu et al. (2025), covering business context, guardrails, human-agent collaboration, customisation, risk management, and adoption, serve as a sensitising framework for the initial deductive coding categories. Phase 1 is expected to refine, extend, or restructure these categories based on the richer and more varied data sources available to this study. The resulting propositions feed Phase 2.

### Phase 2: Quantitative, theory-testing

The design is a cross-sectional firm-level survey. The unit of analysis is the firm. The respondent is one senior information technology, risk, or governance officer per firm, identified by job title. The target sample is 40 to 50 firms, drawn from professional networks, industry panels, and LinkedIn-based sampling stratified by industry and firm size.

The dependent variable is binary: whether the firm experienced a reportable agentic AI incident in the previous 24 months (yes or no). A binary dependent variable is appropriate because incident reporting at the firm level is discrete and the distributional assumptions of ordinary least squares regression do not hold.

Independent variables are derived from Phase 1 themes. The governance maturity index is a count of adopted governance practices, with the initial item pool drawn from the six Vu et al. (2025) governance recommendation categories and refined through Phase 1 coding. Human-in-the-loop control presence is binary. Shadow AI prevalence is estimated as a percentage of agentic AI usage not sanctioned by central security team. Control variables include industry (categorical), firm size (log revenue or log employee count), agent deployment depth (number of agentic workflows in production), and existing IT governance maturity (using adapted items from Weill and Ross's validated IT governance scale).

The analysis is binary logistic regression. Reported statistics include odds ratios with 95 percent confidence intervals, Wald chi-square tests for coefficient significance, McFadden and Nagelkerke pseudo R-squared values for model fit, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and classification accuracy on a hold-out subset. An interaction term between governance maturity and shadow AI prevalence tests Proposition 3. A quadratic term for workflow control rigidity tests Proposition 4. Validation includes variance inflation factors for multicollinearity, Cook's distance for influential observations, and sensitivity analysis on sample composition by industry.

## 4. Proposed Conceptual Framework

The independent variable is agentic AI governance maturity, operationalised as the breadth and depth of formally adopted governance practices for agentic systems. The mediator is workflow control rigidity, the degree to which agent behaviour is bound by deterministic constraints, approval gates, and tool-use restrictions. The dependent variable is enterprise AI risk exposure, measured through incident frequency, financial loss, and compliance failures. The moderator is shadow AI prevalence, the share of agentic AI use that occurs outside sanctioned governance.

Four propositions follow.

**P1. Firms with higher agentic AI governance maturity experience fewer reportable AI incidents than firms with lower governance maturity.** The causal reasoning runs through principal-agent theory. Governance maturity reduces information asymmetry (through visibility mechanisms), constrains authority (through approval and scope controls), and assigns liability (through ownership and accountability structures). Each of these directly addresses a pathway through which an agent's actions diverge from principal intent. Weill and Ross (2004) show the same mechanism operating for traditional IT assets. The claim here is that the mechanism generalises to AI-as-agent. Vu et al. (2025) provide preliminary qualitative support: their practitioners consistently identified governance practices (guardrails, human-agent collaboration, risk management) as necessary conditions for safe agent deployment, though the link to actual incident reduction was not tested.

**P2. The effect of governance maturity on incident frequency is mediated by workflow control rigidity.** Governance maturity is a firm-level property; incidents occur at the level of individual agent runs. The causal path from one to the other is operational. Mature governance is implemented as deterministic constraints on what an agent can do: guardrails, approval gates, scoped credentials, bounded tool use. Without this translation, governance policy is a document rather than a control. Mikalef et al. (2025) document the same mediation structure in responsible AI governance, where knowledge management capabilities carry the effect of governance on performance. The present model claims the mediator is operational, not cognitive: workflow rigidity is what governance maturity does. Vu et al. (2025) report that practitioners recommended "configurable autonomy," granting agents flexibility in low-risk areas while restricting them in high-risk scenarios. This configurable autonomy is the practitioner-language equivalent of workflow control rigidity as operationalised here.

**P3. Shadow AI prevalence moderates the governance-risk relationship such that higher shadow AI weakens the protective effect of governance.** Governance mechanisms apply to sanctioned systems. When employees deploy agents outside sanctioned channels, those agents inherit none of the controls. The larger the shadow portfolio, the smaller the share of agentic activity that governance actually covers, and the weaker the observed relationship between governance maturity and incident reduction. Shadow information technology literature (Haag and Eckhardt, 2017, and subsequent work) establishes that unsanctioned technology creates measurable organisational risk; the present model extends that logic to AI agents, which have a larger action surface than the files and applications the shadow IT literature originally studied.

**P4. The relationship between workflow control rigidity and enterprise AI risk exposure follows an inverted U-shape, such that moderate levels of control rigidity minimise risk, while both low and excessively high levels of rigidity are associated with greater risk exposure.** At low rigidity, agents operate with insufficient constraints, and the principal-agent problem identified in Stream 1 applies in full: the agent acts without adequate oversight, and incidents accumulate. At excessively high rigidity, the operational value of the agent is suppressed. Agents that cannot act without approval at every step become bottlenecks rather than autonomous delegates. Practitioners route work around them, increasing shadow AI prevalence (connecting P4 to P3) or reverting to manual processes that carry their own error rates. The Vu et al. (2025) finding that practitioners recommended a "gradual approach," starting with simple tasks and increasing autonomy as trust grows, implies that practitioners intuitively recognise this non-linearity: too little autonomy wastes the technology, too much autonomy creates unacceptable risk. The inverted U-shape formalises that intuition. Cohen et al. (2024) provide the theoretical anchor for the high-rigidity end: if agents above a certain capability threshold cannot be reliably safety-tested, then constraining them to the point where they operate below that threshold may eliminate the risk but also eliminate the capability the firm deployed them to provide. The optimal point is somewhere in between.

## 5. Expected Contributions

**Theoretical contribution.** The study offers an empirical bridge between tech-side agentic AI governance mechanisms and management-side business value evidence. It extends principal-agent theory to AI-as-agent settings, where the agent is not a human intermediary but a goal-directed program with tool use and persistent state. It also extends Mikalef et al. (2025), whose framework is built on general AI, to the agentic AI case, where the governance object has a qualitatively different action surface. That extension matters because agents act, not just predict. P4 introduces a non-linear dimension to the governance-risk relationship that the existing literature, which assumes monotonic effects of governance on outcomes, has not considered. The theory-in-use analytical lens in Phase 1 extends Vu et al. (2025) by distinguishing between governance as espoused and governance as practised, a distinction their expectation-based data could not capture.

**Practical contribution.** The study produces actionable governance maturity benchmarks for chief information security officers and AI governance leads. Firms currently invest in AI governance with weak evidence on which practices produce measurable risk reduction. Logistic regression with reported odds ratios gives practitioners a defensible answer: for each additional governance practice adopted, by how much do the odds of a reportable incident decline? Shadow AI prevalence becomes a second measurable input to governance design rather than a folk concern. The inverted U-shape in P4, if confirmed, gives practitioners a calibration target: the goal is not maximum control but optimal control, and the data will indicate where the inflection point lies.

**Methodological contribution.** The study demonstrates that exploratory sequential mixed methods are appropriate, and arguably necessary, for nascent information systems topics where validated instruments do not yet exist. Pure PLS-SEM studies of agentic AI governance would operationalise constructs that the literature has not stabilised. Pure qualitative studies would stop at perceptions, as Vu et al. (2025) acknowledge. The two-phase design, with propositions generated from Phase 1 and tested in Phase 2, offers a reproducible template for other emerging IS phenomena. The use of Vu et al.'s six governance categories as a sensitising framework for Phase 1 deductive coding, rather than as a fixed instrument, demonstrates how prior qualitative findings can seed a mixed-methods study without constraining it.

## 6. Limitations and Future Research

The design has known limits. The cross-sectional structure of Phase 2 does not permit causal inference; governance maturity and incident frequency are measured simultaneously, and reverse causation (firms that have had incidents invest more in governance) cannot be ruled out within a single wave. Self-report bias from a single respondent per firm is a second concern; senior officers may under-report incidents or over-state governance maturity. Selection bias in Phase 1 public discourse sources is structural, because firms that speak publicly about AI governance are not a random draw of firms that deploy AI. Logistic regression places a floor on the required sample size, and the target of 40 to 50 firms is adequate for main effects but modest for the interaction term in P3 and the quadratic term in P4. The evidence base is Western and English-language, which constrains transferability to other regulatory and linguistic contexts.

Future research should address these limits directly. A longitudinal panel tracking governance maturity and incident rates over multiple waves would allow causal identification through within-firm variation. Multi-respondent designs, pairing an IT officer with a risk officer or an audit partner, would reduce single-informant bias. Industry-specific models (financial services, healthcare, manufacturing) would test whether the governance-risk mechanism has the same shape across regulatory environments. A qualitative deep-dive into shadow AI dynamics, including why unsanctioned agent use persists even in mature-governance firms, would refine P3 beyond its current formulation. The theory-in-use distinction between espoused and practised governance, introduced here as an analytical lens for Phase 1, could be developed into a standalone quantitative construct in future work, measuring the gap between governance policy and governance execution as a predictor of risk in its own right.

## 7. Conclusion

The gap this paper addresses is narrow and consequential. Agentic AI is being deployed at enterprise scale; governance for agentic AI has been theorised on the tech side, validated for other technologies on the management side, and explored through practitioner expectations on the BPM side (Vu et al., 2025); no study connects these three bodies of evidence to measurable firm-level risk outcomes. This research proposes an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design that discovers agentic AI governance constructs in practitioner data and tests their effect on firm-level risk outcomes in a binary logistic regression model. Four propositions structure the inquiry: a direct governance-to-risk link (P1), a mediation through workflow control rigidity (P2), a moderation by shadow AI prevalence (P3), and a non-linear relationship between control rigidity and risk (P4). The expected contribution is a defensible, measurable account of which governance practices actually reduce enterprise risk from agentic AI, and at what level of control the protective effect is strongest. As autonomous systems take on work that was previously human, the firm's exposure depends less on the intelligence of the agent than on the maturity of the governance around it, and that relationship deserves empirical estimation rather than continued assertion.
