## Summary

DiMaggio's commentary is a companion to Sutton and Staw's "What Theory is Not" in the same ASQ issue. He accepts their "superb description of non-theory" but argues that three further issues make the problem of theory harder than their account of education and talent implies [p.2]. First, there is more than one kind of good theory. Second, the best theory is usually a hybrid that must reconcile competing values. Third, a theory's fate is shaped after it is written, through a reception process driven "more by resonance than by reason" [p.5]. He frames these as two "modest revisions" meant to qualify, not overturn, Sutton and Staw [p.7].

## Key Arguments

### 1. There is more than one kind of good theory

DiMaggio identifies at least three views of what theory should be, each with some validity and each with limits [p.2]:

- **Theory as covering laws.** Theories as generalizations that describe the world as measured. DiMaggio notes Sutton and Staw implicitly reject this, which makes their argument "more radical than it sounds": they reject a focus on explaining variance rather than regularities, "scientific progress as a kind of R2 sweepstakes," and the image of variables explaining one another — what Abbott (1988) derided as "ordinary linear reality" [p.2]. At the limit (economists who accept implausible assumptions if R2s are high, citing Friedman 1953), this view supplies the "what" of theory that Sutton and Staw say is insufficient without the how and why [p.2].
- **Theory as enlightenment.** A view prominent where the social sciences meet the humanities: theory as "a device of sudden enlightenment," complex, defamiliarizing, rich in paradox [p.2]. Theorists enlighten not through conceptual clarity but "by startling the reader into satori." Theory here is a "surprise machine" (Gouldner's 1970 assessment of Parsons) that clears away conventional notions to make room for new insights [p.2].
- **Theory as narrative.** Theory as "an account of a social process," with attention to empirical plausibility and scope conditions [p.2]. The minimalist version (Collins 1981 on micro-translation) requires that hypothesized regularities be accompanied by plausible accounts of how real humans could produce the observed associations; more assertive versions begin from formal generative models (Fararo 1989) [pp.2-3].

DiMaggio reads Sutton and Staw as holding a "temperate version of the narrative approach," a bias he shares, but he argues room must be kept for the other versions and for hybrids — e.g., Cohen, March, and Olsen (1972), which combined narrative modelling with "Zen-like paradox" [p.3].

### 2. Good theory splits the difference

Many of the best theories are hybrids combining covering-law, enlightenment, and process approaches; because these are driven by different purposes and values, the researcher faces "not a list of brightline standards, but a set of vexing choices" [p.3]. DiMaggio names three tensions:

- **Clarity vs. defamiliarization.** Good theory should let a native see their world with new eyes, but not go too far. Neologisms and paradox win attention, yet too many make a theory "too strange to grasp" [p.3]. "All good theory has a germ of paradox"; Hannan and Freeman's (1977) ecology paper worked on a tiny shift of focus, while Cohen, March, and Olsen's (1972) garbage-can model is "awash in paradox." Too much and a theory "begins to sound preposterous. The trick is in the balance" [p.3].
- **Focus vs. multidimensionality.** Programs prize focus (push one model as far as it goes) but also teach exegesis (pick apart what a study omits); some treat multidimensionality — reference to agency, culture, structure — as a decisive adequacy criterion (Alexander 1982) [p.4]. DiMaggio sides with "strategic reduction": abstracting away enough confusion to produce pointed explanations, while noting "where one draws the line is still more art than science" [p.4].
- **Comprehensiveness vs. memorability.** The most interesting causal factors are often not the most important. He recounts Bruce Mayhew asking why he spent forty of forty-five minutes on network measures when organizational size explained twice as much variance in his dependent variables [p.4]. Preoccupation with theoretical novelty leads researchers to overlook "crucial if banal patterns," sometimes omitting "dull" variables and misspecifying statistical models [p.4].

### 3. Theory construction is social construction, often after the fact

Even a well-balanced theory's fate depends on factors outside the author's control: production requires "utmost care," but reception is "ordinarily helter-skelter," a process of appropriation "driven more by resonance than by reason" [pp.4-5]. Theory is created by its readers as well as its writers, then recreated by the authors who employ it [p.5]. Three sub-points:

- **The value of resonance.** DiMaggio offers as "an hypothesis (not a theory)" that reception depends on how far a theory resonates with the cultural presuppositions of its time and audience [p.5]. His example: accounts of sea-lion dominance contests gained qualification only as feminist theory (Haraway 1989) made scientists sensitive to how culture shapes their portrayal of nature — the change was in the intellectual environment, not the theories or the sea lions [p.5]. He asks whether shifts in organization theory (political theories of bureaucracy; garbage-can, resource-dependence, ecological, institutional theories; network-organization views) track cultural change as much as organizational change [pp.5-6].
- **Theories into slogans.** People read quickly and keep only what resonates with prior schemas (Fleck 1979; D'Andrade 1995) [p.6]. The more widely a work is read, the larger its non-expert readership and the more its arguments are simplified [p.6]. DiMaggio's own case: DiMaggio and Powell (1983), "The Iron Cage Revisited," lost its central interorganizational-network argument in reception and became "a kind of ritual citation" for the view that organizations are wacky and people never rational [p.6]. He also criticises classifying theories by "primordial antimonies" ("hard" vs. "soft") rather than multidimensional analytic categories [p.6].
- **Post hoc theory construction.** Theories "take on a life of their own": sophisticated ideas get degraded, half-baked ideas improve in others' hands [pp.6-7]. He revisited Simmel's (1955) essay on conflict and did not find the ideas that "claim his paternity in very crisp form," concluding that being taken up by the likes of Coser (1956), Breiger (1974), and Blau (1977) puts a reputation "in very good hands" [p.7]. The "supreme example" is Chester Barnard (1938): DiMaggio doubts *The Functions of the Executive* satisfies any formal criterion of good theory, but it inspired Simon and March to turn Barnard's convoluted prose into "a crystalline and compelling propositional inventory" (Simon 1957; March and Simon 1958) [p.7].

## Conclusion (author's own summary)

DiMaggio states two "modest revisions" to Sutton and Staw [p.7]: (1) good theory is hard to produce routinely because "goodness" is multidimensional and the best theory combines approaches, requiring compromise between "competing and mutually incompatible values"; (2) theory construction is "a cooperative venture between author and readers" — reception rides on more than scientific potential, reducing theories to slogans in the short run and, in the long run, letting "brilliant expositors turn muddled theories into canonical masterpieces." The first set highlights "judgment and pluck"; the second, "environment and luck" [p.7]. He is explicit that these remarks "qualify Sutton and Staw's account, not to question their main lines of argument," and warns that readers who treat his qualifications as "warrant to disobey Sutton and Staw's injunctions deserve whatever the reviewers deal them" [p.7].

## Methodology

Conceptual commentary (ASQ Forum). No empirical data collection; the argument proceeds through illustration and personal anecdote (the South Carolina talk, the sea lions, the reception of DiMaggio and Powell 1983) [pp.4-6]. Published as part of the ASQ Forum exchange responding to Sutton and Staw (1995) [p.1, running head "ASQ Forum/DiMaggio", pp.4,6,8].

## Relevance to Research

- Complements the Sutton-Staw standard by warning against a single monolithic test of "good theory." When evaluating theoretical framing in a literature review, judge which of DiMaggio's three conceptions (covering-law, enlightenment, narrative) a given work is pursuing before applying a criterion suited to a different conception [pp.2-3].
- The three value tensions (clarity vs. defamiliarization; focus vs. multidimensionality; comprehensiveness vs. memorability) supply concrete self-checks when drafting a theory section — especially the caution against dropping "dull" but high-variance variables to chase novelty [pp.3-4].
- The reception argument (resonance, slogans, post hoc reconstruction) is a caution about citation practice: cited theories may have been simplified in the field, so returning to originals (as DiMaggio did with Simmel) is warranted [pp.5-7].

## Limitations

- Written in 1995 as a short commentary; DiMaggio himself frames the points as "modest revisions" and qualifications, not a standalone framework [p.7].
- Grounded in sociology and organization theory; the running examples (institutional theory, population ecology, garbage-can) are domain-specific [pp.5-6].
- The core reception claim is offered explicitly as "an hypothesis (not a theory)," so it is presented as illustrative rather than tested [p.5].

- "Sutton and Staw display an unerring eye for the dodges we authors use as substitutes for theory. But these ruses are easier to acknowledge than to do without." (p.2)
- "There are at least three views of what theory should be, and each of them has some validity. Each of them also has limitations." (p.2)
- "All good theory has a germ of paradox. ... The trick is in the balance." (p.3)
- "[Theory's] reception is ordinarily helter-skelter: a process of appropriation driven more by resonance than by reason." (pp.4-5)
