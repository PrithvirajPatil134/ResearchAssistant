On a humid night in April 2026, Avinash Kisanrao Patil, founder and sole proprietor of Sai Tex Ltd., stood on the factory floor of his unit in the Pride Co-operative Textile Park in Ichhalkaranji, Maharashtra. Five of his ten Rifa Electronic Rapier looms clattered through their cycles at 300 revolutions per minute. The other five stood dark and still, their reed frames gathering lint. A year ago, all ten had run through the night. Now, half the floor was silent.¹

His phone buzzed twice. The first notification was a ₹120,000 interest debit from his bank — the monthly cost of servicing the ₹1.2 crore (US$144,600)² debt he still owed on the very machines that stood idle before him. The second was a WhatsApp message from Shivmani Mishra, his manager and oldest employee: "Avinash-ji, two more boys from Bihar are leaving next week. The new unit on the other side of the Park has air-jet machines. They are offering 10 per cent more."³

Patil closed the message and opened his personal ledger — a ruled notebook that served as Sai Tex's only financial record. He did not maintain a formal balance sheet; he had never been trained to.⁴ But the numbers he had memorized were unforgiving. In the best months of 2025, the unit had generated ₹378,000 in revenue. In the worst, it had managed just ₹180,000. The average hovered around ₹350,000 per month.⁵ Against this, monthly expenses — labour, electricity, interest, spare parts, transport — consumed ₹510,000 (see Exhibit 1).⁶ Every month, for the privilege of keeping his doors open, Patil was bleeding cash from savings that were almost gone.

By Monday morning, he had to call his bank manager back. The bank had offered to finance a ₹3.6 crore modernization — twelve new air-jet looms to replace the ageing Rifas — but only if Patil pledged his family's bungalow as additional collateral.⁷ His wife had been direct the previous evening: "We want this to work, Avinash. But not at the cost of everything else."⁸

Eighteen families depended on Sai Tex for their livelihoods. Two competitors in the same Park — M.F. Rathi Textiles and P.A. Patil Textiles — had already upgraded and were capturing his old clients.⁹ The job-work rate that determined his revenue had fallen below break-even and showed no sign of recovering. The question was no longer whether to change, but which change would not destroy everything he had built.

## THE MANCHESTER OF MAHARASHTRA

Ichhalkaranji, a town of roughly 300,000 people in the Kolhapur district of western Maharashtra, had been weaving cloth since before India's independence. The powerloom industry in the town dates back to 1904, when the first decentralized powerloom units were established in what was then a quiet agricultural settlement.¹⁰ Over the following century, the cluster grew into one of India's largest centres for greige (unfinished) fabric production, earning the title "Manchester of Maharashtra."

By 2026, the Ichhalkaranji cluster housed over 50,000 ordinary powerlooms, 12,000 air-jet and rapier looms, and 3,000 auto looms — a combined installed base of roughly 65,000 machines spread across thousands of small and medium-sized units.¹¹ The ecosystem operated on a distinctive decentralized model. Thousands of family-owned powerloom units, most employing fewer than 50 workers, collectively produced a significant share of India's total cloth output. Yarn suppliers, weavers, sizing units, processors, and traders all operated within a few kilometres of one another, keeping transaction costs low and enabling rapid order fulfilment.

The model's historical strength was its flexibility. A small unit could switch between fabric types — cotton shirting, polyester-cotton blends, saree material — with minimal downtime. The dense local network meant that a weaver who lost one customer could find another within days. For decades, this flexibility had compensated for the cluster's lack of scale, modern infrastructure, and access to institutional capital.

At the heart of this ecosystem was the job-work model. A job-work unit did not own the raw material or the finished product. A merchant or exporter supplied the yarn, the weaver converted it into fabric on his looms, and the weaver was paid a per-pick rate for the service — typically measured in paisa per pick of the weft thread.¹² The model kept capital requirements low, since the weaver bore no inventory risk, but it also meant that the weaver had no pricing power. The rate was set by the merchant, and the weaver's only negotiating leverage was the quality and speed of his machines.

The workforce that powered this ecosystem had undergone a fundamental transformation. Where Ichhalkaranji's looms had once been operated by local Maharashtrian workers, by 2026 the industry was almost entirely dependent on migrant labour from Uttar Pradesh and Bihar.¹³ These workers, unlike their predecessors, felt no generational attachment to specific mills. They were rational economic actors who compared wages, working conditions, and machinery across units — and they moved. A mill running 15-year-old machines was not merely less productive; it was less attractive to the very workers it needed to operate.

While competing clusters in Surat and Bhiwandi had aggressively adopted air-jet looms — machines that produced four times the volume at significantly higher speeds — Ichhalkaranji's smaller units remained largely tethered to older rapier and shuttle technology.¹⁴ The town's weavers were caught in a commodity trap: their costs were modern and rising, but their production technology was legacy and failing. When the "Manchester of Maharashtra" caught a cold, thousands of families — both owners and workers — felt the chill.

## SAI TEX: A DECADE OF WEAVING

Avinash Kisanrao Patil founded Sai Tex Ltd. in 2010, investing ₹1.2 crore in ten Rifa Electronic Rapier looms imported from China at ₹12 lakh each.¹⁵ Unlike the multi-generational weaving families that dominated Ichhalkaranji, Patil was a first-generation entrepreneur. His early struggles were prosaic but real: finding the right customers willing to trust a new, unproven unit with their yarn.¹⁶ His bet was on technology. The Rifa machines, with their electronic rapier weft-insertion systems operating at 300 revolutions per minute, were a significant step up from the traditional shuttle looms that most of the cluster still used. In 2010, they represented the "modern face" of Ichhalkaranji.

For a decade, the bet paid off. Sai Tex became a respected job-work unit, weaving grey fabric — primarily 100 per cent cotton and polyester-cotton blends used for shirting — from yarn supplied by India's large fabric exporters.¹⁷ The unit operated from a shed within the Pride Co-operative Textile Park, a local textile cooperative that provided shared infrastructure and a community of fellow mill owners.¹⁸ At its peak in 2023, the job-work rate stood at 25 paisa per pick, translating to roughly ₹15 per metre of fabric. On monthly sales of ₹675,000, Sai Tex generated a net profit of approximately ₹603,000 after expenses of just ₹72,000 — a comfortable margin that allowed Patil to service his debt, pay his workers, and support local schools and children in the community (see Exhibit 2).¹⁹

Patil employed a team of 12 workers across specialized roles. Shivmani Mishra, the manager — known locally as the *diwanji* — had been with the firm since its founding, coordinating with traders and suppliers, managing fabric delivery and collection, and purchasing spare parts. Khudbuddin Mujawar, the technician, handled all machine maintenance. Two beam gaiters — Raju and Pintu — tied warp beams for new orders. Four weavers — Shivam, Sonal, Krishnkant Pande, and Ashish — operated the looms. A helper, a sweeper, a *vaiphani* worker who drew warp ends into the reed, and a drop-pin worker completed the team (see Exhibit 6).²⁰ The total monthly payroll stood at ₹200,900.²¹

The bond between Patil and his workers went beyond the transactional. He did not see his payroll as a line item; he saw school fees, hospital bills, and families whose stability depended on Sai Tex's continued operation.²² This deep identification between the founder and the enterprise — the sense that the mill was not merely a business but an extension of Patil's identity and social obligations — was both Sai Tex's greatest strength and, increasingly, its heaviest anchor.

By 2025, the "modern" machines of 2010 had become the relics of 2026. The Rifa looms, once cutting-edge, were now 15 years old. Their 300 RPM operating speed was a fraction of the 1,200 RPM that the latest air-jet looms could achieve (see Exhibit 5).²³ The rapier machines required a humidification system and consumed energy at rates that, while lower than air-jet looms on a per-machine basis, were far higher per metre of output given their slower speed. The cost of replacement reeds — ₹1,300 for a rapier loom versus ₹16,000 for an air-jet loom — was one of the few remaining advantages of the old technology.²⁴

The debt from the original 2010 investment had never been fully retired. Patil still owed ₹1.2 crore to his bank at an interest rate of 14 per cent per annum.²⁵ The machines that secured this debt were now worth roughly ₹2 lakh each on the resale market — a total liquidation value of ₹20 lakh against ₹1.2 crore in outstanding loans.²⁶ The gap between what the assets were worth and what they owed was a daily reminder of how quickly technology depreciated in the textile industry. Sai Tex was no longer a growth story; it was a high-leverage survival act.

## THE PERFECT STORM

Three external forces converged between 2024 and 2026 to push Sai Tex from stagnation into crisis.

**The Tariff Shock.** In August 2024, the United States imposed steep tariffs on Indian fabric exports. The immediate effect was devastating: fabric export volumes from Ichhalkaranji fell by approximately 40 per cent.²⁷ Large export-oriented units, suddenly unable to ship their output abroad, diverted surplus fabric into the domestic market. The resulting oversupply created a classic bullwhip effect: a demand shock at the export end amplified into a price collapse at the production end. Job-work rates, which had been stable for years, fell below break-even almost overnight. For Sai Tex, the pick rate dropped from 25 paisa in 2023 to 17–18 paisa by 2025, and the realized price per metre of fabric fell from ₹15 to as low as ₹6.40 in the worst months (see Exhibit 3).²⁸ The competitors that Patil worried about were not just the upgraded mills within Ichhalkaranji; they were the large export-oriented units themselves, now competing for the same domestic customers with superior scale and established buyer relationships. The China+1 sourcing strategy that some industry observers had predicted would benefit Indian textile MSMEs had, as of early 2026, produced no discernible positive impact on units like Sai Tex.²⁹

**The Energy Squeeze.** Electricity costs in Maharashtra had been rising at 10 to 15 per cent every two years.³⁰ Sai Tex's monthly energy bill had climbed from ₹45,000 in 2023 to ₹90,000 by 2025 — a 100 per cent increase. The energy cost per unit of electricity had nearly doubled, from ₹3.50 to ₹6.50, and the energy cost per metre of fabric had risen from ₹1 to ₹2 (see Exhibit 2).³¹ The Rifa machines, designed in an era of cheaper power, offered no energy-efficiency features. Unlike the newer inverter-driven looms that competitors were installing, the rapier machines had no idle-state power management and no variable-speed drives. The "Expense Category Stats" from Patil's records showed a power bill of ₹65,000 per month even at reduced production levels — a fixed monster that consumed revenue regardless of output (see Exhibit 7).³²

**The Labour Drain.** The migrant workers who formed the backbone of Ichhalkaranji's workforce compared wages, working conditions, and machinery across units — and they moved. Workers left Sai Tex not because of noise or poor conditions, but because newer machines at other units offered approximately 10 per cent higher salaries and, in areas where the cost of living was affordable, a better standard of living.³³ Patil was operating at roughly 50 per cent capacity — production had swung wildly through 2025, from a high of 45,000 metres in January and August to a low of 25,000 metres in October (see Exhibit 3).³⁴ Labour costs had doubled from ₹150,000 per month in 2023 to ₹300,000 in 2025, even as the workforce shrank — a reflection of the wage inflation required to retain even a skeleton crew.³⁵

The combined effect was a vicious cycle. Lower capacity meant higher per-unit fixed costs. Higher costs meant lower margins. Lower margins meant an inability to raise wages. An inability to raise wages meant further worker attrition. Further attrition meant even lower capacity. Patil was running as fast as he could just to stay in the same place — and falling behind nonetheless.³⁶

The financial picture was stark. The Excel workbook that Patil's case researcher helped him compile told the story in two columns. In 2023, Sai Tex had generated monthly sales of ₹675,000 against total expenses of just ₹72,000, yielding a net profit of ₹603,000. By 2025, monthly sales had collapsed to ₹486,000 while total expenses had ballooned to ₹510,000 — yielding a net loss of ₹24,000 per month (see Exhibit 1).³⁷ The swing from ₹603,000 profit to ₹24,000 loss — a deterioration of ₹627,000 per month — had occurred in just two years. Interest payments alone had risen tenfold, from ₹12,000 to ₹120,000 per month, consuming nearly 25 per cent of total revenue.³⁸

Adding to the pressure, Sai Tex had received a notice from the central government requiring the unit to complete exports worth ₹10 lakh or face a penalty fine of ₹6 lakh — a mandate that was nearly impossible to fulfil given that the export market had already contracted by 60 per cent.³⁹ The COVID-19 lockdown had compounded the damage: the factory had been shut during the pandemic, incoming payments had stopped, raw material supply had dried up, and workers had left. It had taken the next three years to return to full capacity — only for the tariff shock to knock the unit back down.⁴⁰

## THE SALE-PURCHASE QUESTION

Beyond the machinery decision, Patil was contemplating a fundamental shift in his business model. The job-work model that had sustained Sai Tex for 15 years was, by its very design, a low-margin, low-control arrangement. The weaver bore all the production costs — labour, energy, maintenance — but had no say in the price of the output. The merchant set the rate, and the weaver accepted it or lost the order.

The alternative was the sale-purchase model: buy yarn directly, weave it into fabric, and sell the finished product independently. Under this model, Sai Tex would capture the full product margin rather than a per-pick fee. Patil estimated that the initial working capital required to start the sale-purchase model — covering two months of yarn inventory and storage space — would be approximately ₹25 lakh.⁴¹

The shift was not merely financial; it was existential. Patil had never dealt with yarn procurement directly. In fifteen years of weaving, he had never had to purchase yarn — it had always been provided by the merchant under the job-work arrangement.⁴² The sale-purchase model would require him to develop entirely new capabilities: sourcing yarn at competitive prices, managing inventory, negotiating with buyers, and bearing the risk of unsold fabric sitting in his shed. Under the job-work model, unsold fabric was the merchant's problem. Under sale-purchase, it would be Patil's. Several other units in Ichhalkaranji were also contemplating the same pivot, driven by the same margin compression, which meant that the sale-purchase market itself was becoming more competitive even as units entered it.⁴³ The export slowdown had compounded this: companies that had previously exported were now selling domestically, increasing competition and pushing down rates for both job-work and sale-purchase models.

The yarn price data from Patil's records illustrated the scale of the raw material cost he would need to manage. Cotton yarn prices had risen sharply between 2023 and 2025: 20-single cotton from ₹100 to ₹120 per kilogram, 40-single cotton from ₹190 to ₹280, OE cotton from ₹110 to ₹150, and 30-single cotton from ₹250 to ₹325 (see Exhibit 2).⁴⁴ Under the sale-purchase model, these input costs would sit directly on Sai Tex's balance sheet — a new and unfamiliar risk for a unit that had never carried inventory.

## THREE PATHS FORWARD

By late 2025, Patil had distilled his options into three distinct paths. Each was mutually exclusive in practice — the financial and operational commitments of any one path would preclude the others.

**Option A: The Green Technical Pivot.** Replace all ten Rifa machines with twelve high-speed air-jet looms at a cost of ₹3.6 crore, install a rooftop solar plant for ₹50 lakh to eliminate the electricity bill, and simultaneously pivot to the sale-purchase model with ₹25 lakh in working capital.⁴⁵ Total new investment: approximately ₹4.35 crore. Combined with the existing ₹1.2 crore debt, total obligations would reach ₹5.55 crore. At 14 per cent interest, the annual debt service alone would approach ₹77.7 lakh (see Exhibit 4).⁴⁶

The air-jet looms operated at 1,200 RPM — four times the speed of the Rifa machines — and each could produce 400 metres per day compared to the Rifa's 100 metres (see Exhibit 5).⁴⁷ With twelve machines, the unit's daily capacity would leap from 1,000 metres to 4,800 metres. Under the sale-purchase model, Patil would capture the full product margin rather than the shrinking per-pick fee. He was open to producing whatever fabric was in high demand and feasible — whether cotton shirting, polyester-cotton blends, or higher-margin technical fabrics — as long as it did not require prohibitive upfront costs.⁴⁸

The Maharashtra Textile Policy 2023–28 offered capital subsidies of up to 25 per cent for eligible modernization investments under the MAHA-TUFS (Maharashtra Technology Upgradation Fund Scheme), potentially reducing the effective investment by up to ₹1 crore.⁴⁹ However, Patil had not been aware of this subsidy until the case researcher informed him — and his lack of formal financial records made the application process daunting.⁵⁰

The risk was existential. The bank had indicated willingness to finance the upgrade — but only with Patil's personal bungalow as additional collateral.⁵¹ If the domestic fabric market softened further, if the subsidy was delayed, or if the air-jet looms could not run at capacity from day one due to the same labour shortages that plagued the rapier machines, the interest on ₹5.55 crore at 14 per cent would consume not just the business but the family home. The air-jet looms also demanded significantly more infrastructure: a complete air-conditioning plant and large compressors for the compressed-air weft insertion system, compared to the simple humidification system the rapier looms required (see Exhibit 5).⁵² Energy consumption per machine would be substantially higher — though the four-fold increase in throughput meant that energy cost per metre of output would likely decrease.

Two competitors within the Pride Co-operative Park had already made the leap. M.F. Rathi Textiles in Ichhalkaranji had upgraded its machinery and was reportedly capturing clients that had once been Sai Tex's. P.A. Patil Textiles, also in the Park, had made a similar transition.⁵³ Their success was both an inspiration and a source of anxiety: it proved the upgrade could work, but it also meant that every month Sai Tex delayed, its competitive position eroded further.

**Option B: The Hybrid Lean Model.** Instead of a wholesale replacement, retrofit the existing Rifa machines with energy-efficient inverter motors and acoustic shielding. Estimated cost: ₹40–50 lakh.⁵⁴ This would reduce the energy bill by 20–30 per cent and extend the operational life of the current machines by several years. It would not, however, solve the fundamental problem: Sai Tex would still be weaving low-value fabric at ₹6.40–₹9.00 per metre on machines that could not attract or retain workers. Option B was a tourniquet, not a cure. It bought time — perhaps two to three years — but offered no path back to profitability. The job-work rate would remain at the mercy of market forces that Patil could not influence.

**Option C: Asset Liquidation and Exit.** Sell the ten Rifa machines at their current resale value of ₹2 lakh each — ₹20 lakh total — use the proceeds to partially retire the ₹1.2 crore debt, and close the factory.⁵⁵ This would immediately stop the monthly losses and preserve whatever equity remained in the family's land and personal assets. But it would also mean the end of Sai Tex — and the displacement of 18 workers and their families in a town where alternative employment was scarce and shrinking. For Patil, who had built the business from nothing in 2010, it would mean acknowledging that the "Manchester of Maharashtra" had no room left for a first-generation weaver running legacy machines.

His family was supportive — they wanted him to turn the business around — but not at the cost of additional stress and debt that could jeopardize the household.⁵⁶ There was no internal resistance to the idea of upgrading; everyone agreed that new machines were the answer.⁵⁷ The question was not whether to modernize but how to finance it — and whether the business, already haemorrhaging cash, could survive the transition.

## THE DECISION

On a Sunday evening in April 2026, Patil sat in his small office adjacent to the factory floor. On one side of his desk lay the bank's loan application for the ₹3.6 crore modernization, with a yellow sticky note marking the page where he would need to sign over the bungalow. On the other side lay a draft "For Sale" listing for the ten Rifa machines, prepared by a broker who had already found a buyer in a smaller town where rapier looms were still considered modern.

Outside, the five running looms clattered on. The five idle ones stood silent. Somewhere in the workers' quarters, Shivmani Mishra was probably wondering whether to start looking for work at one of the upgraded mills down the road. The export penalty notice from the central government — ₹6 lakh in fines if Sai Tex could not complete ₹10 lakh in exports — sat in a drawer, unanswered.⁶⁰

His father's voice echoed: "A weaver doesn't just make cloth; he weaves the lives of his workers together."⁵⁸ But the lives Patil was weaving together were unravelling — and the thread was running out.

The phone rang. It was the bank manager. "Avinash-ji, I need your answer by Monday. Are we processing the MAHA-TUFS subsidy application for the full modernization, or are we just renewing the existing cash credit limit?"⁵⁹

The air-jet looms could produce four times the output. The sale-purchase model could transform his revenue per metre. The solar plant could eliminate his largest variable cost. The subsidy could offset a quarter of the capital expenditure. But the math also assumed that the domestic fabric market would hold, that the subsidy would be disbursed on time, that the air-jet looms would run at capacity from day one, and that the migrant workers would return to a factory that was, for now, still running 15-year-old machines at half capacity. If any of those assumptions failed, the interest on ₹5.55 crore at 14 per cent would consume not just the business but the family home.

Patil picked up the phone.
