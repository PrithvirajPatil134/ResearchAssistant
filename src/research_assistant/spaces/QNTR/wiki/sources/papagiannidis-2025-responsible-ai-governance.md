## Summary

This scoping review synthesizes 48 papers on responsible AI to develop a conceptual framework for responsible AI governance, defined as "a set of practices for developing, deploying, and monitoring AI applications in a safe, trustworthy, and ethical manner that ensures appropriate functionality of AI over the entire lifecycle" (p.6). The framework distinguishes three types of governance practices (structural, relational, procedural), identifies their antecedents (societal expectations, organizational values, responsible AI principles), and maps their effects (business value, social assessment) (pp.10-11).

The paper addresses a critical gap: while many frameworks detail responsible AI principles at a high level, there is limited understanding of how these principles translate into actionable governance practices throughout the AI lifecycle. The authors argue that the literature is "disparate and lacks cohesion, clarity, and, in some cases, depth" (p.1). Their contribution is a unified framework that connects abstract principles to concrete organizational practices, drawing on IT governance theory (Van Grembergen et al., 2004; Tallon et al., 2013) to structure the three practice categories (p.10).

## Key Arguments

- Responsible AI governance requires three interdependent types of organizational practices that operate at different levels (p.10):
  - **Structural practices**: define decision-makers, rights, responsibilities, governance committees, and decision-making protocols (p.11)
  - **Procedural practices**: encompass data/model management processes, pipeline evaluation, human-AI interaction protocols, incident response, and strategic planning (p.13)
  - **Relational practices**: establish links within and beyond the organization, develop responsible AI literacy, and ensure stakeholder involvement in development (pp.13-14)
- Three categories of antecedents drive responsible AI governance (pp.10-11):
  - Societal expectations and norms (evolving regulations, cultural expectations)
  - Prevailing organizational values and path dependencies (filtering external signals)
  - Responsible AI principles adopted by the organization (fluid, contextual, constantly changing)
- Seven responsible AI principles form the foundation: accountability, diversity/non-discrimination/fairness, human agency and oversight, privacy and data governance, technical robustness and safety, transparency, and societal and environmental well-being (pp.5-10, Table 4)
- A principles-first approach may prove "counterproductive and incompatible with the organizational modus operandi" because the diversity and breadth of principles makes holistic implementation challenging (p.7)
- Responsible AI governance can generate organizational value through legitimacy, improved morale, productivity, and employee loyalty, not merely through compliance (p.14)
- Lack of responsible AI practices correlates with low job performance and AI-induced anxiety among employees (p.14)

## Methodology

Scoping review following established systematic literature review procedures (Kitchenham, 2004; Okoli, 2015). Five-step process (p.3):
1. Gathering information: 1,080 documents identified
2. Abstract review: 494 excluded for irrelevance/duplicates, then 378 excluded after abstract reading
3. Approach review: 157 excluded
4. Data extraction into concept matrix
5. Data synthesis using concept matrix

Search conducted across Scopus, Business Source Complete, Emerald, Taylor & Francis, Springer, Web of Knowledge, ABI/Inform, IEEE Xplore, AIS libraries, ScienceDirect, JSTOR, DBLP, and Google Scholar. Data collection commenced November 2022, refined December 2023. Inclusion criteria: AI in organizational context, published from 2017 onwards, English, peer-reviewed journals (p.3).

Final sample: 48 papers. Two researchers independently reviewed papers with a third researcher mediating disagreements. Quality assessment examined study design, methodology, sample size, data quality, ethical considerations, and reporting (pp.3-4).

## Findings

- The authors synthesize seven responsible AI principles with their sub-dimensions (Table 4, p.8):
  - Accountability: auditability + responsibility allocation
  - Diversity/fairness: accessibility + no unfair bias
  - Human agency: human review rights + human well-being
  - Privacy/data governance: data quality + privacy + access
  - Technical robustness: accuracy + reliability + general safety + resilience
  - Transparency: explainability + communication + traceability
  - Social/environmental well-being
- Structural practices are poorly understood regarding: vertical decision-making, power dynamics, horizontal coordination, and extended stakeholder ecosystem involvement (p.11)
- Procedural practices lack strategic-level integration; organizations struggle to harmonize competitive strategies with responsible AI governance (p.13)
- Relational practices require understanding how responsible AI literacy develops collectively and individually, and how informal channels diffuse governance norms (pp.13-14)
- The research agenda (Table 5, pp.12-13) proposes 25+ specific research questions across the framework's dimensions

## Relevance to Research

This paper provides the governance practices taxonomy (structural, relational, procedural) that can serve as a theoretical foundation for operationalizing the dependent variable in the agentic AI governance research. The three practice types, drawn from IT governance theory (Van Grembergen et al., 2004), offer a validated classification that can be extended to the agentic AI context.

The framework's antecedents-practices-effects structure maps directly to the research model: governance maturity (IV, captured through practices) leading to enterprise risk and performance outcomes (DVs, captured through effects). The paper's explicit identification of the principles-to-practices translation gap justifies the qualitative Phase 1 approach to construct development.

The 25+ research questions in Table 5 represent unexplored territory that the agentic AI governance research can address, particularly questions about how structural practices scale, how competing stakeholder interests are resolved, and how responsible AI governance creates business value.

## Limitations

- Papers predominantly from European and US institutions; cultural and ethical norms may not generalize globally (p.15)
- Arguments and relationships proposed require empirical testing (p.15)
- Research questions are "neither exhaustive nor complete" (p.15)
- The framework addresses AI generally, not specifically agentic AI systems with autonomous goal-pursuit
- The review's 2017 cutoff and December 2023 refinement means it likely misses the rapid developments in agentic AI during 2024-2025
- No empirical validation of the framework's causal relationships
