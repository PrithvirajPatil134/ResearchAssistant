## Summary

Kohli and Jaworski set out to give the marketing concept an operational, testable form. They argue the literature had treated the marketing concept as a business philosophy but had produced "a lack of clear definition, little careful attention to measurement issues, and virtually no empirically based theory" (p. 1). Their stated purpose is to delineate the domain of the market orientation construct, provide an operational definition, develop a propositional inventory, and build an integrative framework for future research (p. 1). Using the term "market orientation" to mean the implementation of the marketing concept (p. 1), they define it as "the organizationwide generation of market intelligence pertaining to current and future customer needs, dissemination of the intelligence across departments, and organizationwide responsiveness to it" (p. 6).

This is a theory-construction paper, not a theory-testing paper: "the objective of our research is theory construction rather than theory testing" (p. 16). For the CRO course it is read as an exemplar of how a discovery-oriented, practitioner-grounded method (the approach later codified as Theories-in-Use) is used to move from a fuzzy concept to a defined construct with a propositional inventory.

## Key Arguments

### Redefining the construct around behaviour, not philosophy

The received view rested on three "pillars" of the marketing concept — customer focus, coordinated marketing, and profitability (p. 3). The field interviews revise this. Customer focus is retained but broadened from verbalised customer opinions to "market intelligence," which includes exogenous forces such as competition, regulation, and technology, and both current and future needs (p. 3). Coordinated marketing is narrowed to coordination specifically around market intelligence, which the authors argue makes the construct operationalisable (p. 3). Profitability is dropped from the construct: without exception, interviewees viewed profitability as a *consequence* of a market orientation rather than a component of it (p. 3), consistent with Levitt's objection quoted on p. 3.

The authors also justify the label "market orientation" over "marketing orientation" for three reasons: it signals the construct is not the marketing department's exclusive concern, it is less politically charged inside the firm, and it focuses attention on markets including customers and the forces affecting them (pp. 3-4).

### The three behavioural components

- **Intelligence generation** (pp. 4-5): the starting point; broader than customer surveys, includes analysis of exogenous factors and anticipation of future needs; explicitly not the sole responsibility of the marketing department (R&D, senior executives, and manufacturing all contribute).
- **Intelligence dissemination** (pp. 5-6): communicating and "selling" intelligence to relevant departments through both formal procedures and informal "hall talk"; parallels the "horizontal communication" idea from Zeithaml, Berry, and Parasuraman (1988) (p. 6).
- **Responsiveness** (p. 6): the action taken in response to intelligence — selecting target markets, designing offerings, and producing/distributing/promoting them.

The authors argue market orientation should be conceptualised as a continuum ("many shades of gray," p. 6) rather than a dichotomy, and that the appropriate unit of analysis is the strategic business unit rather than the corporation (p. 6).

### The integrative framework and 19 propositions

Figure 1 (p. 7) organises the framework into four sets of factors: antecedents, the market orientation construct, consequences, and moderators. The authors develop 19 research propositions (stated total, p. 15):

Antecedents — senior management factors: P1a/P1b (variability in the top-management communication-action gap raises junior-manager ambiguity, which lowers market orientation, p. 8); P2 (top-management risk aversion lowers it, p. 9); P3 (senior managers' educational attainment and upward mobility raise it, p. 9); P4 (positive attitude toward change raises it, p. 9); P5 (marketing managers' ability to win nonmarketing peers' confidence lowers interdepartmental conflict, p. 9).

Antecedents — interdepartmental dynamics: P6 (interdepartmental conflict lowers market orientation, p. 9); P7 (interdepartmental connectedness raises it, p. 10); P8 (concern for other departments' ideas raises it, p. 10).

Antecedents — organizational systems: P9a/P9b/P9c (departmentalization, formalization, and centralization each lower intelligence generation/dissemination/response design but raise response implementation, drawing on Zaltman, Duncan, and Holbek's 1973 two-stage view of innovation, p. 11); P10 (market-based reward systems raise it, p. 12); P11 (acceptance of political behaviour raises interdepartmental conflict, p. 12).

Linkages among components: P12a-P12d (source expertise, source trustworthiness, smaller challenge to the status quo, and political acceptability of intelligence affect its dissemination and the responsiveness to it, p. 12).

Consequences: P13 (market orientation raises business performance, with preliminary support cited from Narver and Slater 1988, p. 13); P14 (raises esprit de corps, job satisfaction, and organizational commitment, p. 13); P15 (raises customer satisfaction and repeat business, p. 13).

Moderators of the market-orientation-to-performance link: P16 (market turbulence strengthens it, p. 14); P17 (technological turbulence weakens it, p. 14); P18 (competition strengthens it, p. 15); P19 (a weaker general economy strengthens it, p. 15). Moderators are grouped into demand-side and supply-side (p. 15).

### Managerial implications

Because the antecedent factors are largely controllable by senior managers, a market orientation can be deliberately engendered (p. 15). The authors caution it is not universally worth the cost: under limited competition, stable preferences, high technological turbulence, or booming economies, the orientation may not be strongly related to performance, so managers should weigh the cost-benefit ratio (p. 15). They note change is slow (one firm planned a four-year transition, p. 16), that a perceived gap between current and preferred orientation is a prerequisite for change (p. 16), and that quantity of market-oriented activity does not guarantee quality (p. 16).

## Methodology

Discovery-oriented, practitioner-grounded design (cf. Deshpande 1983; Mahrer 1988), which the authors describe as similar to the qualitative, practitioner-based approach of Parasuraman, Zeithaml, and Berry (1985) and as designed to tap managers' "cause and effect" maps (p. 1). Two data streams:

1. A review of roughly 35 years of marketing literature on the marketing concept, plus related management literature (pp. 1-2).
2. In-depth field interviews with 62 managers in four U.S. cities: 33 in marketing positions, 15 in nonmarketing positions, and 14 in senior management, across 47 organizations (18 consumer-products, 26 industrial-products, 18 services; firms ranging from four employees to tens of thousands). Ten business academicians at two universities were also interviewed (pp. 2). A purposive/"theoretical" sampling plan (Glaser and Strauss 1967) was used because the purpose was theory construction, not representativeness (p. 2). Interviews followed a four-question standard format (meaning of the term; fostering/discouraging factors; positive/negative consequences; situations where it may not matter), typically lasted about 45 minutes, and were audiotaped unless refused (p. 2). Literature-based and field-based propositions were then synthesised into the integrative framework (p. 2).

## Findings

As a theory-construction paper it reports no hypothesis tests. Its deliverables are the operational construct definition (p. 6), the three-component behavioural model, the 19-proposition inventory, and the antecedents-consequences-moderators framework (Figure 1, p. 7). A closing observation positions market orientation as a potential sustainable competitive advantage because it is an organizational process that is "not easily engendered" (p. 17).

## Relevance to Research

### Position in the CRO / theories-in-use lineage

The professor's Session 7 instruction for this paper was to look past the marketing content and assess how the theory-building method was implemented — what was done well and what could be improved (see `wiki/sources/zeithaml-et-al-2020-theories-in-use.md`, "Points Prof. Mukhopadhyay asked the CRO cohort to attend to"). This paper is the applied counterpart to that method paper. Kohli and Jaworski call their approach "discovery-oriented" (p. 1); the two authors later co-authored Zeithaml et al. (2020), which formalises the same style of work as Theories-in-Use and cites this paper as an exemplar.

### How the method was applied — read as a methods exemplar

Grounded observations (each a reading of the cited pages, not a claim from outside the paper):

- The design matches the TIU template: purposive/theoretical sampling for range rather than representativeness (p. 2), an explicit theory-construction (not testing) goal (pp. 2, 16), and elicitation of managers' if-then cause-and-effect beliefs (p. 1) that are abstracted into constructs and propositions.
- Strengths visible in the write-up: the construct definition is derived by comparing the received "three pillars" against field evidence and is revised on that basis (dropping profitability, p. 3); each proposition is tied to specific interview quotations and/or prior literature, giving traceable warrant (e.g., P2 risk aversion, pp. 8-9); the sample deliberately spans marketing and nonmarketing roles, three sectors, and a wide size range (p. 2), which supports the transferability the later TIU paper emphasises.
- Points a reviewer applying the TIU rigor criteria could press on (analytical reading; the paper does not itself report these): the paper does not describe a formal coding procedure (open/axial/selective) or report interjudge reliability, and it does not state whether participant/member checks were used, so the confirmability and dependability criteria later named in Zeithaml et al. (2020) are not evidenced here; the selection of "more interesting" insights (p. 2) is a judgment call the paper acknowledges but does not systematise. [CONJECTURE: these are gaps relative to the 2020 rigor checklist, applied retrospectively to a 1990 paper that predates that checklist.]

### Reuse for a thesis or research paper

- A worked template for moving from a contested concept to an operational, continuum-scaled construct with a stated unit of analysis (pp. 6). Useful when a proposal must justify defining and measuring a fuzzy construct.
- A model for a propositional inventory organised as antecedents/construct/consequences/moderators (Figure 1, p. 7), reusable as a structuring device for a conceptual framework chapter.
- The supply-side/demand-side moderator move (pp. 14-15) is a reusable pattern for arguing that a main effect is contingent rather than universal.

## Limitations

Stated by the authors: the work is theory construction, and "much work remains to be done in terms of developing a suitable measure of market orientation and empirically testing our propositions" (pp. 16-17). Empirical evidence on consequences was thin at the time; the authors cite only preliminary support (Narver and Slater 1988, p. 13) and note a countervailing null result (Lawton and Parasuraman 1980, p. 13). The framework's moderators are proposed, not tested.
