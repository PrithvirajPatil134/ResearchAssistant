Literature Findings: Punctuated Equilibrium and Organizational Restructuring in the Generative AI Transition
Abstract
Between late 2022 and mid-2026, large technology firms reorganized during the generative AI (GenAI) transition. Layoffs.fyi counted more than 260,000 tech-sector layoffs in 2023 and 108,724 across 137 companies in 2026 to date, cuts firms tied to GenAI strategy. Whether that restructuring follows the sequence punctuated equilibrium predicts, a stable period, an environmental jolt, revolutionary transformation, then a new equilibrium, remains untested for the GenAI window: the archival coding rules exist but have not yet been pointed at it. This memo establishes that the three measurement foundations are in place. AI adoption intensity is measurable and non-circular through Alekseeva et al.’s (2026) continuous AI Share; revolutionary versus nonrevolutionary transformation is separable through Romanelli and Tushman’s (1994) coding and Girod and Whittington’s (2015) 0.30 threshold; and organizational structure is recoverable from 10-K and DEF 14A filings (Albert et al., 2026).
1. Research question and sub-questions
Between late 2022 and mid-2026, large technology firms reorganized at a pace the sector had rarely recorded. Layoffs.fyi counted more than 260,000 tech-sector layoffs in 2023 and 108,724 across 137 companies in 2026 to date, cuts that firms routinely tied to generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) strategy shifts. Headcount is only the visible edge. Roles, management layers, divisions, and reporting lines all moved. What the record does not yet hold is a systematic account of the organizational form these firms arrived at, read across companies over time rather than inferred from one-time surveys of adoption rates.
The outcome I want to explain is the shape of the organization, not the rate of adoption. My primary research question:
How do organizational restructuring patterns differ between technology firms pursuing aggressive generative-AI integration and those adopting an incremental approach, and to what extent do these patterns align with the predictions of punctuated equilibrium theory (Romanelli and Tushman, 1994)?
Three sub-questions sit under it:
Which structural elements (management layers, role categories, divisional boundaries, reporting relationships) changed between 2022 and 2027 in the sampled firms?
Do those firms converge toward common organizational forms, or diverge into distinct structural archetypes?
Do the observed changes follow the sequence the theory predicts: a stable period, an environmental jolt, transformation, then a new equilibrium?
The question is answerable because the evidence is public. US-listed firms above $50 billion in market capitalization disclose headcount, segments, named leadership, and restructuring charges in filings and earnings calls, so organizational form can be read from the record. The theory that question invokes, and why generative AI qualifies as the jolt it turns on, is what I set out next.
2. Theoretical framework
The primary research question names punctuated equilibrium (PE), and the theory earns that anchoring role because it makes a specific, testable prediction about the shape of organizational change. Romanelli and Tushman (1994) argue that organizations move through long convergent periods of incremental adjustment interrupted by short bursts of revolutionary transformation. In their test of 25 US minicomputer producers, revolutionary transformations outnumbered gradual ones by at least six to one across every measurement criterion (Romanelli and Tushman, 1994, p.1156). The pattern is not a metaphor. It is a coded, replicable result.
The vocabulary here needs care. The convergence-and-reorientation framework belongs to Tushman and Romanelli (1985), the earlier metamorphosis model. When Romanelli and Tushman (1994, p.1154) coded their dependent variable, they used “revolutionary transformation” versus “nonrevolutionary transformation,” not “reorientation” and “convergence.” A revolutionary transformation is coded when substantial change occurs across all three domains (strategy, structure, and power distribution) within a rolling two-year window (Romanelli and Tushman, 1994, p.1154). I keep the two references distinct throughout: the 1985 paper supplies the lineage, the 1994 paper supplies the coding rule.
What triggers the punctuation matters most for this study. Romanelli and Tushman (1994) tested three candidate triggers and found that major environmental change raised the likelihood of revolutionary transformation (b = 1.513, p < .01), as did CEO succession, while a market-share performance crisis did not (p.1159). They coded major environmental change as a dummy for three industry shifts severe enough to threaten firm survival (p.1155-1156). This environmental jolt is what the whole sequence turns on.
That gives the study its skeleton: a stable convergent period, then an environmental jolt, then revolutionary transformation across strategy, structure, and power, then a settling into a new equilibrium. I treat generative AI (GenAI) as the candidate jolt for large technology firms across 2022-2027.
Disruption theory enters as a complementary lens, not a second anchor. My supervisor asked me to name the theoretical framework explicitly and offered disruption and decision theory as examples (supervision meeting, 17 June 2026). I read disruption as the account of why GenAI qualifies as a jolt forceful enough to compel reorientation, where PE describes the shape the response takes. The corpus does not yet hold a full-text disruption-theory source, so I keep this lens at the conceptual level and rest no coded claim on it.
One distinction closes this out. The coding method for revolutionary versus nonrevolutionary transformation is established and, as the next section shows, transferable to public disclosure. Whether the punctuated pattern actually holds under a GenAI jolt is the open empirical question this thesis tests, and that is the contribution rather than a weakness. Testing it needs three measurable foundations.
3. Literature survey across the three foundations
Three things must be measurable before that sequence can be tested: which firms are adopting generative AI and how intensively (the independent variable), whether a firm’s transformation over a given window is revolutionary or gradual (the dependent variable), and the organizational structure whose change the coding turns on. The literature supplies a usable method for each. None has yet been pointed at the generative-AI window.
Foundation 1: classifying firms by AI adoption intensity
Firm-level AI adoption intensity is measurable from archival data, and it is measurable as a continuous quantity rather than a yes/no flag. Alekseeva et al. (2026, p.10) construct AI Share, the proportion of a firm’s annual job postings that are AI-related, across 823 firms observed from 2010 to 2022; the measure is continuous, with a sample mean of 0.5% and a rise from below 0.1% to roughly 1% across the window (p.12). McElheran et al. (2024) confirm that intensity is gradable rather than binary: their Annual Business Survey instrument records use of five AI technologies at less than 5%, 5 to 25%, and more than 25% of production across roughly 850,000 firms (p.42, p.48). For a spending-based numerator, Mithas and Rust (2016, p.230) supply the ratio template, IT spending over sales revenue, with a sample mean of 4.09%, a design the study can adapt to an AI-spending measure. The authors flag that their strategic-emphasis classification rests on a single self-report item (p.237), a limitation any adaptation inherits.
One objection to any intensity construct is that technology pursuit is too diffuse to measure reliably. Zahra and Covin (1993) answer part of that worry. Their aggressive technological posture measure, a continuous three-item scale anchored against competitors, reaches a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.75 in a sample of 103 mature-manufacturing firms (p.461, p.464). That establishes that the intensity of a firm’s technology pursuit can be captured with a validated, reliable instrument. It does not supply an AI-specific measure, and it is a continuous scale, not a leader-versus-follower typology. I treat it as construct-validity support for the archival path, and at most as a fallback survey instrument should a later mixed-methods phase be warranted, not as a co-equal measurement route.
What the literature does not yet provide is a validated, disclosure-based intensity measure for the generative-AI period. Alekseeva’s AI Share ends in 2022 and captures the predictive-AI wave; the authors themselves note that generative and agentic systems redistribute cognitive complexity across organizational roles in qualitatively different ways (2026, p.4). The design’s most exposed proxy addresses this head-on. I would build a disclosed AI-intensity measure for 2022 to 2027 from public filings and earnings-call text, then validate it by benchmarking each firm’s classification against McElheran’s production-intensity thresholds of under 5%, 5 to 25%, and over 25% (2024, p.48). Because Alekseeva’s instrument predates the generative wave, McElheran’s graded scale, not AI Share itself, is the anchor a new disclosed measure must reproduce before it can carry the independent variable.
Foundation 2: operationalizing punctuated equilibrium in archival data
The dependent variable has an established coding rule, and it is fully archival. Romanelli and Tushman (1994, p.1154) code a revolutionary transformation whenever substantial change occurs in all three domains of strategy, structure, and power distribution within a two-year window, restarting the count after each transformation; two independent coding teams reached 87% agreement across 25 minicomputer firms (p.1149). Their test also identifies the trigger this study cares about: major environmental change raises the likelihood of revolutionary transformation (b = 1.513, p < .01), while performance decline does not (p.1159). Lant, Milliken and Batra (1992, p.593) set a lower bar. They make a strategy change necessary but not sufficient, requiring it to couple with change in at least two of three supporting domains, and report 88% intercoder agreement across stable furniture and turbulent software firms (p.593). Girod and Whittington (2015) convert the idea into a continuous measure: a structural change ratio summing absolute year-over-year shifts in title proportions, with a 0.30 cut-off for discontinuous restructuring that they validated through interviews (pp.1525-1526). That continuous form sits most comfortably with an archival design.

One feature of the canonical rule is a data-driven compromise worth flagging. The 1985 framework behind it proposed five deep-structure domains: culture, strategy, structure, power distribution, and control systems. Romanelli and Tushman collected all five but dropped culture and control systems because firms “reported information about them infrequently and inconsistently,” coding only three (1994, p.1147). Richer 2026 disclosure, in particular earnings-call transcripts amenable to natural-language processing, may make the two dropped domains recoverable. Whether it does is untested. I frame recovering the culture and control-systems domains as a candidate methodological contribution worth testing, not a promised deliverable.
The coding rules transfer cleanly. What they cannot tell us is whether the punctuated pattern itself holds under a generative-AI jolt. Every one of these operationalizations was built for a pre-generative setting: minicomputers, furniture, software, and large industrials. Each is therefore a method for how to code transformation, not evidence about how AI-era firms behave. Applying the all-three-domains-in-two-years rule, or Girod and Whittington’s 0.30 ratio, to the sample firms across 2022 to 2027 is what turns that open question into a test. Each firm-period gets classified as revolutionary or nonrevolutionary, and the distribution of those classifications is the finding.
Foundation 3: measuring organizational structure from disclosure
Structure itself, the object whose change the transformation coding turns on, is measurable from corporate disclosure. Albert, Eklund and Tang (2026) built a top management team structure database covering 161,028 executive-firm-years from 1993 to 2020, drawn from 10-K, 20-F, and DEF 14A filings and labeled into six role groups and twelve hierarchical levels by a fine-tuned GPT-4o model that reached 98.1% accuracy against a hold-out sample (p.14, p.16, p.17). Their recommended change measure is proportional rather than absolute: they track year-over-year shifts in the share of business-unit versus functional roles, and use exactly that to detect reorganizations at Hewlett-Packard and General Electric (p.21). Structure can also be coded in simpler binary form. Lee et al. (2015, p.259) classify a firm as customer-centric or internally aligned from its reportable-segment disclosures under SFAS No. 131, using two independent expert coders with under 7% disagreement across 137 firms and 1,241 firm-year observations. The classification is deliberately binary, a customer-centric dummy against an internally aligned residual, not a multi-category scheme. At the most elaborate end, Larcker, Richardson and Tuna (2007) show how to compress disclosure into a structural index: 39 proxy-statement variables reduced by principal component analysis to 14 components, retaining eigenvalues above one under oblique rotation with a 0.40 loading threshold and a 1,000-sample bootstrap, across 2,106 firms (p.966, p.974).
None of these instruments has yet been pointed at the generative-AI window. Albert’s database ends in 2020, with extension to 2021 through 2024 planned but not yet published (2026, p.11); Lee’s panel closes in 2010; Larcker’s is a single 2002-to-2003 cross-section. The machinery for reading structure off filings is in good order. Evidence on how that structure moves in 2022 to 2027, when the jolt in question lands, is what remains thin, and earnings-call text as a structure signal is still a frontier method rather than a settled one.
The test follows Albert’s taxonomy. For each sample firm across 2022 to 2027 I would code top management team composition into the six role groups and twelve levels, then read structural change from proportional shifts in that composition, the approach the authors recommend to avoid confounds from differences in reporting breadth (2026, p.3). That structure series is what feeds the transformation coding of Foundation 2: a substantial proportional shift is the structure-domain signal the Romanelli and Tushman rule requires, and Lee’s binary form or Larcker’s index can serve as sensitivity alternatives to it.
What remains open across the three foundations
Three gaps run through these foundations, and each is one of insufficiency rather than absence. First, the literature remains limited on a validated, disclosure-based intensity measure for the generative-AI era; the strongest existing archival measure, AI Share, predates it (Alekseeva et al., 2026, p.4). Second, existing punctuated-equilibrium operationalizations were built for pre-generative settings, so systematic evidence on whether the pattern holds under a generative-AI jolt is scarce. Third, whether richer 2026 disclosure can recover the culture and control-systems domains that Romanelli and Tushman dropped for data reasons (1994, p.1147) has not yet been examined. Each gap has a paper that fixes the method even where it cannot supply the evidence, and those ten papers, with their roles made explicit, are the spine the next section lays out.

Two of the five load-bearing papers supply evidence about the phenomenon, and three supply method. Alekseeva et al. (2026, p.10) give the primary independent variable, AI Share, built as AI-related postings over total postings across 823 firms. McElheran et al. (2024, p.48) fix the yardstick that validates it, the <5% / 5-25% / >25% intensity scale, because Alekseeva’s window is pre-generative. Albert et al. (2026, p.16) fix the dependent-variable data source, structure read from SEC filings as 6 role groups by 12 hierarchical levels at 98.1% labeling accuracy. Romanelli and Tushman (1994, p.1154) fix the coding rule for revolutionary transformation, substantial change across all three domains inside a two-year window. Girod and Whittington (2015, pp.1525-1526) fix the continuous alternative, a structural change ratio with a validated 0.30 restructuring cutoff.
The supporting five do bounded jobs. Mithas and Rust (2016, p.230) template an intensity ratio from spending over revenue. Zahra and Covin (1993, p.464) show technology-pursuit intensity is measurable with a validated instrument (alpha=0.75), construct validity only. Lant et al. (1992, p.599) offer a lower-bar strategy-plus rule, Lee et al. (2015, pp.258-259) a binary disclosure classification, and Larcker et al. (2007, p.974) a multi-indicator index built from proxy variables.
One line governs the tags. The method-spine papers are cited for how to code archival change, not as evidence about how generative-AI-era firms behave. Whether that pattern holds is the open question. With the spine fixed, the three foundations collapse into one testable design.
5. How the theory will be tested
The spine papers combine into a single archival design that never leaves public disclosure and requires no primary data collection for its quantitative phase. I would take six to eight US-listed technology firms above $50 billion in market capitalization, observe them across fiscal years 2022 to 2027, and split them into aggressive versus incremental generative AI adopters. That split is the independent variable, built from inputs each firm files itself: an AI Share ratio of AI-related to total job postings, following Alekseeva et al. (2026, p.3, p.10), read alongside the AI language in their 10-K filings.
The measure needs a validity bridge. Alekseeva’s AI Share runs from 2010 to 2022 (Alekseeva et al., 2026, p.11), the predictive-AI period, not the generative one. So a disclosed 2022 to 2027 measure has to be benchmarked against an external scale, and McElheran et al. (2024) supply one: their intensity bands of under 5 percent, 5 to 25 percent, and over 25 percent of production (McElheran et al., 2024, p.48) give a published yardstick for sorting firms into light, moderate, and heavy adoption. As secondary evidence that technology-pursuit intensity is measurable at all, Zahra and Covin’s (1993, p.464) three-item aggressive technological posture scale reached an alpha of 0.75. It backstops the archival path; it does not co-anchor it.
The dependent variable is structural form, coded two ways so the finding rests on no single rule. First, revolutionary versus nonrevolutionary transformation as Romanelli and Tushman (1994, p.1154) code it: a revolutionary transformation requires substantial change in strategy, structure, and power distribution inside a two-year window. Second, Girod and Whittington’s (2015, p.1526) structural change ratio, with its 0.30 dummy cutoff for discontinuous restructuring. Both read off top management team composition drawn from 10-K and DEF 14A filings using Albert et al.’s (2026, p.14) role-group and hierarchical-level taxonomy, scored as proportional change rather than raw headcount (Albert et al., 2026, p.3).
This answers the circularity worry carried from the two-page note. The independent variable is a labor and spending input, disclosed AI intensity. The dependent variable is organizational form, TMT composition change. Neither embeds the other, so a firm can score high on adoption and low on restructuring, or the reverse.
A word on what this design is and is not. It is a deductive test of an existing theory against public archival data, the same shape as Romanelli and Tushman’s (1994) own empirical test, which coded transformation from objective measures and reported results across four sensitivity thresholds rather than resting on a single cutoff (Romanelli and Tushman, 1994, pp.1151-1153). It is not a case study built on interviews, and it does not set out to build new theory. The six-to-eight firms are chosen to contrast aggressive and incremental adopters so the test is sharp, not to sample a population, so the finding confirms or disconfirms whether the pattern punctuated equilibrium predicts appears in these firms rather than estimating how common that pattern is across the sector. Questions of prevalence, how many firms and how often, belong to the later quantitative phase the design already anticipates (Phase 2), not to this archival read.
The expectation follows. Aggressive adopters should cross into revolutionary transformation, multi-domain change within the window or a ratio above 0.30, while incremental adopters stay nonrevolutionary, with disruption theory supplying why the generative-AI jolt is the trigger. The tools are established and transferable; whether the punctuated pattern holds under this jolt is the open question. A richer 2026 disclosure record, earnings-call text in particular, might also let the study recover the culture and control-systems domains that Romanelli and Tushman (1994, p.1147) dropped for want of data, which is worth testing rather than promising. Known: the coding works on public filings. Added: a first archival read of whether large tech firms punctuate under generative AI.
